Sun Protection for Dogs: What UPF Clothing Can and Can't Do

By Hannah WrightSep 3, 2026Pet Apparel4 min read

Plain answer: UPF clothing can block a useful amount of UV from the skin it actually covers, which helps fair-skinned, thin-coated, or scarred dogs in a high-UV country like ours. As a vet nurse, I'd say it is a genuinely helpful layer, not a complete sun solution. A shirt won't cover the nose, ears, or belly, and it does nothing once the dog is out of it, so shade, timing, and watching exposed areas still matter most.

What UPF actually means on a dog shirt

UPF is a rating for fabric, the same idea as SPF for sunscreen but for cloth. A higher number means the weave blocks more ultraviolet light. A shirt rated UPF 40 lets through only a small fraction of the sun that would otherwise hit that patch of skin, which is meaningful for a dog who burns easily.

The catch is the rating applies to the fabric, not to the gaps. A loose shirt with a belly band still leaves the underside and legs open to the sun.

What the clothing can do well

For a hairless or light-coated dog, or one with a shaved patch from surgery, a UPF shirt is a practical shield during midday toilet breaks and short outings. It stays put better than sunscreen on a wriggly dog, and you don't have to reapply it every couple of hours.

  • Protects the back and sides on fair or thin-coated dogs.
  • Covers shaved or scarred skin that burns easily.
  • Stays effective without reapplication, unlike cream.
  • Helps on boat trips and beach days where reflection is high.

What it can't do

A shirt leaves the nose, ear tips, and often the legs exposed, and those are exactly where Aussie dogs get sun damage. Pale noses and thin ears are common trouble spots, and no torso shirt fixes that. Clothing also can't replace getting out of the sun during the peak UV window.

If a dog overheats in a long-sleeved shirt on a hot day, the protection becomes a problem of its own, so lighter UPF fabrics and breaks in shade are the sensible middle path.

Habits that matter more than the shirt

Walk early or late, stick to shade, and check the UV index before heading out. For the bits clothing misses, ask your vet about pet-safe sunscreen for noses and ears. I'm a nurse offering supportive care advice, not diagnosing, so a vet should weigh in on any skin changes you notice.

Choosing a shirt without the guesswork

When I help owners pick sun clothing, I start with the fabric rather than the colour photo. Hold it to the light: if you can see through it easily, the weave is loose and the UPF claim is suspect. A denser, darker, tightly woven shirt blocks more, and a label with an actual UPF number is worth more than vague sun-safe wording.

Fit matters for comfort as much as for coverage. A shirt that rides up leaves the exact belly strip that burns on a beach day, and one that is too tight makes a dog fret and overheat. I tell owners to size for the chest and check the leg openings before they buy.

The shirt is one part of the plan, not the whole of it. Shade, timing, and protecting the nose and ears still do the heavy lifting, and a vet can point you to a pet-safe cream for the spots fabric simply cannot reach. Used together, they cover far more than any single item alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is UPF clothing safe for dogs in summer heat?

Lightweight UPF shirts are generally fine for short, shaded outings, but any covering adds warmth. Choose a breathable fabric, keep walks brief in peak heat, and remove the shirt if your dog pants heavily or seems restless in the sun.

Can a shirt replace sunscreen entirely?

No, because it leaves the nose, ears, and often legs exposed. Use the shirt for the torso and ask your vet about a pet-safe cream for the areas the fabric can't reach, especially on pale or thin-coated dogs.

Which dogs benefit most from sun shirts?

Hairless breeds, very light-coated dogs, and any dog with a shaved or scarred patch gain the most. Dark, thick-coated dogs rarely need one, though a vet can advise if your pet has a specific skin concern.

How do I know the UPF rating is real?

Look for a stated UPF number from a recognised standard on the label or listing. If a product won't say, that is a red flag. A tight, dark, closely woven fabric blocks more than a thin pale one even without a label.

Should my dog wear it all day outside?

Not usually. A shirt helps during timed outings and toilet breaks in the UV peak, then comes off in shade or indoors. All-day wear risks overheating and rubbing, so balance coverage with comfort and breaks.
