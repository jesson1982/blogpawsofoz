Sofa-Style, Flat Mattress or Raised Cot? The Three Shapes Compared

By Tessa NgataApr 10, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

There are a lot of bed shapes on the market, and most of them are variations on three. Once you can tell which of the three you're looking at, the whole category gets much easier to shop, because the choice stops being about fabric and colour and starts being about what your dog does with their body when they sleep.

I'm Tessa, a vet nurse and animal rehabilitation assistant in Hobart, and I've watched a great many dogs refuse a great many beds. The refusals are almost always shape-related rather than comfort-related. Here's the comparison, including the parts of each option that don't work.

Sofa-style with bolsters

This is the shape that looks most like furniture: a base with a raised padded edge along one, two or three sides. It suits dogs who lean. If your dog sleeps with their head hanging off a cushion, or wedged against the arm of the lounge, or pressed against your leg, a bolster gives them that same point of contact, and most of those dogs take to it immediately.

The raised edge also provides a sense of enclosure, which is genuinely settling for a lot of dogs. That's the reason these are sold as a calming shape, and I think the claim is fair in that narrow sense: something to lean into helps a dog settle. It is worth saying plainly, though, that a bed doesn't treat anxiety or any other condition. If your dog is distressed, destructive when left alone, or has suddenly changed their behaviour, that's a conversation with your vet or a qualified behaviourist rather than a purchase.

The trade-offs are real. That raised rim eats into usable sleeping space, so a medium sofa bed sleeps a noticeably smaller dog than a medium flat mattress. The sides trap heat, which makes this a poor summer choice outside Tasmania and Victoria. And the step over the bolster is a genuine obstacle for a stiff older dog — I've seen twelve-year-olds walk past a bolster bed because getting a back leg over it hurt. For those dogs a low entry on one side, or no bolster at all, is the kinder option.

Flat mattress

The least glamorous and, in my experience, the most used. A flat mattress gives maximum usable sleeping area for the footprint, which makes it the right call for any dog who stretches out. It slides into a crate, works under a desk, stacks in the laundry, and it's the easiest shape to wash and dry because there's no rim holding its shape.

It's also the shape I reach for with senior dogs, because there's nothing to step over, and with multi-dog households, because two dogs can share a flat mattress far more comfortably than a bolstered one.

What it lacks is head support and that enclosed feeling. A dog who likes to curl up tightly and press against something may use a flat mattress but never look properly settled on it, and they'll often be found burrowed under a blanket on top of it. If that's your dog, put a folded blanket on one half, or accept that you bought the wrong shape.

Raised cot

A stretched fabric panel on a frame, sitting ten to twenty centimetres off the ground. The whole point is airflow — air moves underneath as well as over, so it's the coolest of the three by a wide margin. That makes it the standout choice for humid summers, for outdoor use, and for dogs who run hot and spend their evenings on the tiles.

It's also the easiest to keep clean. Hose it off, wipe it dry, done. No cover to remove, nothing to put through a machine, nothing that holds onto odour. For a dog with allergies, or a household that washes bedding constantly, that's a strong practical argument.

Where it falls down is softness. A cot is firm and it flexes slightly under weight, and some dogs simply don't like either. Thin-coated breeds and older dogs with bony hips often find it uncomfortable, and I would not put an arthritic greyhound on one. They're also the least successful indoors in winter, because there's no insulation at all underneath. And a fabric panel will be chewed through quickly by a determined dog — worth knowing before you buy one for a puppy.

How to pick, and why most homes end up with two

Watch where and how your dog already sleeps, and match that rather than your idea of what they should like. A dog who sleeps flat out on their side with their legs in the air wants a flat mattress and will be frustrated by a shape built for a curler. A dog who sleeps tightly wound with their chin on something wants a bolster. A dog who seeks out the coolest surface in the house in February wants a cot, at least for three months of the year.

That last point is why the two-bed household is so common and so sensible. A padded sofa bed for winter and a cot or flat mat for summer covers the year properly in most of Australia, and it costs less in the long run than replacing one expensive bed that's wrong for half of it. Pip has a bolster bed in the lounge for winter and a flat mat she moves around after the sun in summer, and she uses both for what they're good at.

Placement still wins over everything. Whichever shape you choose, put it where the dog already sleeps. Move it later, a metre at a time, once it's being used.

If you take one thing from this, let it be that the shape matters more than the padding, the price or the colour, and that there is no single right answer for every dog or every season. Match the shape to the way your dog already sleeps and you'll get it right far more often than not. — Tessa, Hobart, where a greyhound is currently spread across a flat mat in a patch of afternoon sun.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which dog bed shape is best?

It depends entirely on how your dog sleeps. Bolstered sofa-style suits leaners and head-resters, a flat mattress suits dogs who stretch out and works in a crate, and a raised cot is the coolest option for hot weather and outdoor use. Match the shape to the dog, not the room.

Are raised dog beds better than mattresses?

Not universally. A raised cot gives better airflow, stays cooler and hoses clean, which suits humid climates, outdoor use and dogs who run hot. A mattress is softer, warmer and easier to wash in a machine, which suits older dogs, thin-coated breeds and southern winters.

Are bolster beds good for older dogs?

Sometimes, but check the step over the raised edge. A stiff or arthritic dog may find a tall bolster awkward or painful to climb over and will avoid the bed entirely. A flat mattress with nothing to step over, or a bolster with one low entry side, is usually the safer choice.

Do calming beds help an anxious dog?

A raised rim gives a dog something to lean against, which helps many dogs settle. That is a comfort benefit and not a treatment. A bed does not treat anxiety or any medical condition, so if your dog is distressed or their behaviour has changed, speak to your vet or a qualified behaviourist.

How many beds should a dog have?

Two is common and sensible in Australian conditions: a padded bed for the cold months and a flat mat or raised cot for summer. It also means one can be washed while the other is in use, and it lets you put a bed in each room the family actually sits in.
