Boarding, Groomers and Recovery: The Short-Term Stress Nobody Prepares You For

By Nadia BrooksFeb 10, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

Most of the anxiety advice people find is about long-term problems — thunderstorms, separation, noise. The ones that actually ambush you are the short ones. A two-week stay in a kennel over Christmas. A groomer appointment before a wedding. Ten days of strict rest after a cruciate repair. All of them are short, all of them are predictable, and almost nobody plans for them until the week before.

I've managed all three at my place, and the pattern is the same each time: the thing that makes them survivable isn't a product, it's rehearsal done well before the event.

Boarding: the trial night is not optional

We booked Juniper into a kennel for two nights in March, four months before we actually needed them for a wedding in July. That single trial told me more than any brochure. She ate on the first evening, refused breakfast the second morning, and came home with a stress-related bout of colitis that took a fortnight to settle.

So we changed approach entirely and found a sitter who keeps three dogs in her own house in the Adelaide Hills instead. Juniper went for a day visit, then an overnight, then a weekend, all before the real trip. She now gets dropped off with her own bed and her own food and comes home tired rather than wired.

Whatever option you pick, do one short stay first. A dog who has never been boarded and is dropped off for fourteen days is being asked to cope with absence, unfamiliar dogs, unfamiliar smells and unfamiliar routine all at once, in the two weeks of the year when the facility is busiest and loudest.

Grooming is a handling problem, not a vanity one

The groomer isn't frightening because of the scissors. It's frightening because a stranger holds your dog still and touches their feet, ears, tail and face for forty minutes, and most dogs have never agreed to any of that at home.

So we practised it. Five seconds of paw handling, then food. Then ten. Then a brush near the ear, then food. Four months of that and the groomer became an extension of something she already tolerated. I also asked for the first appointment of the day and told them plainly that she's noise-sensitive around dryers, which got us a quieter corner and a groomer who didn't take offence.

Recovery, which is the hardest of the three

Ten days of crate rest after a stifle repair nearly broke both of us. A dog who is used to running and is suddenly confined, in pain, and wearing a cone is a dog with nowhere to put any of it. What worked was structure rather than sympathy: a strict timetable, frozen Kongs on rotation, a rotation of three different resting spots so she wasn't staring at the same wall, and short leash-only trips to the garden for toilet breaks that were completely boring.

I also learned to expect a behaviour dip around day three and day seven. Knowing it was coming meant I didn't panic and unwind the whole plan at the first bad afternoon. Ask your vet what normal looks like before you start, because it varies a lot by procedure.

Where a collar does and doesn't belong

For boarding and grooming, timing is everything: on about a week before, off once they're home and settled. One collar runs roughly eight weeks, so a single one will usually cover a holiday including the recovery week afterwards. I put it on ahead of time rather than in the car on the way, because it needs days on the neck to do anything meaningful.

Post-surgery is the one case where I'd stop and ask your vet first. If there's an incision nearby, a collar could rub it or trap moisture, and some vets prefer the neck left completely clear while a wound heals. That's a thirty-second phone call and it's not worth guessing.

All three of these are predictable, which is the whole point. You know when the wedding is. You know when the surgery is booked. Whatever support you're going to use, start it a week early, rehearse the hard part once while the stakes are low, and tell the humans involved what your dog finds frightening. — Nadia, Adelaide, with a very well-rested greyhound.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I trial a kennel before a long stay?

Always. Book one night, ideally several months before you actually need it. A short stay tells you whether your dog eats, settles and comes home in good order, and gives you time to find an alternative if they don't.

How do I prepare my dog for grooming?

Practise handling at home months ahead: paws, ears, tail and muzzle, for a few seconds at a time with food. Book the first appointment of the day, and tell the groomer in advance what your dog finds frightening, such as dryers or nail clippers.

Can my dog wear a calming collar after surgery?

Ask your vet first. If there is an incision near the neck, or any wound that needs to stay dry and uncovered, your vet may prefer the area kept clear. It's a quick call and not worth guessing about.

How do I manage a dog on crate rest?

Use a strict routine, rotate enrichment like frozen Kongs, move their resting spot occasionally so the view changes, and keep toilet breaks short and boring. Ask your vet what a normal recovery dip looks like so you don't abandon the plan on a bad afternoon.

When should the collar go on before boarding?

About a week before drop-off, not on the day. It needs time on the neck to build up, and a single collar lasts around eight weeks, which usually covers a holiday plus the settling-in week afterwards.
