What UPF Means on a Dog Shirt (and Why Most Listings Won't Say)

By Maddie SorensenMar 5, 2026Pet Apparel6 min read

A client brought me three pet sun shirts she'd ordered online, all described as offering sun protection, none with a number anywhere on the listing or the packaging. She wanted to know which one was best for her white bull terrier, who had already had one vet visit for a burnt nose.

I couldn't tell her, and not because I didn't want to. There was no way to know from what she'd been given. That's the honest state of this category: sun protection is claimed freely and measured rarely, and it's worth knowing how to tell the difference.

What UPF actually measures

UPF stands for Ultraviolet Protection Factor, and it works like the SPF number on sunscreen but for fabric. A shirt rated UPF 50 allows roughly one fiftieth of the ultraviolet radiation through to the skin. UPF 30 lets through about one thirtieth. There's also a labelling ceiling — in the Australian and New Zealand standard, anything above 50 is labelled 50+, because the differences beyond that are small and the number stops being meaningful.

Australia and New Zealand developed the world's first standard for sun-protective clothing, which tells you something about how seriously this country takes UV. If a product cites a tested rating to that standard, it means something. If it just says sun protection with no number, it means the seller thinks the word will help them sell shirts.

Three things that decide how protective a fabric is

Weave density is the big one. Hold a fabric up to a bright light. If you can see clearly through the weave, UV is getting through too. A tight weave blocks more, regardless of what the fabric is made of.

Colour matters more than people assume. Dark and saturated colours absorb more UV before it reaches the skin than pale ones do. This is mildly annoying for hot climates, because dark clothes also absorb more visible heat — which is why the better summer shirts use light colours with a tight weave and rely on density rather than dye.

Fibre type matters too. Polyester and nylon generally perform better than cotton or linen, largely because they can be woven tightly and hold their structure. This is useful news for anyone buying pet clothing, because most of the lightweight technical fabrics used in this category are polyester anyway.

Wet and stretched fabric performs worse

Two things quietly undo a shirt's protection. Getting it wet reduces the protection of many fabrics, sometimes significantly — which is worth knowing if you're putting a sun shirt on a dog who's just been in the water. And stretching a fabric pulls the weave open, so a shirt that's too tight is protecting less than the same shirt in the correct size.

Both point the same way: if you're relying on a shirt for sun protection, fit it properly and let it dry. A stretched, damp shirt is doing a fraction of what you think.

What a shirt can't cover

This is the part I keep coming back to, because it's where people get a false sense of security. A shirt covers the back and flanks, which is a large area and worth covering. It does not cover the nose, the ear tips, the eye area or the belly, and on a pale dog those are precisely the places that burn first.

So a shirt is one tool in the set, not the set. The rest is timing — keeping pets out of the sun between roughly ten and three — shade, and for the exposed bits, either a pet-safe sunscreen from your vet or a hat for dogs who will tolerate one, which is a smaller group than the internet suggests.

How I'd actually shop for one

Look for a stated tested rating rather than the words sun protection. Then check the weave yourself by holding it up to the light. Then check the fit, because a shirt that rides up or stretches across the back is protecting a fraction of what you paid for. Then be realistic about what's left uncovered.

And be sceptical of any listing making medical claims — preventing skin cancer, treating sun damage, anything phrased like a treatment. That's overselling, and in Australia it also brushes up against consumer law. A decent product says it helps reduce UV exposure. It doesn't promise outcomes.

The client's three shirts were, as far as I could tell, near-identical products with three different listings. She kept the one with the tightest weave and the best fit, and we booked her dog in for a nose check. If you remember one thing: hold it up to the light. That tells you more than the packaging will. — Maddie, Perth, permanently squinting at fabric.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does UPF 50 mean for pet clothing?

UPF stands for Ultraviolet Protection Factor. A UPF 50 fabric allows roughly one fiftieth of UV radiation through to the skin. Australia and New Zealand set the standard for this rating, and anything above 50 is labelled 50+.

How can I tell if a pet shirt blocks UV without a rating?

Hold the fabric up to a bright light. If you can see clearly through the weave, UV is passing through too. Tight weaves, darker colours and synthetic fibres such as polyester generally perform better than loose weaves and pale cotton.

Does a wet shirt still protect against the sun?

Less well. Many fabrics lose protection when wet, and stretching a fabric pulls the weave open, so a shirt that is too tight also protects less. Fit it properly and let it dry between swims.

What areas does a sun shirt not cover?

The nose, ear tips, eye area and belly. On pale and short-coated dogs those are the areas that burn first, so a shirt is one part of the plan. Add shade, sensible timing and a pet-safe sunscreen from your vet for exposed skin.

Should I avoid listings that make medical claims?

Yes. Products claiming to prevent skin cancer or treat sun damage are overselling, and that kind of claim also raises consumer law issues in Australia. A reasonable product says it helps reduce UV exposure.
