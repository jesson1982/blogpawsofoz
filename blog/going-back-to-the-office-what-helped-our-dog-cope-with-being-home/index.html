Going Back to the Office: What Helped Our Dog Cope With Being Home Alone

By Nadia BrooksFeb 10, 2026Calming & Anxiety7 min read

Three weeks of annual leave and Juniper had forgotten what an empty house felt like. There was someone in the kitchen at all hours, someone to follow to the letterbox, someone to lie on. Then the second Monday in January arrived, I picked up my keys at seven-fifteen, and by the time I'd reversed out of the driveway she'd worked out that the pattern had changed. I know this because I'd left my phone recording on the bench, and I watched the whole eight hours on my lunch break.

It wasn't the destruction I'd braced for. It was worse in a quieter way. She walked from the front window to the back door for forty minutes, then lay down facing the front door and didn't move for six hours. She didn't eat. She didn't drink. She just waited. That video was the thing that made me take separation anxiety seriously rather than shrugging about it.

The collar on its own did nothing, and I want to be honest about that

I tried the calm collar first because it was the easiest thing to buy, and I'll save you the three weeks I wasted: on its own it changed nothing measurable. Juniper still waited by the door and still refused her breakfast. That's not a criticism of the collar so much as a criticism of what I expected from it. A collar can take the jagged edge off a stress response, but it cannot teach a dog that being alone is survivable. Only repetition does that.

What it did do, once I'd built the rest of the plan around it, was make the training possible. She was calmer enough to actually engage with a frozen Kong instead of ignoring it, and that was the whole point. If you're looking at separation anxiety support and hoping one product will solve it, I'd gently lower that expectation now rather than in three weeks.

The departure routine was the part that worked

Separation distress almost always starts before you leave. Juniper wasn't reacting to my absence; she was reacting to the twenty minutes of signals that predicted it. Keys, shoes, bag, the specific way I said goodbye. So I dismantled all of it. I started picking up my keys and then sitting back down. Putting my shoes on and then doing the dishes. Walking to the door and coming straight back. Forty times a day, for about ten days, until the cues meant absolutely nothing.

Then I rebuilt absences from nothing. I walked out the front door and came back in through it four seconds later. Then thirty seconds. Then two minutes. Then nine. The rule was that she never got to the point of distress, and if she did, I'd gone too far too fast. It's tedious, unglamorous work and it took most of February, but it's the reason she now sleeps in the loungeroom instead of the hallway.

Where the collar ended up fitting

Once the training was underway, I put the collar back on and this time it earned its place. It went on about ten days before I went back to work rather than on the morning I started, because it needs time on the neck to build up, and one collar lasts roughly eight weeks. That timing meant it was already working when the hard days arrived.

I fitted it with two fingers of slack under the collar, measured with a soft tape rather than guessed from her weight, and I left it on through the day and took it off in the evenings for the first week so she didn't associate it only with me leaving. It's a small, boring support that runs in the background while the real work happens. That's genuinely all it is, and it's enough.

The boring things that mattered more than I expected

A frozen Kong given the second I walked out, then removed when I got home so it stayed special. A radio left on low in a different room from the one she slept in. A proper walk before work rather than after, because a tired dog settles faster and I'd been doing it backwards for years. Blinds left exactly as I found them so the light in the house didn't change. None of this is clever, and all of it helped more than anything I bought.

I also want to say that if your dog is injuring themselves, howling continuously, or not eating for a full day, this is a vet conversation, not a blog conversation. Prescribed medication alongside a behaviour plan is a completely normal outcome for severe cases, and there's no prize for struggling through it alone.

Six weeks in, she eats her breakfast after I leave and sleeps in the loungeroom with the radio on. She still watches the driveway more than I'd like, and that's probably just her. If you're heading back to the office in January, start in December — that's the only piece of advice I'd push on anyone. — Nadia, Adelaide, writing this while a greyhound snores behind the couch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a calming collar stop separation anxiety on its own?

Usually not. It may take the edge off the stress response, but teaching a dog that being alone is safe requires gradual absence training and a desensitised departure routine. Think of the collar as background support that makes training easier, not as a replacement for it.

When should I put the collar on before returning to work?

About seven to ten days before your first day back. These collars need time on the neck to build up, and a single collar typically releases for around sixty days, so it will still be working through the hardest weeks.

Why does my dog react to me picking up my keys?

Because those cues reliably predict you leaving, and your dog has learned the sequence. Picking up keys and then staying home, dozens of times a day, breaks the prediction and takes most of the anxiety out of your departure routine.

How do I know if it's separation anxiety or boredom?

Distress that begins within minutes of you leaving, refusing food, and pacing or vocalising continuously point towards anxiety. Destructive behaviour that starts an hour in, or only when there's something to reach, is more often boredom. A video of an ordinary day tells you which one you're dealing with.

When should I involve my vet?

If your dog is injuring themselves trying to escape, howling without pause, refusing food or water for a full day, or showing no improvement after several weeks of consistent training, book a vet appointment and ask about a referral to a veterinary behaviourist.
