Bumper Flap and Side Walls: Protecting More Than the Boot Floor

By Mick O'DonnellAug 22, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers6 min read

Mick from the Bush — dog car-travel & ute gear

A flat liner on the boot floor is a start, but it is only half the story. The damage to a car does not stop at the carpet. Banjo does not step neatly into the boot like a city dog at the vet. He launches. He lands on the metal lip of the tailgate, scrabbles for grip, and claws his way in. Without the right bits on the liner, that lip and those side panels cop it every single time.

I learned this the hard way with the old wagon. Two years of Banjo launches and the paint on the bumper was gone, the plastic was scored, and the panel beater in town started greeting me by name. A bumper flap dog boot liner would have saved me a few hundred bucks. Live and learn, as the old man used to say.

The bumper flap is the unsung hero

The bumper flap is the strip of material that hangs down over the outside edge of the tailgate when the boot is open. When your dog jumps in or out, their claws hit the flap, not your paint. It sounds small. It is not. That lip is exactly where the wear shows first, and once the paint is gone the metal starts to rust, and then you have a real problem.

When we go camping, the flap earns its place twice over. The esky comes out, the swags come out, the wet dog towel comes out, and all of it scrapes the lip. The flap takes it. On the drive home from a wet weekend, Banjo shakes in the boot and the water runs down the flap instead of pooling on the paint. A dog boot liner for camping without a flap is, to my mind, half a liner.

Side walls keep the mess off the trim

The side walls are the taller panels that run up the inside of the boot, protecting the plastic and the lower door cards. Banjo leans on them when he stands to look out the back window. A working dog does not sit still. Side walls stop the claws marking the trim and stop the mud from the beach running down into the panel gaps.

They also corral the sand. On a flat liner, sand slides to the edges and vanishes into the seams. With side walls, it stays in the tray and I tip it out at the servo with a broom. Small thing, but the missus notices when the car is clean, and that is worth something.

Non-slip base and waterproof build

The non-slip backing is what keeps the whole liner where you put it. An EVA or rubberised base grips the carpet so the liner does not slide forward when you brake hard on the gravel. The waterproof layer, usually a PU or coated Oxford face, keeps the water off the carpet underneath. Out here, "water-resistant" is not good enough. You want "waterproof" printed on the box.

A dog cargo liner for SUV use should have all four of those as a baseline. If a product skips the flap or the side walls to hit a price point, you are trading a few dollars for panel damage down the track. I have seen it a hundred times.

Materials and what holds up

The liners that survive regional life are 600D Oxford with a PP cotton fill and an EVA backing. The Oxford face shrugs off claws, the fill gives Banjo a bit of cushion on the long drive, and the EVA keeps it planted. The top-shelf versions use PU leather, which wipes clean faster than fabric and looks smart, but it warms up in the sun, so I would not leave a dog on it in a hot car — nobody should leave a dog in a hot car, full stop.

Getting the fit right

Side walls only work if they actually reach the panels. Measure your boot height, not just the floor. A liner sized for a small hatch will leave a gap on a big wagon, and that gap is where the mud gets through. Most SUV liners run 40 by 70 inches up to 55 by 91 inches flat, and the wall height varies, so check both numbers before you buy.

If you camp, the flap is non-negotiable. If your dog is calm and clean, you might get away with a flat liner and skip the walls, but Banjo has never been calm or clean a day in his life, so we run the full setup. The dog cargo liner for SUV that we use has the flap, the walls, the grip and the waterproof face, and after three years the boot still looks new.

Frequently asked questions

What does a bumper flap actually do?

It is the strip that hangs over the tailgate lip when the boot is open. Your dog's claws hit the flap instead of the paint, which stops the scoring and rust that starts once the paint is gone.

Are side walls worth the extra cost?

If your dog stands, leans or shakes in the boot, yes. They protect the trim from claws and keep sand and mud in the liner instead of down the panel gaps.

Will a non-slip base really stop sliding on gravel?

An EVA or rubberised backing grips the carpet and holds the liner in place under hard braking. A liner without it slides forward and bunches, which is worse than no liner.

Is a bumper flap dog boot liner good for camping?

It is close to essential. The flap takes the scrapes from eskies and swags as well as the dog, and it channels water away from the paint on the drive home.

PU leather or 600D Oxford for a working dog?

Oxford with EVA backing is cooler and tough enough. PU leather wipes faster and looks smarter but heats up in the sun, so never leave a dog on it in a warm car.

How do I know the side walls will reach my panels?

Measure boot height as well as floor length and width. Check the liner's wall height against your car. A gap means mud gets through, so size up rather than down.
