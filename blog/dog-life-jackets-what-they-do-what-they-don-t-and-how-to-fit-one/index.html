Dog Life Jackets: What They Do, What They Don't, and How to Fit One

By Maddie SorensenMar 5, 2026Pet Apparel8 min read

I used to think a dog life jacket was mostly something people bought for the photograph. Olive is a whippet. She swims like an otter on the days she feels like it. Then I spent an afternoon at a boat ramp on the Swan River with a client and her three-year-old bulldog, and I watched what fifteen minutes of enthusiastic paddling did to a dog with a chest like a barrel and a face like a squashed cushion. He wasn't drowning. He was tired, his head was going lower every minute, and he was swallowing a lot of river.

We got him back up the ramp and he was fine. What stayed with me was how little his owner had seen coming. Since then I've fitted a good deal of flotation gear and talked a fair few people out of the wrong purchase. Here is what I tell them.

What a flotation vest actually does

A vest buys time. That's the honest version. It adds buoyancy so a tired dog doesn't have to work as hard to keep their head up, it gives you a handle to grab, and it makes a dark animal in murky water easier to see. That matters at boat ramps and on rock platforms, where a slip puts a dog into deep water with nothing to climb out on.

What it does not do is make a dog safe. I want to be blunt, because the marketing here is slippery. A flotation aid does not prevent drowning, and it will not stop a dog panicking, swallowing water or getting swept under a moored boat. What keeps a dog alive is an adult watching them. The vest gives that adult a few more seconds and something to hold.

The dogs that genuinely need one

Some dogs are naturals and some absolutely are not. The ones I fit without hesitation are the heavy-chested dogs who sit low and paddle hard in short bursts: bulldogs, Frenchies, pugs, staffies, mastiff crosses. Short-legged dogs are the second group — a dachshund or a corgi works twice as hard for half the result.

Brachycephalic breeds get a mention twice over, because they have the chest problem and the airway problem at once: a flat face sits forward and low, and holding the nose clear takes effort. Add older dogs with arthritis, dogs with chronic ear trouble, and any dog who's anxious in new places.

Then there's the category that surprises people: good swimmers on boats. Olive can out-swim me and I'd still put a vest on her for a run to Rottnest, because a tired dog two hundred metres from a boat ramp is not a strong swimmer.

Float vest or full life jacket?

The names get thrown around interchangeably and it confuses everyone. Most of what you'll see in Australia is a flotation vest: closed-cell foam over the back and chest, a belly strap, a neck opening and a handle. It supports a conscious dog who is swimming. It is not designed to roll an unconscious animal face-up, and I'd be sceptical of any pet product claiming it does. There's no Australian standard I'd point you to, so I judge on shape, on where the buoyancy sits, and on whether it stays put when a wet dog shakes.

What I look for, in order

The handle comes first, and not just any handle. You need to get a whole hand through it and lift, because hauling a wet fifteen-kilo dog out of the water is the entire reason it exists. I've seen handles stitched on with a single line of thread I wouldn't trust with groceries.

Second, a D-ring or leash attachment on the back, clear of the handle so the lead doesn't tangle when you grab. Third, colour: red, orange, bright yellow. Not navy, not black, not a tasteful grey with a subtle stripe. A dog in the water is a small dark shape.

Reflective trim matters more than people think, because the dog sits low where your torch beam is weakest. Then the important one: buoyancy under the chest rather than a collar of foam up at the neck, which tends to tip a dog forward and put their face down. Look for a panel running from between the front legs back along the belly.

Fitting it, and the mistake I made

Measure the chest at its widest point behind the front legs and the back from the base of the neck to the base of the tail, then match those to the brand's own chart. Weight alone is a terrible guide: a lean whippet and a stocky staffy can weigh the same and be entirely different shapes.

Two fingers flat under every strap and no more. Snug through the chest is what stops the vest sliding sideways or riding up over the head when a dog rolls. Loose is not safer; it's a vest that isn't there any more.

My own mistake was skipping the water test. I fitted one perfectly on a client's labrador in her driveway, took the money and drove off. She rang me two days later to say the jacket was riding up around the dog's ears the moment she paddled. Buoyancy pushes a garment away from the water, and nothing in a driveway tells you that. Test in shallow water, dog supported by hand, before you trust it anywhere deeper.

Let the dog get used to it dry first. Ten minutes in the loungeroom, treats, no water. A dog who panics the first time the foam lifts them will remember it.

Rinse it in fresh water after every swim and dry it in the shade. Salt and chlorine break the fabric down, and a vest left damp in a boot box grows something unpleasant within a week. Check the handle stitching each season; if the foam has gone flat, it has had its run.

And a vest is only one part of dog swimming safety in Australia, alongside knowing the water and staying close enough to intervene. I carry one on the ute from October to April and I still don't take my eyes off Olive when she's in the river.

Olive has three coats and a flotation vest she tolerates with poor grace, and she'll never need any of them as much as she needs me to look up from my phone. Buy the vest. Then watch the dog. — Maddie, Perth, wet shoes, towel on the passenger seat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my dog need a life jacket in Australia?

If your dog is heavy-chested, short-legged, flat-faced, older, or spends time on a boat, a jetty or a rock platform, then yes, it is worth carrying one. I also fit them for strong swimmers who swim for a long time, because fatigue is what catches good swimmers out.

Can a dog life jacket prevent drowning?

No. I want to be very clear about this. A flotation vest is an aid that helps a conscious, tired dog stay up and gives you a handle to grab. It does not prevent drowning, and it is never a substitute for actively supervising your dog around water.

What is the difference between a dog float vest and a full life jacket?

Most dog products sold in Australia are flotation vests: foam panels, a belly strap, a neck opening and a handle, designed to support a swimming dog. They are not certified marine life jackets and they are not designed to roll an unconscious dog face-up. Treat them as an aid, not as safety equipment.

How tight should a dog flotation vest be?

Two fingers flat under every strap and no more. Snug through the chest, with the buoyancy panel sitting under the chest rather than bunched at the neck. Then test it in shallow water with the dog supported, because buoyancy lifts the garment and a vest that fits on the driveway can ride up over the ears in the water.

Can all dogs swim?

No, and it surprises owners every summer. Plenty of dogs paddle instinctively, but heavy-chested breeds, short-legged breeds and flat-faced breeds often cannot keep their nose clear for long. Others simply panic. Never assume a dog can swim until you have watched them do it in shallow, calm water with you right beside them.
