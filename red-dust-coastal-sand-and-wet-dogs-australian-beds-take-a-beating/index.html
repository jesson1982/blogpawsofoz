Red Dust, Coastal Sand and Wet Dogs: Australian Beds Take a Beating

By Tessa NgataApr 10, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

Short answer: the bedding advice written for grass-and-carpet homes fails here, so match the wash routine to the conditions, not the price tag. I've worked with a kelpie in Kalgoorlie and a labrador near a Sunshine Coast beach: red dust, salt sand and wet fur make beds filthy faster and hold odour longer than any catalogue predicts. In short, shake and vacuum before the machine, wash on a proper cycle, and dry in the sun. None of it is alarming, just a different climate.

Most of the pet bedding advice online was written for a climate that looks nothing like ours. It assumes a dog who walks on grass, comes inside dry, and lives in a house with carpet and central heating. Then it gets applied to a kelpie in Kalgoorlie or a labrador two streets back from a Sunshine Coast beach, and quietly falls apart.

I have worked with dogs at both ends of that range, and the pattern is consistent. Pet bedding here gets dirty faster, holds odour longer, and fails in ways that have little to do with what it cost. None of this is alarming — it is just a matter of matching the cleaning routine to the conditions.

Four conditions that make the difference

Red dust across Western Australia and the Northern Territory is the one people talk about most. It is not ordinary dirt sitting on the surface. It is iron oxide ground fine enough to behave like a pigment, and once a dog has worked it into the fabric by turning in circles before lying down, it stains. It also holds a faint metallic smell that a normal wash does not shift.

Coastal sand and salt affect far more Australians, and they cause a different set of problems. Sand is abrasive: every time a damp dog lies down, those grains grind against the fibres and wear the fabric thin from the inside out. Salt draws moisture out of the air and holds it in the fabric, so a beach dog's bed never quite feels dry.

Humidity is the one that does real damage to beds that are otherwise fine. From about Brisbane north, and through the Sydney and Melbourne humid stretches, a bed that goes back on the floor slightly damp will develop mildew. Once that is established in a foam core, it is very hard to remove.

The fourth is simply water. A wet dog on a bed several times a week is a very different load from an occasional damp patch.

Before the machine: shake, vacuum, rinse

The mistake almost everyone makes is putting a dusty or sandy bed straight into the washing machine. You are not washing the grit out, you are washing it into the mechanism and the filter, and you are grinding it through the fabric at the same time.

Take it outside first and shake it hard. Then vacuum both sides with the upholstery attachment, paying attention to the seams where grit collects. If the bed is sandy or salty, give it a cold rinse in the tub or a thorough hosing before it goes anywhere near the machine. Salt dissolves; sand needs to be flushed out, not agitated. That one step will add a year to the life of the cover.

How often to wash a dog bed

A fortnightly cover wash is a reasonable baseline for a healthy adult dog who lives mostly indoors. That is not an official rule, just what keeps ahead of odour and skin oils in most households.

Adjust it both ways. In the humid months, or if your dog swims regularly, weekly is better. In a dry inland climate with a mostly outdoor dog, three or four weeks is often fine. Puppies, dogs with skin conditions, and dogs who have had an accident need washing on the spot rather than on a schedule.

Wash immediately after any accident, any illness, and after any flea or parasite treatment. If a dog in the house has been diagnosed with something contagious, ask your vet what they want done with the bedding, because the answer is sometimes a hot wash and sometimes disposal.

Washing and drying properly

Check the label first, then wash the cover on its own. Dog hair and grit in with your clothes is unpleasant, and a near-empty drum lets the fabric move properly. Use a warm cycle rather than hot unless the label permits it, since heat degrades water-repellent finishes and elasticised seams. A cup of white vinegar in the rinse neutralises odour and is safer around pets than heavily perfumed softeners.

Avoid fabric softener. It coats the fibres, reduces absorbency and leaves a residue against a dog's skin. For stubborn smells, a pre-soak in warm water with an enzyme-based cleaner does more than any amount of fragrance, because it breaks down the organic material causing the smell rather than masking it. That is the one product I would actually spend money on.

Drying is where most people lose. The cover must be completely dry before it goes back on the bed — dry in the seams and the piping, not just on the flat panels. Full sun is ideal, since sunlight does much of the deodorising for free. If it has to be a dryer, use low heat and check it twice.

A routine that holds up

What I do at home, and suggest to clients, is unglamorous and takes five minutes. Shake and vacuum the beds on a Sunday. Wash the covers every second Sunday, more often in summer. Hose before washing after any beach or river week. Rotate between two covers so a wet one is never an emergency. Air the inners in the sun whenever a cover comes off.

None of that needs special products beyond an enzyme cleaner, and all of it is easier than replacing a bed every year. In dusty and coastal areas, buying a spare cover when you buy the bed is the best decision you can make — the cover is the part that wears, and having one on hand means the bed never has to come off the floor.

I used to think a bed that smelled bad two days after washing was a bad bed. It was usually just a bed that went back on the floor damp. Sort the drying out and most odour problems disappear on their own. — Tessa, Hobart, currently waiting for a greyhound to move off the only sunny patch of lawn.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I wash my dog's bed in Australia?

A fortnightly cover wash is a sensible baseline for a healthy indoor dog. Move to weekly in humid months or if your dog swims or sleeps inside after being outdoors. Wash immediately after accidents, illness, or parasite treatment rather than waiting for the usual day.

How do I get red dust stains out of a dog bed?

Shake it out and vacuum both sides first, then rinse cold before machine washing — never put a dusty cover straight into the machine. Red dust is iron oxide and behaves like a pigment, so a pre-soak with an enzyme-based cleaner before a warm wash gives you the best chance.

Why does my dog bed still smell after washing?

Almost always because it went back on the floor before it was fully dry, especially through the seams and piping, and mildew has taken hold. Dry covers in full sun or on low heat and check them twice. A cup of white vinegar in the rinse also helps neutralise odour.

Should I rinse a sandy or salty bed before machine washing?

Yes, every time. Sand is abrasive and will wear the fabric from the inside and clog your machine, while salt holds moisture in the fibres. A cold hosing or a tub rinse first, then a normal warm wash, will noticeably extend the life of a washable dog bed in Australia.

Can I use fabric softener on pet bedding?

I would not. Softener coats the fibres, cuts absorbency and leaves a residue against your dog's skin, which some dogs find irritating. Use white vinegar in the rinse instead, and an enzyme-based pre-soak for stubborn smells, since that breaks down the cause rather than masking it.
