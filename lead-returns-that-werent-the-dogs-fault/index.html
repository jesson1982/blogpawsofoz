The Lead Returns That Weren't the Dog's Fault

By Nina CarusoSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear7 min read

Short answer: the lead failures that come back to us are visible a fortnight before they happen, and the check that catches them takes fifteen seconds a month. Thumb-test the clip, run the webbing or rope through your hands, look at both stitching joins in daylight, and give the handle a firm tug. Clips that pop open, frayed splices and slipping retractable brakes are replacement jobs rather than repairs.

Nobody writes in to say the dog behaved badly. Lead returns come with a story, and the story is almost always about hardware: a clip that let go at the park, a rope that frayed through in a fortnight, a retractable that jammed and then whipped back. I take these messages all day, and the pattern is consistent enough that I can now predict which lead a customer is going to send back by looking at what they ordered. Here are the five failures, how each one announces itself, and the check that catches it early.

Failure one: the clip that opened

The most alarming message I get, and the most preventable. Trigger clips fail in two ways: the gate spring softens until the gate can be opened by a twist against a harness ring, or the clip's swivel seizes and the whole assembly works loose. Both are visible before they fail. Test the gate with your thumb each week: it should snap shut with a firm spring and no rattle, and it should not open when you twist the clip against a fixed ring. If the clip is the cheap pressed-metal kind on a lightweight lead and your dog is strong, that combination is the one that ends up in the incident story.

Failure two: the rope that frayed

Rope leads fail at the same place every time: where the rope enters the clip or handle. A splice with whipping survives; a heat-sealed end stiffens, cracks and then quietly loses strands under the collar of the splice. The tell is a fuzzy patch or a stiff, shiny section of rope near the hardware. Run the rope through your fingers every few walks, and if you feel a rough patch or see core showing through the outer braid, retire the lead. It costs less than the alternatives by a wide margin.

Failure three: the retractable we refund quietly

I will be careful here, because plenty of people use retractable leads happily. But they occupy a big slice of our lead returns for two reasons: the locking mechanism wears until it stops holding under load, and the cord itself becomes the complaint, usually after it has run across someone's hand or leg during a sudden sprint. We refund these rather than argue, because the customer's experience is what it is and the mechanism genuinely does wear out. If you use one, test the brake before every walk by locking it and pulling firmly, and retire the lead the first time it slips.

Failure four: the lead that was the wrong length in practice

Not a defect, and still the reason behind a good number of returns. People buy a 1.2-metre lead for a suburban walk and find it too short for a sniffy dog, or buy a 1.8-metre lead for busy streets and find themselves managing slack in traffic. The number on the packet means nothing until the dog is on the end of it. My advice is to be honest about your usual walk: if you cross roads and stand at school gates, get the double-handle design so you can switch between lengths mid-walk. If you are in a park all day, go longer. The regret in both directions is the same, and it is entirely avoidable.

Failure five: chewed in the first week

Puppies and adolescent dogs chew leads because they are boring, dangling and attached to excitement. A lead chewed through in week one is not a defect and rarely a return we can accept, but it is very preventable: keep the lead looped high rather than trailing on the ground while you put shoes on, do not leave it attached to a tethered dog in the yard, and give the dog something legal to chew instead. If your dog has already chewed two leads, buy the cheapest one that fits properly for the next six months and expect it to be the sacrificial one, because webbing left within reach of a teenage dog is not a matter of if.

The maintenance habit, and why it is worth fifteen seconds

Once a month, sit down with the lead on your lap and do four things: thumb-test the clip, run the webbing or rope through your hands, look at both stitching joins in daylight, and give the handle a firm tug. Then put it back on the hook. Fifteen seconds, replaced with a new lead when anything fails the test rather than when it fails at the park. In all the lead failure messages I have read, I can count on one hand the ones where the owner was surprised; almost everybody had noticed something odd and kept using it. That small habit is the entire difference.

  • Thumb-test the clip gate, then twist it against a harness ring to check the spring.
  • Run the webbing or rope through your fingers and feel for rough or stiff patches.
  • Look at both stitching joins in daylight rather than in a hallway.
  • Give the handle a firm tug, then hang the lead back on its hook.

Frequently Asked Questions

My lead's clip pops open when my dog pulls hard. What should I do?

Stop using it immediately and replace it with a bolt snap or a heavier trigger clip. That popping sensation means the gate is not engaging properly, and the next hard pull is the one that releases. Take a photo before you retire it, because if the clip failed within a reasonable period the seller may replace it under warranty, and a clear photo of the mechanism makes that conversation quick.

How long should a lead last?

With a monthly check and a dog that does not chew, a decent webbing or rope lead should give you years. What kills them early is load beyond their design (a strong dog on a light lead), chewing, and being left on a tethered dog in the weather. Treat it as a consumable rather than a fixture: cheap enough to replace, important enough to inspect, and never worth stretching one more week out of.

Are retractable leads safe for small dogs?

For a calm small dog in open space with an attentive owner, they can be. The problems arrive with tension: thin cord under load, a lock that stops holding, and the recoil if the mechanism releases. If you use one, do the brake test before every walk and keep a fixed lead in the car as a backup. The mixed approach most owners settle on is retractable for the park and a fixed lead for anywhere with roads.

What is the strongest lead attachment for a big dog?

A bolt snap on a wide webbing lead with multi-pass stitching, attached to a harness with a welded ring. That combination covers the load path from the dog's chest to your hand without a weak link. If your dog is a serious puller, a double-ended lead clipped to two points on the harness spreads the load further and reduces the sideways swing that makes big dogs hard to hold.

Can I repair a frayed lead instead of replacing it?

For a rope lead, a proper splice with whipping is a real repair if you know how, and some rope leads are designed to be re-spliced. For webbing, a frayed join means the stitching is compromised and a home repair is not worth trusting. Given what a lead costs against what a loose dog on a road costs, my honest recommendation is always replacement, and I have never had a customer tell me that was the wrong call.
