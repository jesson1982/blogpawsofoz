When the Power Goes Out: A Storm Plan for Anxious Dogs

By Rebecca HalloranSep 1, 2026Calming & Anxiety7 min read

Short answer: When a storm knocks out the power, your usual calming aids stop working at the worst possible moment. Build a low-tech backup before the season so your dog has a dark, contained, familiar space that does not depend on electricity to feel safe.

Last January a storm rolled over the Gold Coast and took the grid down for most of the night. Messages came in from owners whose dogs had never coped with thunder, the diffuser dead, the white noise machine silent, the house pitch black. I added a power-outage page to the safety checklist I hand out, because the calm plan most people build relies on something with a plug. If the plug stops, the plan stops. A dog does not need electronics to feel safe. It needs a small space it knows, a familiar smell, and an owner who is not flapping with a phone torch. That is the room, the dog and the household, in that order.

Preparing the room before the storm arrives

Pick the room your dog already gravitates to during noise and make it the storm room before any warning. A spare bedroom, the laundry, or a corner of the lounge with a closed door all work away from the windows that rattle. The point is a space that already smells of home, not something new on the night. Put the dog's bed, a worn t-shirt of yours, and a few toys in there a week ahead so the scent is settled. Tape cardboard over the worst rattling frame so the noise has one less way in. Charge a torch and leave it on a high shelf. None of this needs power when the grid goes down.

What to do when the lights go out

When the power drops, your first job is your own behaviour, not the dog's. Move slowly, keep your voice low, and do not shine a bright torch in the dog's face, because sudden light in a dark room spikes stress for everyone. Use the torch to find the storm room, guide the dog in, and close the door so the house outside stays a mystery. If your dog is crate trained, the crate is already there, so you are simply shutting a familiar door. Sit on the floor rather than looming, speak in the few words you always use, and let the dark do the work. Keep phones in your pocket with the screen dimmed.

Containing the dog without powered gear

The diffuser and the white noise machine are the two aids owners lose first, and both are nice to have rather than need to have. Containment is what matters when the power is gone. A dog that paces during thunder finds every rattling window and every flashing neighbour's light, so keep the world small with a closed door. If your dog is not crate trained, a baby gate across the doorway still limits the space without trapping the dog against a wall. Avoid tying the dog up, because a tethered dog that panics has no way to move to where it feels safer. The goal is a room the dog can choose to hide in, with you nearby and the door shut.

Household tasks that make the night easier

A blackout is easier on a dog if the rest of the household is not in chaos. Fill the esky with ice and keep water in it so you are not opening the fridge each time the dog drinks. Charge every power bank you own when a storm is forecast, and keep one torch per person in a known spot. Tell everyone the plan before the storm, including the kids, so nobody bolts the storm room door or lets the dog out to investigate. Write the after-hours vet number on paper and stick it to the fridge, because a dead phone will not show a contact list. Staying calm so your dog stays settled

Your own nervous system is the strongest signal in the room, and dogs read it before they read the weather. If you are jumpy, the dog is jumpy, so keep your voice and movements steady during a blackout. Breathe out long, move with purpose, and do not narrate the storm in a worried tone. Some owners find that sitting on the floor with a dim phone, saying nothing, settles the dog faster than talking. If the dog comes to you, let it, but do not chase or scoop it up. A dog that chooses to lean is making its own decision to feel safe.

Packing a power-outage kit for next time

Once the storm passes, plan the next morning, not next spring. Pack a small kit and leave it in the storm room so the next blackout is a known routine instead of a scramble. Include two torches, one battery radio, a power bank, a roll of tape, and a worn item of your clothing. Add a written list of the after-hours vet contact and the dog's microchip number, and ask your vet before changing anything about a treatment plan. Check the batteries every three months and swap them for fresh ones, the way you check a smoke alarm. A kit that is packed is the difference between a dog contained in the dark and one loose among flashing lights.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I drive my dog somewhere during a blackout storm?

If the storm is active, stay put. Driving in high wind and falling branches is riskier than a dark lounge room. Keep the dog contained at home and wait the weather out. If you think the dog is hurt, phone your vet or an after-hours clinic first and follow their advice.

Can I use a candle for light in the storm room?

No. An open flame in a room with a stressed dog is a fire risk, and a dog that knocks a candle over creates a second emergency. Use a torch on a high shelf the dog cannot reach, or sit by a phone glow in your pocket. Battery light is safer and keeps the room dim.

What if my dog panics and tries to escape the room?

Do not chase it, because a panicking dog reads the chase as another threat and runs harder. Block the route with your body or a closed door, then give it space and let the noise pass. A covered crate in the storm room is calmest, since the sides cut the flashing light. If escaping behaviour is new or worse, that is a question for your vet.

How do I know if the dog is actually hurt?

You are looking for changes you can see, not a diagnosis. Limping, a wound, or a dog that will not get up are reasons to phone the clinic. I do not interpret symptoms, that is the vet's job, but I can tell you what to look for. Keep the dog still and ring the number on the fridge.

My dog is fine after the storm, do I still call the vet?

If the dog is eating, drinking, moving and behaving as normal once the noise stops, you probably do not need to call. Watch for changes over the next day, because some issues show up later. Keep water available and let the dog rest. If anything looks off, a quick call to your vet costs less than waiting.
