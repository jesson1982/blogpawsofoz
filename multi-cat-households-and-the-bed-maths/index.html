Multi-Cat Households and the Bed Maths Nobody Warns You About

By Ruth AldertonSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: buy one more bed than you think you need and put it in a second room. Cats claim by preference rather than evenly, so a household of four will contest two beds, ignore one and compete for the sunny spot at 3pm. Place every bed against a wall with a clear sightline to the doorway, prefer flat mattresses for cats who have to negotiate access, and keep a spare cover.

Thirty cats under one roof, changing over every few days, taught me more about furniture than any amount of reading. The lesson that surprised me most is that beds are not sleeping places, they are territory, and territory in a multi-cat house gets negotiated whether or not you are involved. Get the numbers and the placement right and the negotiations are short. Get them wrong and you get the behaviour people describe to me on the phone: one cat who sleeps in the bathroom, one who guards the hallway, and four beds sitting empty in the loungeroom. Here is the maths as I learned it.

Cats never divide beds evenly

The advice to buy one bed per cat comes from a good place and does not describe what happens. Cats do not claim evenly; they claim by preference. In a house of four cats, my experience says two beds will be contested daily, one will be ignored entirely, and one will be the favourite that everybody wants at 3pm when the sun lands on it. So the useful question is not how many cats you have, it is how many beds can be occupied simultaneously without anyone having to share or wait. That number is usually one more than the number of cats, and the extra bed should be in a different room.

Height, exits and why corner beds get claimed

Position decides occupancy more than padding does. Cats choose sleeping spots on three criteria: a wall or barrier at their back, a clear line of sight to the room's entrances, and elevation if they can get it. A bed wedged into a corner with a view of the doorway will be used every day, and the identical bed in the middle of an open floor will be ignored for months. In the cattery, the beds against the divider walls were claimed first, every single time, and cats arriving later went for elevation rather than a better mattress. So before you spend money on a better bed, spend five minutes on a better position.

  • A wall or a barrier at their back.
  • A clear line of sight to the room's entrances.
  • Elevation, if they can get it.
  • A corner with a view of the doorway, which beats the same bed in the middle of an open floor.

Scent and the new-bed problem

A new bed smells of the shop, and a multi-cat house notices. Two practical fixes, both learned the hard way: put the new bed where the household's communal scent already lives for a few days before moving it into position, and give it a used blanket or a worn t-shirt so it stops being an alien object. In the cattery, the trick was simply time and a shared sleeping area: after three days, a new bed was indistinguishable from the rest. At home, if a new bed is being avoided specifically by one cat, it is usually that cat's scent that is missing from it, not the bed's fault as such.

Sprays, covers and the quarantine habit

One habit from the cattery is worth carrying into a home: wash the cover of any bed a cat has been unwell on, separately and before it goes back into circulation. That is not about illness prevention, it is about scent and hygiene: a bed that carries a strange or stressed smell changes how the household divides itself, and nervous cats read that instantly. Keep a spare cover per bed so a wash never means a bed out of service, and keep a cheap mat for the cat who is currently negotiating, so somebody always has somewhere to land.

How my own four cats settled it, in the end

It took a fortnight and one purchase. The purchase was a third bed, moved into the second bedroom, which gave the two younger cats an unclaimed room to retreat to. After that, the arguments ended: the senior cat took the sunny loungeroom bed permanently, the pair split the bedroom and the hallway, and the fourth cat, who had been orbiting everyone, claimed the windowsill beside the third bed. Four cats, five sleeping spots, and no shared bed at all. Total cost was one extra bed rather than the four I had been told to buy, and the difference was placement plus one escape hatch of a second room.

What I would tell anyone with more than two cats

Buy one more bed than you think you need, put it in a second room, and place every bed against a wall with a clear sightline to the doorway. Prefer flat mattresses for cats who have to negotiate access and donut beds for cats who are confident, because a nervous cat in a deep rim feels trapped. Keep a spare cover and a cheap backup mat, and accept that one bed in four will end up unused, which is not a failure. That is the entire system, and it held up through a boarding season of thirty cats and a home of four.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do cats share beds with each other?

Sometimes, and much less often than people hope. Sharing usually happens between cats who were raised together or who have lived together for years, and it is usually a warm-weather-adjacent or sun-related event rather than a nightly arrangement. Plan your bed count as if everyone needs their own spot, then be pleasantly surprised when two of them pile up together.

My cat sleeps on the bathroom floor instead of the bed I bought. Why?

Tiles are cool, the room is quiet and small, and the door gives a clear sightline. That is a cat making a perfectly reasonable choice, and it usually means the bed you bought is in a busy or exposed position. Move it closer to the bathroom or buy a second bed for the bathroom corner, and you will often find the cat chooses it once the position makes sense.

How do I place beds in a small flat with two cats?

Two beds, two different rooms if your flat has them, each against a wall with a view of the doorway. In a one-bedroom flat, use the height variation instead: one bed on the floor in the loungeroom and one on a shelf or a chest of drawers. Elevation counts as a separate spot to a cat, so vertical separation replaces the second room.

Should every cat have their own food, water and bed?

Yes for feeding and watering, with the bowls separated by some distance, and effectively yes for beds with the extra-one rule. In a multi-cat house, shared resources are the most common source of quiet stress, and the stress shows up as avoidance rather than fighting. Duplicating the resource costs money; not duplicating it costs behaviour.

What about beds that smell like another cat?

Wash the cover and let it dry naturally, then re-introduce it with a familiar scent on it, like a blanket both cats use. In the cattery, a washed bed went straight back out with no problem because every cat there was new anyway; at home, the only time scent matters is when a returning cat comes back from the vet smelling differently, and then the shared bedding is what settles them fastest.
