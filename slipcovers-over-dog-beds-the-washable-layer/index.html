Slipcovers Over Dog Beds: The Washable Layer a Stylist Adds

By Sofia MarchettiSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: a fitted, washable slipcover lets you clean the layer that actually gets dirty without dragging the whole bed, foam and all, to the machine. It protects the bed underneath, dries in hours instead of days, and swaps the colour of the room's dog zone in the time it takes to unzip a cover.

The bed I see most often in Brisbane living rooms is a good one gone tired: decent foam, a shape that still supports, and a cover that has been through the wash so many times the zip has given up. The owner washed the entire bed because that was the only option, and the whole thing spent two days drying on a clothes rack. A slipcover solves this before it starts. I fit one over nearly every client dog bed I meet, not to protect an investment so much as to keep the washing chore small, the bed smelling fresh, and the corner of the room looking considered rather than resigned.

Why the whole bed rarely needs washing

Think about what actually gets dirty. Paws, drool, muddy coats and the occasional accident all land on the top surface. The foam core underneath stays clean unless liquid soaks through, and foam is exactly what you do not want to wash: it holds water for days, can tear in the drum and rarely returns to its original density once soaked and spun. A slipcover intercepts everything at the surface. You wash a light cotton layer when it needs it, and the foam beneath stays dry, springy and smelling of nothing at all. It also extends the bed's life, because the fabric that wears out first, the panel under your dog's elbows, becomes a cheap replacement rather than a reason to replace the whole bed.

Fit is the whole game

A loose cover is worse than no cover. Excess fabric pools at the edges, a dog paws it into a nest, and within a week the bed looks rumpled no matter what you do. Measure the bed's length, width and height before buying, then choose a cover that matches within a few centimetres either way. Zip closures beat elastic edges on square and rectangular beds because they hold the corners sharp; elastic suits round beds and owners who want a five-second strip-off. Look for a slightly grippy underside or corner ties, since smooth covers slide across timber floors every time a dog flops down. If the fit sits between two sizes, take the smaller one. Stretch fabric forgives; excess does not.

Fabrics that take a beating

Cotton canvas and cotton duck are my defaults. They are tightly woven enough to resist claws and loose hair, they wash at warm temperatures without shrinking out of shape, and they soften into a lived-in look rather than pilling into fuzz. Denim works brilliantly in casual rooms. Drill and cotton-poly blends are the workhorses for puppies and chronic diggers. I avoid plush and sherpa covers in main living areas; they photograph warmly, collect every pale hair off a dark floor, and never quite look clean. Dark colours hide stains but show lint, while mid-tones and heathered weaves hide both, which is why greys and warm neutrals dominate my client installs. Whatever you choose, pre-wash it once before the first fitting so any shrinkage happens before you judge the fit.

  • Measure length, width and height first, and buy the closer fit rather than the looser one.
  • Zip closures hold corners square; elastic edges suit round beds and quick strip-offs.
  • Cotton canvas and denim survive claws, hair and warm washes without losing shape.
  • Pre-wash any new cover once so shrinkage happens before the first proper fitting.
  • Keep two covers per bed so one is always clean while the other dries.

Using covers to settle the room

This is where the stylist's best trick hides: a slipcover is the cheapest room refresh available to a dog owner. When a client's lounge gets new cushions, the dog bed cover changes with them, and suddenly the pet zone reads as part of the scheme rather than an apology beside the sofa. I keep a small stack of covers in each regular client's palette, a warm oatmeal, a slate, a stripe, and swap them seasonally. Summer gets pale covers in lighter weaves; winter can go darker and heavier. The bed itself never moves, the dog keeps their familiar scent and spot, and the room changes around them. In open-plan homes this matters even more, because the bed is visible from the kitchen and deserves the same treatment as any other soft furnishing. When a client picks a new bed from our pet beds range, I order the slipcover before the bed even arrives.

Two covers in rotation

One cover per bed means the bed is bare on wash day, and a bare bed invites a dog onto the sofa. Two covers in rotation solves it: one on the bed, one folded in the cupboard, swapped whenever the first gets gnarly. Wash on the warm cycle the care label allows, skip the fabric softener because it coats fibres and reduces their grip on hair, and line-dry in the sun where possible. Sunlight freshens cotton canvas better than any additive, and a cover dried flat keeps its shape. Make sure the cover dries fully before it goes back on, because a damp cover trapped over foam is how musty smells start. Zip it on, smooth the corners, and the swap takes two minutes.

What I tell clients to skip

Skip the decorative covers with buttons, ties and ruffles. Anything that sticks out from the surface is a chew target and a washing snag, and I have unwrapped enough ties from determined jaws to stop recommending them entirely. Skip one-size-fits-all covers, which fit nothing properly. Skip thin zips at the cheap end of the market; the teeth separate after a few wash cycles and the cover is finished. And skip the idea that the cover must match the bed it came from. A dull brown orthopaedic bed under a crisp striped canvas cover looks like a different piece of furniture, and the dog is no wiser. The bed is for the dog. The cover is for the dog, the washing machine and the room, in that order.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I put a slipcover over a bed that already has a cover?

Yes, and it is often the smartest move for a bed with a worn or ugly original cover. Fit the slipcover over the top as long as the combined height does not make the edges unstable or too tall to climb. Check the inner cover occasionally after accidents, since the outer layer can hide a spill that is soaking toward the foam.

How often should a dog bed slipcover be washed?

Fortnightly suits most indoor dogs, and weekly for dogs who swim, shed heavily or head straight for the mud. Wash sooner after any accident or a noticeable doggy smell rather than sticking to the schedule. Between washes, a quick vacuum or a shake outside keeps hair and grit from working into the weave.

Will a slipcover stop my dog scratching the bed?

It will slow wear on the bed underneath, but no fabric truly stops a determined scratcher. Tightly woven canvas resists better than loose weaves, and keeping claws trimmed helps more than any cover. If your dog tears at bedding every night, that habit is worth mentioning to your vet before you spend money on tougher covers.

What size slipcover suits a raised cot or an unusual shape?

Raised cots and oval beds rarely take standard covers well. For cots, look for replacement fabric slings made for the frame rather than forcing a rectangular cover over the bars. For odd shapes, a large flat throw tucked firmly under the mattress is the honest answer, and it often looks better than a strained fitted cover.

Are zip covers or elastic covers better?

Zips for square and rectangular beds, because they hold corners square and survive frequent washing. Elastic for round beds, for dogs who cannot wait while you unzip, and for anyone who hates fiddling with fastenings. Check zip quality before buying; a chunky tooth zip outlasts a fine one many times over on a bed that gets washed weekly.
