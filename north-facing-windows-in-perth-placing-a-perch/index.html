North-Facing Windows in Perth: Placing a Perch for Heat and Light

By Maddie SorensenSep 2, 2026Cat Window Perches8 min read

Short answer: in Perth a north-facing window is the best winter perch in the house and a poor summer one, because the sun swings west after lunch and the glass holds the heat. Put the perch on north glass for morning light, set it back from the pane, and treat west windows as winter-only seats.

Most of my fitting work happens in other people's lounges, and the same conversation comes up every January. A cat has claimed a north window, the owner has bought a perch, and by mid-afternoon the pad is too warm to touch and the cat is flat on the tiled floor instead. Perth hands us a lot of north and west glass and not much of a buffer: clear sky, a dry 38C afternoon, one pane doing exactly what a single pane does. I carry a compass and a thermometer in the van now, and I check the window before I check the perch.

How the sun crosses a Perth window

Perth sits just past 32 degrees south, so the sun tracks across the northern half of the sky all year. A true north window takes sun for most of the day in winter, when the arc is low and the light reaches deep into the room and lands well past the sill. In summer the arc is much higher, so that same window gets a short, steep hit around the middle of the day and then loses it as the sun swings away west. A west window is the mirror image: quiet all morning, then a long low strike from about three in the afternoon until sunset. Two windows in the same house, a few metres apart, behave nothing alike in February. That is the whole placement problem in one sentence, and it is why a perch that works beautifully in one room can sit unused in the next.

Why west glass is the harder problem

Low afternoon sun is not only brighter, it is harder to shade. A blind dropped halfway down handles high sun well; it does almost nothing for light arriving at eye level from the far side of the room. The second difference is glass temperature rather than air temperature. On a still 36C afternoon the pane itself runs warmer than the air around it, and a pad pressed against it has nowhere to shed that heat. I test with the back of my hand, held flat on the pad for a slow count of five. If I want to pull away, the cat will too, and no amount of padding fixes a surface that is radiating at the cat. That test costs nothing and it tells you more than any label on the box.

Reading the room before you buy

Before I look at mounts I walk the room and find where the cat already sits. Cats choose a spot for the view and the exit route, not for our idea of a tidy corner, and a perch at the wrong height gets used once and then abandoned. The useful numbers are height and setback. Most cats want the pad somewhere between a metre and a metre and a half off the floor, high enough to watch the room and low enough to drop down without a scramble. Setback matters just as much. A pad 10cm from the glass cooks; the same pad 25cm back keeps the view and leaves a pocket of moving air behind the cat. Watch the cat in that room for ten minutes before you drill anything.

The shadow test that saves a purchase

Here is what I do in an unfamiliar house. Either I visit at three separate times, or I ask the owner to photograph the same window at 9am, 1pm and 4pm on a clear day. I am looking for where the light edge falls on the floor. If the shadow line crosses the sill by early afternoon, that sill is a morning spot and nothing more, whatever the listing says about all-day sun. If the recess stays shaded while the room beyond it is bright, the window is doing the job properly: light without a bake. Most owners already have these photos on their phones, taken for entirely unrelated reasons, which makes the whole exercise quicker than it sounds.

  • North glass is a winter seat; treat it as a morning spot from November to March.
  • West glass takes the hardest summer sun from about three in the afternoon.
  • Hold the back of your hand on the pad for five seconds before you trust it.
  • Keep the pad 20 to 25cm back from the glass so air moves behind the cat.
  • Photograph the window at 9am, 1pm and 4pm before you stick or drill anything.

Matching the mount to the glass

Suction cups are the default for renters and they are fine on clean, flat, undamaged glass. They are less fine on windows with aftermarket tint or a reflective film, because the cup seals against the film rather than the glass, and a warm afternoon is when that shows up. Sill perches suit the deep recesses in older Perth homes, where a 15cm ledge needs no fixing at all. Wall brackets are the most stable of the three but they put holes in the wall, which is a conversation with a landlord rather than a hardware problem. Whatever you choose, the perch has to clear the flyscreen or sit outside it, never press into it. The cat window perches on the site are sorted by mount type, which makes the comparison quicker than reading boxes in an aisle.

Shading the top, not the middle

Shade the top of the glass rather than the middle. A roller blind dropped 20cm from the top of a north window cuts the high summer sun and leaves the view at cat height completely clear, which is the trick most people miss. On a west window, shade outside the glass is worth more than anything fitted inside, because the heat is stopped before it crosses the pane, but renters rarely get a say in that. Inside, a sheer curtain across the top third does some of the work. I also move the perch rather than the cat: a second pad on an east window for January and February, then back to the north glass in May. Cats accept a moved perch faster than owners expect.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a cat get too hot sitting in a north-facing window?

It can, mainly in the middle of a summer day when the sun is high and the glass is warm. Cats are good at moving themselves, but a perch that traps heat gives them nowhere to go. If the pad is uncomfortable to your hand after five seconds, shift the perch back from the glass or offer a cooler spot nearby.

Is morning sun better than afternoon sun for a perch?

In Perth, in summer, yes. Morning light arrives at a steeper angle and the air is still cool, so a cat can sit in it comfortably for an hour or more. Afternoon sun comes in low from the west, lands on the back of the room and holds heat into the evening. In winter the afternoon sun is genuinely welcome and the same window becomes the best seat in the house.

Can I put a suction perch on a window with tinting film?

Ask before you stick anything to it. Suction cups need a clean, flat, stable surface, and film is neither as hard nor as firmly bonded as glass once it warms up. Many films also come with care instructions that rule out suction mounts. If the window is filmed, a sill perch or a freestanding frame avoids the question entirely.

My window has a deep sill, so do I need a perch at all?

Often you do not. A sill 15cm deep or more gives a cat a flat, stable platform with the full view and no fixing at all, which suits renters and older cats alike. Add a nonslip mat and a folded towel and you have most of what a bought perch offers. Sills under 10cm are the ones that need help.

Will adding a perch make the room hotter in summer?

Not in any way you would notice. The perch is a small object and the heat crosses the glass whether a cat sits there or not. What changes is where the cat spends the afternoon, and a cat pressed against a hot pane does warm that corner slightly. Shading the top of the window does far more for the room than removing the perch would.
