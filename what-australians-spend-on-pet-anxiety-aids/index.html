What Australians Spend on Pet Anxiety Aids Each Year: An Honest Estimate

By Marcus HaleSep 1, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

There's no single official tally of what Australians spend on pet anxiety aids, so any figure has to be an estimate built from retail ranges and ownership numbers. Industry estimates put the category somewhere in the tens of millions of dollars a year, spread across collars, diffusers, sprays and supplements. The honest move is to give a range and show the working rather than fake a precise total. Here's a grounded breakdown of where that money most likely goes.

Why there's no clean number

Pet anxiety aids sit across pharmacy, pet specialty, supermarket and online channels, and none of them report a neat shared line item. That means any national spend figure is a modelled estimate, not a counted one. I'd rather be upfront about that than quote a number that sounds authoritative and isn't, because a false decimal misleads more than a clear maybe.

So when I say tens of millions, I mean a broad order of magnitude derived from price points and the share of households with a noise- or separation-sensitive pet, not a surveyed total. It could be meaningfully higher or lower, and the shape of the argument matters more than the headline, because the shape is what an owner can actually act on.

Online marketplaces make it harder still. A decent volume of calming collars is now bought through platforms that report no category splits at all, and some of it comes from overseas sellers where the unit price barely clears ten dollars. That spend is real but largely invisible to anyone trying to sum the local market from retail data alone.

Building a range from the parts

Start with the share of households that own a dog or cat, then take a rough slice of those with an anxious pet, then apply a typical annual spend per household. At common Australian retail prices, a household running a collar and a diffuser might spend forty to one hundred dollars a year, while a one-off spray buyer spends far less.

  • Households with pets: a large share of Australian homes.
  • Share with anxiety concerns: a meaningful minority.
  • Typical annual spend: tens to low hundreds of dollars.
  • Multiplied across millions of households: tens of millions.
  • Online and discount channels: undercounted, not zero.

The format split, by cost

Collars and diffusers take the larger share of the money because they're bought repeatedly through a season, while sprays are cheap and occasional so they add less in total even if they sell in high unit counts. Supplements are the wild card: pricier per item and often used longer, so they punch above their unit count in the spend picture, though the evidence base there is its own conversation.

I'd estimate collars and diffusers together account for the majority of the category by value, with sprays a smaller slice and supplements a noteworthy but harder-to-pin chunk. None of those is a counted figure, just a reasoned split from where the repeat purchasing concentrates, and I'd flag it as a read rather than a receipt.

Why the range is honest, not evasive

Giving a band instead of a point isn't hedging to be safe, it reflects that the inputs are themselves ranges. Pet ownership estimates shift between surveys, anxiety prevalence is self-reported, and retail prices swing from twelve dollars to ninety. Multiply ranges and you get a range, so a single tidy number would actually be the less honest answer, and I'd push back on anyone demanding one.

The useful takeaway for an owner is personal, not national: work out what your own dog needs for the season, buy the format that fits, and you'll likely land in that forty-to-one-hundred-dollar band rather than overspending on a cupboard of gadgets. The national figure is interesting; your own bill is the one you control, and the data says most households sit modestly within it.

What would sharpen the estimate

A proper tally would need the major retailers and the pharmacy channel to agree on a shared category code, plus the online platforms to report pet splits, none of which happens today. Until then, industry estimates and modelled ranges are the best we have, and they're good enough to show the category is real and sizeable without pretending to a precision nobody can defend. Honest ranges beat fake certainty every time.

For owners worried they're overspending, the personal maths is reassuring: most households using one or two formats land well inside the hundred-dollar band, and the national tens-of-millions figure is simply that modest band multiplied across millions of homes. The money isn't going to a few big buyers, it's spread thin across a lot of ordinary pets, which is exactly why a per-household view is more useful than a headline total. Spend on what your dog needs for the season and you're squarely in the normal range.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do Australians spend on pet anxiety aids?

There's no official tally, so the honest answer is a modelled estimate of tens of millions of dollars a year, built from retail price ranges and the share of households with an anxious pet. It could be higher or lower, and I'd treat it as an order of magnitude rather than a counted sum. The category is clearly sizeable, but anyone quoting a precise national figure is overstating what the data can support.

What does one household typically spend?

For a pet using a collar and a diffuser through a season, roughly forty to one hundred dollars a year is a fair band at normal Australian retail prices, while a one-off spray buyer spends far less. Supplements can push a household higher if used long term. Most owners land in that modest range, and buying the format that fits the problem avoids the cupboard of half-used gadgets that inflates the bill.

Which format costs owners the most?

Collars and diffusers take the largest share by value because they're replaced repeatedly through storm season, even though sprays sell in high unit counts at low price. Supplements are pricier per item and often used longer, so they add a noteworthy chunk despite lower volume. The repeat purchasing, not the sticker price, is what drives where the money actually goes across the year.

Why can't we just count the spending?

Because the products are sold across pet specialty, pharmacy, supermarket and online channels that don't share a category code, and the big marketplaces report no pet splits at all. So any national figure is modelled from parts, not counted from a receipt. A real tally would need retailers and platforms to agree on reporting, which doesn't happen today, so honest ranges are the best available answer.

Is the estimate reliable enough to trust?

As a direction, yes; as a precise number, no, and that's the honest limit. The inputs, pet ownership, anxiety prevalence and prices, are themselves ranges, so multiplying them gives a range, and a single tidy total would be the less truthful output. Use it to understand the category is real and sizeable, and to benchmark your own spend, not to quote a decimal as fact.
