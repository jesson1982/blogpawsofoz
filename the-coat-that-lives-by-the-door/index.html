The Coat That Lives by the Door: What Actually Gets Worn

By Sophie BennettSep 3, 2026Pet Apparel8 min read

Short answer: About three of the eleven dog garments we own do all the work, and they are the plain ones with one fastening, a harness slot and no leg straps. Everything else failed on fit or fuss, not warmth. Buy fewer coats, measure the chest properly, and test the fastening with one hand before you commit.

We keep a hook by the front door of our Perth rental, and everything hanging on it has already proved itself. Last winter I emptied the cupboard under the laundry sink to see what the hook was competing with: eleven pieces of dog clothing for one Cavoodle, a few worn twice, one still with the tag on. Miso slept in the empty box while I laid the lot out on the floor and wrote down which ones Wally had stood still for. The list was shorter than I expected, and it has changed how I buy.

What earned its place on the hook

Two things hang there. A fleece-lined harness coat for cold mornings, and a thin rain shell that folds into its own pocket. Both go on in under five seconds, both clear the tail, and neither asks the dog to thread a leg through anything. The rain shell earns its spot because it lives by the door and weighs almost nothing, so it ends up in the car or the bottom of a bag without a second thought. The fleece comes out for the 6am walk when it is 8 degrees and the pavement is still wet. Everything else lost to those two, and none of it lost on quality. It lost on the half second at the door when the dog was already spinning, and a coat that needs two hands and a flat surface is a coat that gets left behind.

The fastening decides everything

Wally will stand for a coat, but only for about four seconds. That number rules out most of what we own. Velcro is fast but loud, and the rip of it makes him turn around to check what is happening behind him. Buckles under the belly are fine if there is one clip and not three. Zips along the back are the worst of the lot, because they need the dog to hold still while you line up the pull, and then the fabric bunches at the shoulder. The two coats on the hook both close with a single clip at the chest and a panel that folds over the back. I can do either of them with one hand while holding a lead in the other, which on a wet morning is the only test that matters. What I want is a coat that goes on in the time it takes to clip a lead, and almost nothing in the cupboard does.

Why the good coats stayed in the cupboard

The most expensive coat we bought is a padded thing with a chest panel, a collar tab, a back zip and two elastic leg loops. On the hanger it looks like proper gear. On the dog it takes ninety seconds and one of us kneeling on the tiles. The leg loops pull his stride short and he walks like he is wearing someone else's shoes. A knit jumper went the same way: the fit was right, the colour was good, and he refused to move more than a metre in it. We kept both for a year in case he changed his mind. He did not. Cost had nothing to do with any of it, and the cupboard was full of coats that suited the idea of a dog more than the dog standing in front of us.

  • A coat you can fasten with one hand is the only coat that gets used.
  • Velcro is quick, but the noise alone rules it out for a nervous dog.
  • Leg straps change how a dog walks, so test them on the footpath.
  • If the coat hides the harness ring, you will stop using both.
  • Keep two coats by the door, not eleven in a cupboard, and rotate them.

The harness slot rule

Every coat we keep has an opening over the shoulders that lets the harness D-ring sit clear, and I will not buy one without it. Without that slot the choice is coat under harness, which bunches and rubs, or harness over coat, which slides sideways. Both end with me fussing at the dog on the footpath while traffic goes past. The slot needs to sit far enough forward that the lead clips on without lifting the back of the coat. If the opening is a slit rather than a bound hole, check it after a month, because a slit stretches and the ring ends up under fabric. I apply the same rule to everything we look at in dog apparel: no slot, no sale.

What we do before we buy now

Three things, all of them in the shop or in the hallway before the tags come off. I measure the chest behind the front legs and the length from the base of the neck to the tail, and I take both numbers with me. I put the coat over the harness the dog is already wearing, not over a bare back, because that is how it will be worn. Then I time the fastening with one hand while the other hand holds a lead. If it takes more than five seconds, it goes back on the rack. It sounds fussy. It has saved us from four more cupboard coats, and cheap or expensive does not come into the decision until those three checks pass.

What the hook tells you by March

By the end of winter the hook is honest in a way a review is not. If a coat is on it, the coat works. If a coat is still folded in the cupboard in August, it is not going to be worn in September either, and it can go to the op shop or to a friend with a smaller dog. The two on our hook have been rained on, dragged through sand at the dog beach and washed more times than I can count, and they still close with one hand. Perth winters are short and mostly wet rather than freezing, so a shell does more work here than a padded coat ever will. Eleven garments, two hooks, and a much shorter list of things I would buy again.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many dog coats does one dog actually need?

For a Perth dog, two is plenty: a thin rain shell for wet walks and one warmer layer for the few cold mornings. A third coat only makes sense if you travel somewhere colder or the dog is clipped very short. Past that you are buying for the cupboard rather than for the dog.

What should I do with the coats my dog refuses to wear?

Try once more with the fastening done up before the coat goes near the dog, so there is nothing to fumble with at the last second. If the refusal holds, stop. Some dogs never accept leg straps or back zips, and pushing it makes the next attempt harder. Pass the coat on.

Does a dog coat need to cover the belly to be warm?

Not always. A coat covering the back and sides traps most of the warmth, and a belly panel helps mainly on cold, windy mornings. A deep belly panel on a male dog can catch urine and stay damp, which is worse than having no panel at all. Check underneath after a walk and see for yourself.

Should I buy a size up so the dog grows into it?

Not for a coat. A loose coat shifts sideways, the back hem drops over the tail and the front rides up into the throat, so the dog walks badly and you conclude the dog hates coats. Buy for the chest measurement you have today. With a puppy, expect to buy again in a season and keep the first one cheap.

Is a jumper enough for a Perth winter?

On most winter days, for most Perth dogs, yes. Our coldest mornings sit around 5 to 8 degrees, which a knit or fleece handles well while the dog is dry. The gap is rain, because a jumper soaks through and then holds the cold against the skin. A thin shell over the top does more than a thicker jumper.
