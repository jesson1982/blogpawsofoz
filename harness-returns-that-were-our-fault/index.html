Harness Returns That Were Our Fault: Three Fixes We Made

By Nina CarusoSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses8 min read

Short answer: Most harness returns are not buyer error. Ours kept repeating because our sizing chart, our size-up habit and our step-in instructions each sent people in the wrong direction. We changed all three, and those particular returns have mostly stopped coming back.

Last Tuesday I processed four harness returns before morning tea. Three of them said the same thing on the form: wrong size. When I rang the buyers back, the stories did not sound like buyer error at all. One woman had measured exactly where our chart photo told her to. One man had done what our chat agent suggested and sized up. The third had bought a step-in harness that our instructions described in two sentences. That afternoon I pulled the month's harness returns and sorted them by what we had said versus what each buyer had done. Three patterns repeated, and every one sat on our side of the counter.

A returns audit from our side

The audit was not complicated. I took one month of harness returns, listed the reason the buyer gave, then rang or emailed enough of them to find out what had actually happened. I wanted the conversation before the return, because that is where our advice lives. Two things became obvious fast. First, the same three confusions appeared whether the harness was a budget webbing model or a padded ninety-dollar one. Second, not one of the buyers had ignored our guidance. They had followed it. A returns shelf tells you what people send back, not what we told them first. From then on I stopped recording return reasons as buyer mistakes and started recording them as instructions we had not written properly.

The girth chart pointed at the neck

The first pattern was girth. Our listing said to measure the widest part of the chest, but the photo we used showed the tape sitting well forward, close to where a collar sits. Buyers measured there, got a smaller number, ordered the size the chart spat out, and the harness arrived too tight behind the front legs. The buyers had done exactly what the page said. The fix cost us an afternoon. We rewrote the instruction to name the landmark in plain words, the widest point of the chest, two fingers behind the front legs. We moved the photo so the tape sat in the right place, and we added a second shot from the side. Girth returns dropped off within the month, and the few that still come in arrive with the measurements attached, which makes the exchange quick.

Our size-up habit caused escapes

The second pattern was our own default advice. For years, when a buyer sat between two sizes, we told them to size up. What it actually produced was harnesses with slack in the wrong places. A harness that is too big does not stay put. It rotates, the chest strap drops, and a determined dog learns in one walk that backing up frees them. We heard the same story from kelpies and one very committed beagle: the harness came home in the car, still done up. So we retired the size-up line. Now we ask for both the neck and the girth measurement, we trust the chart for the brand in question, and if a dog genuinely sits between sizes, the girth decides. Escape returns did not vanish, but the ones left are usually five-minute adjustment calls.

Step-in instructions skipped one step

The third pattern was the step-in harness. Ours fold flat and the two straps need shortening before the first fit, because they arrive at their longest setting. Our instructions never said so. Buyers clipped the harness on, saw straps hanging loose, concluded the size was wrong, and sent it back. The fix was one honest sentence on the listing: shorten both straps a few centimetres at a time before you judge the fit. We also added a line to the despatch email, because most people never reread a listing. That single sentence cut step-in returns more than anything else we changed. It was not a sizing problem at all. It was an instruction problem wearing a sizing problem's clothes, and it had sat on the page for two years.

  • Measure the girth two fingers behind the front legs, never up near the collar.
  • Between sizes, let the girth measurement decide instead of automatically sizing up.
  • Shorten step-in straps a few centimetres at a time before judging the fit.
  • Do the two-finger check on every strap before the first walk.
  • When a return repeats, audit the page the buyer read first.

What changed across the product pages

Once the three fixes were live, we went back through every harness listing and asked the same question: could someone following only this page get the fit wrong? We replaced jargon like sternum and rear girth with plain landmarks. We made sure each measurement had exactly one photo, taken from the angle a person actually holds a tape. We wrote a short fit-check paragraph for the end of every listing, the same three checks every time. And we changed the despatch email to say the harness should feel snug, not loose. When people browse our dog harnesses now, the sizing help reads like something a person on a phone can follow, because that is where most of them read it.

How the returns desk runs now

The biggest change is not on any product page. It is at the desk. Every harness return now gets logged against the page the buyer used, not just the reason they ticked. If the same confusion shows up twice, the page gets fixed that week, not someday. I also ring a handful of buyers each month to hear where our words failed them. That habit has found more problems than any audit I could run alone, and it is slower and messier, but it works. Returns still happen, because dogs are wiggly and tape measures are optimistic. But the share that come back because we pointed the wrong way keeps shrinking, and that is the only number I actually watch.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know a harness is genuinely too small?

Slide two flat fingers under each strap. If you cannot, or the harness leaves deep marks behind the front legs after a short walk, the size or the adjustment is wrong. Loosen the straps first, because a new harness often arrives mid-range. If the fit is still tight at every strap's widest setting, exchange rather than persist. Ask your vet about any rubbing that looks sore or lasts more than a day.

Should I size up when my dog is between sizes?

Not automatically, and that advice used to come from us. A larger harness can rotate and slip, which is how dogs learn to back out of them. Check the chart for the exact brand, because sizes are not standard across makers. When a dog truly sits between two sizes, the girth measurement decides, and the straps then take up the rest. If the numbers still confuse you, send both measurements to support before you order.

Can I return a harness my dog has already worn?

Most Australian stores, ours included, accept a harness worn once or twice for a sizing exchange, as long as it is clean, the tags are on and you are inside the returns window. What we cannot take back is a harness that has been worn for weeks or washed. Keep the packaging until you are sure of the fit, and check the store's policy before ordering, because policies differ between shops.

Why does the same size fit differently in two brands?

Because there is no industry standard for pet harness sizing. A medium in one brand can measure larger than a large in another, and cut differently through the chest too. This is exactly why we stopped advising people to size up across brands. Ignore the letter on the label, measure your dog's neck and girth, and compare both numbers against the chart for that specific harness. It feels slower but beats a return.

What measurements help you recommend the right harness?

Three numbers, taken while your dog is standing. First, the neck where a collar would sit. Second, the girth at the widest point of the chest, two fingers behind the front legs. Third, your dog's weight, which we use as a cross-check against the chart. Use a soft tape, keep it snug but not tight, and take each measurement twice. Wriggly dogs are easier with a second person holding a treat at nose height.
