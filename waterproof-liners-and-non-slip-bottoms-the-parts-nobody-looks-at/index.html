Waterproof Liners and Non-Slip Bottoms: The Parts Nobody Looks At

By Tessa NgataApr 10, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

Short answer: what keeps a dog bed in service for years is the liner, the non-slip base and the zip, not the colour or soft top in the photo. The one I bought Pip when she retired looked lovely, then eighteen months on the foam smelled like a wet dog, because no liner protected it. You'll want a waterproof layer between cover and fill, a grippy underside, and a zip that survives the wash. Judge it by the parts nobody photographs, and it outlasts three prettier ones.

People buy dog beds on sight. They see a colour they like, a shape that suits the corner, a fabric that feels soft, and that is the decision made. I have done it myself, and I work with animals for a living. What decides whether that bed is still in service in three years is a set of features you cannot see in a photograph: the liner between the cover and the fill, the treatment on the underside, and the zip.

The bed that taught me this was one I bought for Pip when she retired. It looked lovely. Eighteen months later the cover came out of the wash fine and the foam underneath smelled like a wet dog that had given up. There was no liner, so every bit of drool and damp had gone straight into a core I could neither wash nor replace. The cover was immaculate and the bed was finished.

Waterproof versus water-resistant, honestly

These two words get used interchangeably in listings and they mean quite different things. Waterproof means a membrane or coating in the construction genuinely stops liquid passing through — tip a cup of water on it and the fill underneath stays dry. Water-resistant means the fabric sheds light moisture briefly, a damp dog shaking off, and then gives up and soaks through.

Both have a place. A waterproof liner is worth paying for if you have a puppy, an older dog who has started having accidents, or a dog who drools heavily. A water-resistant cover usually suffices for a dry, house-trained adult. The honest downside of a full membrane is breathability: some are noisy under a dog who shuffles about, and some trap more warmth than you want in a Queensland summer. If a bed sounds like a chip packet when your dog lies down, they may well avoid it.

Where the liner sits matters too. The useful arrangement is cover, then liner, then fill. Without one, every accident reaches the core, and a foam core soaked through once will smell for the rest of its life.

Why the base is not a minor detail

Australian homes have a lot of hard flooring. Tiles, polished concrete and floating floors are everywhere, and they are slippery. A bed with a smooth polyester base will slide — not a little, either. A Labrador stepping onto it sends it halfway across the room.

This matters more than it sounds. A young dog will put up with it, or treat the sliding as a game. An older, larger or already cautious dog will not. They step onto a bed that moves under them once, decide it is unsafe, and go back to the floor. I have seen it dozens of times in rehabilitation work: an excellent bed that simply will not stay still.

Look for a dotted or textured non-slip treatment rather than plain woven fabric. It does not need to be rubberised sheeting, which tends to crack and peel after repeated hot washes. A decent textured grip holds well on tiles and concrete and stops grit being dragged across your floor every time the bed shifts. Test it the day it arrives: press down firmly and see whether it moves.

Zips are where beds usually die

In my experience the zip fails long before the fabric does. The teeth are usually fine; it is the pull and the stitching around the tape that go. A bed washed fortnightly goes through that zip twenty-six times a year, and a cheap one does not survive it.

Things worth checking before you buy: a covered zip garage so the pull tucks away from chewing, a metal pull rather than thin plastic, and a zip running along at least two sides. That last one is the one people skip, and it makes an enormous difference when you are wrestling a bulky inner back inside a freshly washed cover.

Close the zip before washing, wash covers inside out on a cool or warm cycle, and hang them to dry. Heat is what kills both the water-repellent finish and the elastic in the seams.

Why separate pieces beat one big cushion

A single-piece bed, cover sewn to fill, has to go in the machine as a whole unit. A king single sized one barely fits, takes forever to dry, and if it comes out damp in the middle you get mildew. In a Hobart winter, or anywhere along the eastern seaboard in the humid months, drying a solid bed through is genuinely difficult.

A cover that comes off plus an inner pad is easier to live with. The cover is small, washes quickly and dries in an afternoon. The inner can be spot cleaned, aired in the sun, and replaced on its own once it compresses. It also lets you buy a second cover and rotate them, which is the most useful thing I have done in my own household. Wash day stops being an emergency when there is a clean one in the cupboard.

What to look for, in order

If I were standing in Petbarn with a client, this is the order I would have them check things. Does the cover come off completely — if not, walk away. Is there a liner, and does the listing say waterproof or water-resistant honestly. Pick it up and look at the base, then test it on a hard floor at home. Open and close the zip a few times and look at the stitching around it. Only then think about the colour.

That last point is not a joke. The pretty bed with a bonded cover and a smooth base will be in landfill while the plain one with a liner and a grippy underside is still going. None of this photographs well, which is exactly why it gets overlooked.

The bed Pip uses now has a liner I can wipe down, a textured base that has not moved on our floorboards in two years, and a zip I have replaced once. It is not the nicest looking bed in the house and it has outlasted three prettier ones. Look at the parts you cannot see — that is where the money is. — Tessa, Hobart, with a greyhound who drools on everything.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a waterproof and a water-resistant dog bed liner?

A waterproof liner has a membrane or coating that genuinely stops liquid reaching the fill, so accidents and drool stay on the surface. Water-resistant fabric sheds light moisture for a short time then soaks through. For puppies, older dogs or heavy droolers, waterproof is worth the extra cost.

Why does my dog bed keep sliding on tiles and polished concrete?

Smooth polyester bases have almost no grip on hard Australian flooring. Look for a dotted or textured non-slip base instead. A bed that slides makes older and larger dogs reluctant to step onto it at all, which is worth fixing before anything else.

Are machine washable dog bed covers really necessary?

In my experience, yes. A removable washable cover dog bed is far easier to keep clean than a one-piece bed, because the cover dries quickly and the inner can be spot cleaned and aired. In humid Australian conditions a solid bed often does not dry through before mildew sets in.

How do I stop a dog bed zip from breaking?

Choose a bed with a metal pull and a zip garage that tucks it out of reach, close the zip before every wash, and wash covers inside out on a cool or warm cycle. Check the stitching around the zip tape before buying, since that is where failure starts.

Can I just replace the inner instead of the whole bed?

Often yes, and it is worth favouring beds designed that way. A replaceable inner pad means a compressed or soaked core does not condemn the entire bed. Buying a second cover at the same time also makes a fortnightly wash far easier to keep up with.
