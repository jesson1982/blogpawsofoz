Keeping Place Training Working Away From Home

By Daniel WhitlockSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: teach place on one mat at home, then move that same mat to a new room, a new building and finally a new town, adding one new thing at a time. Most dogs lose the cue away from home because the mat never travelled.

I was called out to a rental in Footscray last winter. The dog, a two-year-old staffy cross, had a beautiful place cue at home. On her own mat, in her own loungeroom, she would hold for twenty minutes while her owner ate dinner. That owner took her to a friend's place for a long weekend and rang me on the second night. The mat went down on unfamiliar carpet, the cue was given, and the dog stood up within forty seconds and paced the hallway. Nothing about the training had failed. The mat had never been anywhere except one room in one house.

Why place training collapses outside your loungeroom

There is nothing mysterious about it. Dogs learn the picture as much as the word. If every repetition of the place cue happened on one mat, in one corner, with the same afternoon sun and the same kitchen noise, the dog has learned that whole picture. Change the carpet and you have quietly changed the question.

Trainers call this a lack of generalisation, and it is the most common reason a solid home behaviour turns to sand on a holiday. The behaviour is not weak. It is narrow. Treat the mat as the place, rather than the spot on the floor, and you can carry the cue in your boot. That means going back a step at home before you pack the car.

Choose one travel mat and stick to it

Choose a single mat and use it for every place repetition, at home and away. A thin, washable travel mat around 60 cm by 90 cm suits most medium dogs and folds into the boot. Rubber backing matters on tiles and vinyl, because a mat that slides under a stepping dog teaches nothing useful. If you are still choosing the mat itself, the pet bed guides here cover thickness and washability.

The drill is short. Mat down, guide the dog onto it with a treat in your fist, mark, pay on the mat, release after two or three seconds. Five reps, then stop. Next session, shift the mat two metres. When that is easy, use a different room. Keep it under three minutes so the dog is still keen when you finish.

The order to introduce new places

Introduce locations in a fixed order and do not skip ahead when a weekend away is coming. Start in your own loungeroom, then a second room, then the backyard, then the front yard, then the parked car or ute with the engine off. Next comes a quiet park on a long line, then a friend's empty loungeroom, then the same room with one calm adult, then an outdoor cafe table.

A caravan, a motel room or a holiday house sits near the end of that list, not the start. Raise one variable at a time. A new room is enough for one session. New noise is enough for the next. Change the space, the people and the noise at once and you will not know which part the dog could not cope with.

  • One mat does the work, so the cue travels with the object.
  • Raise one variable at a time: place, then noise, then people.
  • Keep the first session in a new place under three minutes.
  • Pay on the mat, not from your hand.
  • Never ask for place in a space where the dog is overwhelmed.

The first ten minutes in a new room

Arrive with the dog on lead and walk the room once, letting them sniff the skirting boards and the doorway. Give that a minute. Skip the excited greeting at the door if the host is there, because it lifts the dog right when you need them low. Then put the mat against a wall, clear of the path between kitchen and couch, and give the cue once.

Mark and pay every few seconds for the first rep, then release after a minute. Do that three times over ten minutes and stop. On a first visit, a settle of ninety seconds is a genuine result. Twenty minutes of stillness in a strange house is a goal for the third or fourth trip.

Caravans, motels and other people's loungerooms

Unfamiliar floors matter more than people expect. A dog that holds place on carpet at home may scramble on vinyl or polished tiles, so check the surface before you cue and put a non-slip rug under the mat if the floor is slick. Noise is the other half of it: a caravan fridge, an air conditioner or a hallway door closing every few minutes will keep a dog lifting their head.

Place the mat where the dog can see you and you can see the dog. In shared accommodation and caravan parks, ask the host or manager what they prefer before you set up. If your dog is stiff or sore after travel, ask your vet about surfaces rather than guessing.

When your dog breaks place on the road

Breaking place away from home is information, not disobedience. If the dog stands up, walk back without talking, guide them onto the mat with a hand target or a treat, cue again and pay for a shorter stay. More reward, less duration. Do that two or three times and finish while it is going well.

If the dog breaks three reps in a row, the room is too hard today. Move to a quieter corner, or go back to the car park and do one easy rep, then stop. Rotate through the place types you have already proofed instead of pushing a new one. That keeps the cue strong and gives you something to build on when you get home.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a dog hold place away from home?

Start with what the dog can hold in that room, not what they manage at home. In a brand new space, sixty to ninety seconds is a real result, and you can release and reset three or four times. Build towards five minutes across a weekend, then ten.

Can I just use a towel instead of a mat when I travel?

You can, though a towel bunches and slides, which makes the spot harder for the dog to read. A thin rubber backed travel mat around 60 cm by 90 cm sits still on carpet, tiles and vinyl, and folds into a bag. Use the same one at home first.

My dog settles at home but paces in a motel room. What do I do first?

Walk the room with the dog on lead for a minute before you unpack anything. Let them check the skirting boards and the bathroom door, then put the mat down and ask for one short settle, and repeat that three times. Pacing is usually a smell and noise problem.

Should I pay with treats every time the dog goes to place?

At home, yes, for a long time, and away from home, always at first. The reward is what makes the mat the good spot rather than the place where the dog waits. Pay on the mat, not from your hand. Once the dog chooses the mat unasked in a given room, thin the food out.

Is it worth taking a bed on a caravan trip?

Take the mat you trained on plus a blanket the dog already sleeps on, rather than buying something new for the trip. Familiar smell does a lot of the work when the room is strange. If your dog is stiff after long days in the car, ask your vet which surface suits them.
