What I Teach in Every Pet First Aid Class About Calming Products

By Rebecca HalloranSep 1, 2026Calming & Anxiety8 min read

Short version: most calming products are harmless, a few are useful, and none of them are the thing that saves your dog's life. I teach pet first aid short courses on the Gold Coast, and at least once a month somebody asks me whether a calming collar or a diffuser would have prevented the emergency that brought them to class. The honest answer is almost always no, because what lands people in my classroom is heat, traffic, snake bite, choking and falls, not ordinary anxiety. But the question deserves a better answer than a shrug, so here is the talk I give before we get to the bandaging.

Why a first aid instructor ends up talking about calming gear

Anxious dogs turn up in emergencies more often than relaxed ones, and panic makes everything worse. A frightened dog bites the person trying to clear its airway. A stressed cat wriggles out of a towel wrap and disappears into traffic. So I care about calming gear for one practical reason: a calmer animal is a safer animal to help, and a calmer owner is safer still. That is also why the advice below leans cautious. A product that sedates your dog slightly is not dangerous on a Tuesday, but on the day of the thunderstorm, the house fire or the trip to the emergency vet, it changes how your animal responds to the world, and you should choose that deliberately rather than by accident.

The three questions I ask before anyone clips anything on

First: has your pet seen a vet about this? New anxiety in an adult animal is a symptom before it is a personality trait. Pain, thyroid trouble, hearing loss and dental disease all show up as nervousness, and a pheromone collar will do nothing for a dog whose teeth are the problem. In class I tell people to spend the vet visit money first and the product money second, and not just because it is good medicine. It changes what you expect from the product, which changes how you judge whether it worked.

Second: what exactly are you trying to change? There is a big difference between a dog who pants through the first ten minutes of a storm and a dog who breaks through a flyscreen. Mild coping problems respond nicely to routine, a safe spot and sometimes a collar. Genuine panic is a veterinary conversation. Somewhere in the middle sits the grey zone, and that is where most of the products get sold and most of the disappointment happens.

Third: what does the label actually say? Check the active ingredients, the species it is made for, and the age and weight limits. Dog products are not cat products. Some essential-oil-based formulas that settle a dog are genuinely rough on a cat's liver, and cats groom whatever lands on their coat, including the collar their housemate wears. In class this gets a laugh, but it is not a joke: multi-pet households need to check every product against every animal in the house.

What a calming collar can and cannot do

The collars most commonly sold in Australia fall into two broad camps: pheromone-based, which mimic the scent signals that prompt settling behaviour, and plant-based, which lean on smells most animals find uninteresting or mildly pleasant. Neither is a sedative. The honest framing I use is that a collar can take a three out of ten down to a two, sometimes, for some animals. What it cannot do is hold a line against genuine panic, and what it should never be asked to do is stand in for training, exercise or a plan.

The failure mode people don't expect is fit. A collar works by the animal smelling it, not by contact with the skin, so the classic mistake of fitting it like a flea collar, snug against the neck, does nothing except rub. Follow the packet: usually two fingers of slack, trim the excess, and replace it on schedule because the scent fades long before the collar looks used up. If your dog chews collars, this is not the product line for you anyway, because a chewed collar is a swallowed collar, and swallowed collars are an obstruction appointment.

Red flags that mean skip the product and call the vet

There is a short list I put on the whiteboard in every class. Sudden anxiety in a pet that has never been anxious. Anxiety with physical signs: panting that has nothing to do with heat, pacing that doesn't stop, trembling that isn't cold, a belly that seems tender. Anxiety with a change in appetite or toileting. Self-harm, which includes chewing paws raw or breaking teeth on bars and doors. Any of those, and the calming aisle is not the answer; the vet is, ideally this week. Pheromones are cheap. The mistake they help you make, which is waiting months on a medical problem, is not.

One more from the emergency side of my teaching: never stack sedatives on top of products without telling your vet. If your animal is already on veterinary medication for storms or travel, adding an over-the-counter calming product to the mix is a conversation, not a purchase. Bring the packet to the appointment. Vets read labels faster than you think and would much rather answer the question than treat the interaction.

Fitting, placement and the day-to-day details

Diffusers go in the room where the animal actually settles, not the hallway where the front door is. They work best plugged in low, because pheromone plumes drift with the air con, and a Gold Coast living room in January has a lot of air con. Sprays go on bedding and bandanas, not directly onto the animal, and they need a minute or two to settle before the pet is near them. Collars get checked at every bath, because some formulations lose their run once wet repeatedly. And every product gets a fair trial of a few weeks with a simple score: note the behaviour that bothers you, one to ten, and re-score weekly. Memory lies about this stuff. A number on the fridge doesn't.

What I would put in a storm-season kit instead of a second product

If I could only pick one thing for a Gold Coast storm kit it would not be from the calming aisle at all. It would be a written plan: which room, which mat, which treat, who does what when the radar goes red. Add a worn t-shirt for scent, a long-lasting chew, water in a familiar bowl, and a towel that fits the pet properly, because a good wrap is a restraint you already trust. Products slot into a plan. They don't replace one. That is the whole lesson, and it is the one the emergency ward sees the absence of most often.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a calming collar interfere with my dog's medication?

Plant-based products can, because the oils involved are metabolised by the same liver pathways as some medications. Pheromone-only products are generally fine alongside veterinary medicine, but the safe habit is to bring the packet to your vet and ask directly. It takes them a minute and it removes the guesswork entirely.

How long before a storm should I put the collar or spray on?

A collar is a background product, so it needs to already be on and working before storm season, not clipped on when the radar looks ugly. Sprays are the opposite: they are short-lived, so they go on bedding and bandanas thirty to sixty minutes before the weather typically arrives. Neither should be introduced for the very first time on a storm day, because you want the first exposure to happen on a calm afternoon.

My cat grooms my dog. Is that a problem with collars?

It can be, particularly with plant-based collars, because cats are sensitive to several common essential oils and they will ingest whatever transfers to their own coat during grooming. In a mixed household I suggest either a pheromone-only dog collar or a plug-in diffuser that covers the room both animals share, and a call to the vet if the cat starts drooling or acting flat.

Do calming products stop a dog from panicking in an emergency?

No, and this is the point I open the first aid class with. Genuine panic overwhelms scent-based products completely. What helps in an emergency is restraint you have practised, a towel or wrap your animal already knows, and a calm human voice. Practise handling before you need it: ears, paws, being wrapped. That practise is worth more than anything you can clip onto a collar.

How do I know if a product is actually working?

Score the specific behaviour weekly, one to ten, and keep the notes somewhere you will actually look. Look for changes in recovery time rather than perfection: a dog who used to take two hours to settle after a storm and now takes forty minutes is responding, even if the storm itself still worries them. If six weeks pass with no change in the numbers, retire the product and spend the money on a trainer or a vet visit instead.
