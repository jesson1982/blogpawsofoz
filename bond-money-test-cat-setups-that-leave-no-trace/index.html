The Bond-Money Test: Cat Setups That Leave No Trace

By Ellie NguyenSep 2, 2026Cat Window Perches7 min read

Short answer: most cat gear in a rental costs you nothing at the exit if you clean against your entry condition report rather than guessing. Suction cup rings, silicone haze and sill scuffs wipe off with warm water, and what actually costs money is carpet odour, torn flyscreens and scratched door frames near a litter area. My pack-down takes twenty minutes, and three inspections so far have produced no deductions.

Every cat purchase I make as a renter passes through the same filter: will this cost me my bond? Not in a paranoid way. The rules are more reasonable than renters expect, and wear and tear is legally distinguished from damage. But the distinction is decided by what an agent can see and photograph, and a flat with two cats accumulates small marks faster than one without. This is the list I wish someone had given me when I moved in with a perch, a playpen and two cats who treat the windowsill as a stage.

What an inspection actually looks for

Not perfection. Entry condition reports compare present state against the state at the start of the tenancy, so the two documents that protect you are the condition report you filled in properly and the photos you took on move-in day. Inspectors look for damage beyond reasonable wear: holes in walls, stains on carpets, broken fixtures, pet odours that have soaked into soft furnishings. A cat setup that leaves a cleanable ring on glass or a scuff on a sill is not the same category as a torn flyscreen or a scratched door frame. Knowing which side of that line you are on is most of the work.

Damage types that actually cost money

From what I have seen across four rentals, the expensive items are consistent: carpet stains and pet odour that survives cleaning, scratched door frames and skirting near litter areas, torn flyscreens, and pulled curtain tracks. Cheap: glass marks, silicone residue, small paint scuffs on a sill, and dried litter crumbs. The pattern is that cost follows absorbency and surface: anything that soaks (carpet, underlay, upholstery) is expensive, and anything sealed and cleanable is cheap. So my whole strategy as a renter is to keep cats off absorbent surfaces and confine their gear to sealed ones.

Perch marks: the rings, the streaks and the film

Suction cups leave a ring of compressed dust and a faint silicone haze that is invisible until the light hits it. Detergent-free warm water and a microfibre cloth removes both, and it needs doing before an inspection rather than after. The streak nobody warns you about runs down the glass below a perch: cup rubber and cat fur leave a light film that shows up in railings and inspection photos. The fix is a monthly clean of the whole lower pane, not just the cup itself, plus a wipe of the sill where paws land. Dried, done, twenty seconds a month.

Window tracks, flyscreens and the bits nobody checks

The window track is the rental detail I see ignored. A track full of cat hair and litter grit sticks, and a sticking window during an inspection reads as a maintenance complaint, which turns into a note on your file even when nothing is broken. Clean the track with a vacuum nozzle and a damp cloth every few months. Flyscreens are the other one: cats push at them, and a sagging or torn screen is a real repair cost. Make sure the screen cannot be pushed out by a leaning cat, and check it whenever you clean the window, because a small tear discovered early is glue and a large one is a new screen.

The pack-down routine before an inspection

Twenty minutes, in this order: take down the perch and clean the glass and sill; vacuum the window track and the floor under the window; pick up the litter tray area and wipe the floor; open windows for the hour before the agent arrives; and put the toys in their box rather than leaving them scattered. I also keep a small repair kit, which for me is a magic eraser, a matchstick-sized pot of colour-matched paint for chunked corners, and glass cleaner without additives. None of it is deception. It is showing a clean, well-kept flat, which is exactly what an inspection is asking about.

  • Perch down, then glass and sill cleaned with warm water and a microfibre cloth.
  • Window track vacuumed with a nozzle, plus the floor underneath the window.
  • Litter tray area picked up and the floor around it wiped.
  • Windows opened for the hour before the agent arrives.
  • Toys into their box rather than left scattered across the floor.

My own setup and its inspection record

Two lever-lock perches on the bedroom window, a litter setup on a sealed floor with a washable mat, and toys in a lidded box. Three inspections so far, no deductions, and the agent has never commented on any of it, which is the result I am after. What I have deliberately avoided in a rental: anything that hooks over a door frame (marks the paint), anything that tension-mounts into a wall (marks the plaster), and any scratching post that sheds cardboard onto carpet. Every one of those is a lovely product in a house you own, and a small liability in a flat you do not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a landlord charge me for suction cup marks on glass?

Not for marks that clean off, and they almost always do with warm water. A charge would need to be damage beyond fair wear and tear, evidenced against your entry condition report. Photograph the window when you clean the perch, keep the photos in a folder with your move-in pictures, and any conversation about it becomes very short in your favour.

Is cat hair in the carpet going to cost me my bond?

Hair alone, no; it vacuums out. What costs money is odour and staining, which are different problems and both come from litter and accidents rather than shedding. Confine the litter area to a sealed floor with a washable mat, scoop daily, and a carpet clean on exit is normally all that is needed. If your cat has toileted on carpet, deal with it immediately rather than at the end of the tenancy, because set-in odour is the expensive one.

Should I tell the agent I have cats?

Follow your lease and your local rules, and be accurate on any required pet form. Being upfront protects you: undisclosed pets can breach an agreement regardless of whether any damage exists, and it removes the awkward version of the conversation. What keeps your bond is a clean, well-maintained flat, and that is easier to demonstrate when you are not managing a disclosure problem as well.

How do I stop cats scratching door frames in a rental?

Give them a legal target right beside the frame they favour: a tall, stable scratcher placed exactly where the habit happens, plus a temporary protector on the frame itself. Cats scratch for territory and stretch, so placement beats product choice. Check frames monthly, and treat small paint scuffs with a matching touch-up pen before they become the thing an inspector notices.

What is the fastest way to get a rental inspection-ready with cats?

Twenty minutes in a fixed order: perch down and glass cleaned, window track vacuumed, litter area wiped, floors vacuumed, toys in a box, windows open for airflow. Do the airflow part earlier than you think, because a flat that smells like a closed-up litter tray reads badly no matter how clean it looks. The routine works because it is always the same, so nothing gets forgotten under time pressure.
