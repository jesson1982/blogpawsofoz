Coats Over Harnesses: Layering Without Bulk or Rub

By Daniel WhitlockSep 3, 2026Pet Apparel7 min read

Short answer: Fit the harness first, then put the coat over it, and choose a coat cut with enough room across the chest and shoulders. Layering works when the coat takes pressure off the straps, not onto them. Run a short movement check at home before you head out, because most rubbing shows up within the first ten minutes of a walk.

Last July I worked with a cavoodle in Brunswick who had started planting his feet the moment his owner pulled out the coat. The coat itself was fine. The problem sat underneath it: the chest strap of his harness had been riding a centimetre forward all winter, and the fleece lining of the coat had been dragging it back and forth across the same spot with every stride. By the time I saw him he had a rubbed patch under each front leg. Nothing we did with the coat mattered until we re-fitted the harness and changed how the two pieces sat together. That dog taught me the lesson I now pass on to every Melbourne client through winter: layering is a fitting job, not a shopping job.

Fit the harness first, then the coat

The order matters because the harness needs to sit where it can do its job, and the coat needs to fit around that. If you put a coat on first and wrestle the harness over it, straps end up bridging fabric, the chest plate sits high, and everything creeps as the dog moves. Fit the harness on bare coat or skin as you normally would, check the two-finger room at the straps, then dress the coat over the top.

One detail most owners miss: the harness should be adjusted to the dog, not to the coat. If you loosen a strap to make room for a thick fleece, you have changed where the load sits on the body. Better to pick a coat with enough cut to fit over the harness as it is.

The contact points that do the rubbing

Rub almost always happens where two layers slide over a bony or mobile spot. On harness dogs there are four usual suspects. The armpit, where the strap crosses skin that folds with every step. The point of the shoulder, where a coat seam can sit directly over a strap. The chest plate, where padding presses fabric into the sternum. And the back, where a coat's Velcro or zipper hardware can sit on top of the strap buckle and grind against it.

Run your hand under the coat and along each strap after a walk. You are feeling for warmth, dampness, flaked fur or a tacky patch of skin. Any of those means the layers are moving against each other at that point, and you need to shift either the coat seam or the strap position before the next walk.

A movement check before you leave home

Before you clip the lead, put the layered dog through a two-minute drill in the hallway. Walk the dog first and watch whether the shoulder blades lift the coat. Ask for a sit and check that the hem does not drag the back strap out of line. Ask for a down, then a stand, and look for the coat bunching under the belly and pushing the chest strap forward. Then trot the dog ten paces and watch the coat from behind.

If the coat twists more than a hand's width to one side, the fit is not right. A coat that rotates will carry the harness with it over a full walk, and the dog will spend the whole outing correcting against a strap that keeps moving. Fix the twist at home, not at the park.

  • Harness on first, coat over the top, never the other way round.
  • Check armpits, shoulder points, chest plate and back strap after every walk.
  • A coat that twists on a trot will drag the harness with it.
  • Re-fit the harness to the dog, then choose a coat that fits over it.
  • Skip the coat if the movement check fails twice in a row.

Coat styles that leave harness room

Not every coat layers well, and the differences come down to cut more than warmth. A plain fleece pullover with a deep chest panel usually sits over a harness without fighting it. A softshell vest with a contoured chest works over an H-style or step-in harness but often conflicts with a chest-plate design. Quilted parkas are the awkward ones: warm as anything, but the chest panels and belly flaps are exactly where straps want to sit.

When you browse our pet apparel range, look past the warmth rating and read the cut. You want clearance over the point of the shoulder, a hem that stops short of the armpit fold, and fastenings that do not land on a strap line. If a coat only works with the harness loosened, it does not work.

When the rubbing will not settle

Sometimes you fix the fit and the dog still fusses at the layers. First rule out the mechanical causes again: a strap that has stretched, a coat that has shrunk after a hot wash, a puppy growing through a harness size. If the layers are correct and the dog still scratches at one spot or moves stiffly on one side, stop layering and have a proper look at the skin. A rubbed patch can turn into something that needs treatment, and that is a conversation for your vet rather than a fitting problem.

What I do with my own clients' dogs is strip back to the harness alone for a week, then reintroduce the coat for short walks only. If the fussing returns the moment the coat goes on, the problem is the coat's cut, not the dog's patience.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I put the coat on first and the harness over it?

You can, and some working dogs wear very slim straps that way, but I advise against it for most dogs. A harness bridging a coat compresses the fabric against the body, shifts with every stride, and hides strap fit from view. You cannot check strap contact properly through a coat, so problems go unnoticed until the rubbing starts. Harness under, coat over, every time.

How much room should a coat have over the harness?

Enough to slide a flat hand between the coat and the harness at the chest, and enough shoulder clearance that the coat does not lift when the dog trots. If you can see the coat tenting or pulling at the strap line, there is too little room. Too much is also a problem, because a loose coat slides sideways and drags the straps with it.

Do I need to re-check the fit every time I add the coat?

For the first week, yes. Layers settle and stretch, and a harness that sat correctly on day one can creep by day five. After that, a quick hand check along the straps after a walk is enough. If your dog's coat changes across seasons, or you swap between a thin shell and a thick parka, start the checks again from scratch.

Is it safe to attach a car restraint to a harness under a coat?

Yes, and it is the right setup. The restraint clips to the harness, not the coat, and the coat simply sits over both. The only check I make is that the clip and webbing sit clear of the coat's fastenings so nothing presses on the buckle during a trip. If your harness has a car-specific attachment point, follow the manufacturer's instructions rather than improvising.

Does layering change how I wash the coat and harness?

Wash them separately, and check the coat's care label before a hot wash, because some linings shrink enough to change the fit over the harness. Brush dried mud off straps rather than washing the harness weekly, since repeated washing softens webbing and can loosen stitching. Let every piece dry fully before layering again, because damp fabric against a strap is what starts rubbing.
