Matching the Lead to the Walker, Not Just the Dog

By Nina CarusoSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear8 min read

Short answer: The lead should fit the person holding it, so a household with two walkers often needs two leads. Handle size, grip strength, height and who does the road crossings matter more than the dog's weight. A shorter walker usually needs a shorter lead and a traffic handle, while the taller walker can carry the extra gather.

A woman rang us about a lead that kept slipping out of her hand. She had bought the same one her husband used with their Labrador, on the logic that it worked for him. It did work for him. He is 190 cm with hands like dinner plates and walks the dog on the flat before work. She is 158 cm, walks the dog after school pickup along a road with two busy crossings, and could not hold the loop comfortably for a whole walk. The lead was fine. The match was wrong. I now ask who else walks the dog before I ask anything about the dog.

Why one lead rarely suits two walkers

Most lead advice is written for the dog, which makes sense until you watch two people walk the same animal. The dog does not change between walkers. Grip size, arm length, stride length, shoulder height and confidence around traffic all do. A lead that hangs at the right height for a tall walker sits low enough for a shorter walker to feel like they are holding it near their knee, and that changes how much control they have. A handle that fills a large hand rolls around inside a small one.

None of this shows up in the product listing, because the listing is written for the household, and a household is two sets of hands, two heights and often two different routes. That is why my first question is about people, not dogs.

Hand size and grip decide the handle

Handle choice is the most personal thing about a lead. A flat loop is cheap and works for most hands, but it has no give, and if your fingers do not fill the loop the lead will slide. A padded or neoprene handle spreads load across the palm, and it is what I suggest to anyone who has had a lead pulled out of their hand. An ergonomic moulded grip suits smaller hands, and a traffic handle, which is the second short handle close to the clip, earns its place near roads.

The test at the counter is simple. Close your fingers around the handle and check that you can still open your hand slightly without the lead sliding off. If the loop pinches after two minutes of holding, the handle is wrong for you regardless of what it costs.

Height and length are not the same question

Lead length gets sold as a training decision, and it is, but it is also a height decision. A 120 cm lead on a 190 cm walker has a comfortable amount of slack. The same lead on someone 30 cm shorter leaves the dog more room while the handler has less, and the slack sits somewhere awkward to gather. That is the main reason a shorter walker ends up looping a lead around a wrist, which is the one thing I ask people never to do.

When two people of different heights walk the same dog, matching the lead to the shorter walker is the safer default. The taller walker can comfortably gather the extra in one hand. The shorter one cannot invent length that is not there.

  • Ask which hand holds the lead, and whether it ever gets swapped.
  • Measure the loop against the hand, not against the dog.
  • Match lead length to the shorter walker in the household.
  • Buy a second grab point if any walk involves a road.
  • Store the shared lead where both walkers can reach it easily.

Who does the crossings and the car park

The person who walks at 7am down a quiet footpath has different needs from the person doing the 3:30pm school run across two roads. Crossings, car parks, the vet waiting room and a narrow aisle at the pet shop all call for a lead you can shorten with one hand, without reaching down to the clip. That points to a traffic handle or a lead with a second grab point rather than a longer lead. Our dog leads range is sorted by handle type for that reason, because handle shape is the part buyers get wrong most often.

If two people walk the same dog, be honest about which walk happens more often and buy for that one. A lead that is perfect on the quiet footpath and clumsy at the crossing is not a good lead for that household.

The handover between two walkers

Shared leads have a habit of drifting. One walker lengthens it, the next one does not reset it, and by the end of the week nobody knows what length it is meant to be. If both people use the same lead, pick a length, note it, and reset it at the end of each walk.

Storage matters too. A lead that lives in the boot of the car or on a hook by the back door picks up grit, salt and damp, and a gritted clip is the clip that jams. Store it dry, on a hook rather than in a pile, and rinse the clip after a wet walk. A lead that is looked after outlasts the walker who buys a new one every season.

Buying two leads without doubling the cost

Two leads does not have to mean two premium leads. Give the better one to whoever needs the most control, which in most households is the person doing the crossings, the school run or the wet weather walks. That person needs the padded handle and the second grab point. The other walker can usually do fine with a plain flat lead in the right length.

Materials are the other lever. Webbing is the cheapest and the least forgiving once it is wet and gritty, while rope and biothane handle salt and mud with far less fuss. If you are replacing both at once, it is usually smarter to buy one good lead and one sensible one than two identical mid-range leads that suit neither walker properly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can two people really need different leads for one dog?

Yes, and it is more common than people expect. The dog is the same, but hand size, height, grip strength and the route walked all differ. A lead that suits a tall walker on a quiet footpath can be awkward for a shorter walker on a busy road. Buy for the walk that happens most.

Should the strongest walker get the longest lead?

Not automatically. Length should suit the handler's height and the route, not their strength. A longer lead gives a strong dog more room to build speed, which is the opposite of what you want near traffic. If your dog pulls, a shorter lead with a traffic handle usually gives better control than a long lead held tighter.

Is a hands-free lead a good idea for one walker but not the other?

It can be, as long as the person wearing it is comfortable with the dog's weight and the route. A hands-free belt suits a confident walker on open ground. It is a poor match for someone who wants the lead in one hand near traffic. Try it on the quiet walk first.

How do I know if a lead handle fits my hand?

Close your fingers around it and check that your fingertips reach the base of your palm without the loop squeezing. You should be able to open your hand slightly without the lead sliding free. Moulded grips and wide flat loops suit larger hands, narrow ergonomic ones suit smaller hands.

What should I do if a lead keeps slipping out of my hand?

Check the handle before you blame the dog. A loop that is too big, a slippery webbing finish and a spare hand holding a phone will all cost you the lead. A padded handle, a slightly narrower loop and a second grab point fix most of it. Never wrap the lead around your wrist.
