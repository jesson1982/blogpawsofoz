Toilet Breaks and Long Drives: A Vet Nurse's Timing Notes

By Hannah WrightSep 1, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers8 min read

Short answer: I plan a stop every two to three hours for an adult dog, and closer to ninety minutes for a puppy, a senior or a dog built low to the ground. Each stop needs ten minutes, water, shade and a walk on the lead, not a quick stretch beside a bowser.

The call I get most summers is from someone packing the car the night before a long trip. Last December a woman rang about a nine hour drive to Victoria with a six month old Labrador and a fourteen year old terrier in the back. She had the seat cover fitted, the water bowl packed and the crate strapped down. What she did not have was a plan for when to stop. That is the part people skip, and it usually decides whether a dog arrives settled or wound up.

Why the clock matters more than kilometres

Distance tells you very little. A hundred kilometres of highway is a different proposition from a hundred kilometres of winding hills, with the dog standing up every time the car leans. What I plan around is time in the crate or on the seat, because that is what the dog experiences.

An adult dog used to the car will generally hold for two to three hours. A puppy under six months, a dog over about ten years and a dog built low to the ground will often want a break closer to ninety minutes. These are starting points, not rules. Write the stops into the same plan as the fuel stops so they do not get dropped when you are running late. If your dog is asking to stop far more often than usual, that is a question for your vet.

Puppies, seniors and the middle years

A puppy has a small bladder and a short attention span, and both work against a long stretch in the car. At six months and under, plan on ninety minutes and treat every stop as a chance to toilet, drink and sniff something new, because the sniffing is what makes the next leg easier.

A dog in the middle years is the simplest passenger. Between about one and eight, a healthy dog who travels regularly tends to settle and sleep through long sections.

Seniors are the ones people misjudge. A twelve year old may not need to toilet any more often than they did at five, but they do need to stand up, shift weight and walk out the stiffness of lying in one position. I stop a senior at least as often as a puppy, for movement rather than for the bladder.

What a proper stop actually involves

Most roadside stops I see are two minutes on hot bitumen beside a bowser, which does very little for anyone. A stop worth the name takes ten to fifteen minutes and has three parts.

First, the dog stands on grass or gravel rather than asphalt, on the lead and well away from moving cars. Second, they get to walk and sniff at their own pace, which lowers the tempo of a trip in a way that standing still does not. Third, water, a few mouthfuls rather than a full bowl, especially on a warm day.

The toilet part usually happens on the walk rather than on command. Give them five minutes and a patch of grass, and do not stand over them, because that has the opposite effect.

  • Plan stops as fixed points on the map, not as things you will do when someone needs one.
  • Give a puppy or a senior a break about every ninety minutes, fuel stops or not.
  • Ten minutes on grass with the lead on beats two minutes on hot bitumen.
  • Offer water in small amounts at each stop rather than one big bowl at the end.
  • Settle the seat cover flat again before the dog climbs back in.

Small bladders and short legs

Body size matters more than breed labels. A five kilogram terrier crosses the same distance on a fraction of the water, and their bladder is proportionally tiny, so a two hour stretch a Labrador shrugs off is a long time for them. Low set dogs also cover ground differently, and a walk that barely registers for a cattle dog is a proper outing for a dachshund.

Giant breeds sit at the other end. They can hold for a couple of hours, but they need room to stand, turn around and lie flat when they do stop.

The dog who drinks a lot on the road is the one to watch. Some dogs drink out of boredom, then need to stop twenty minutes later, and small sips at stops rather than a full bowl evens that out.

Water, heat and the drinking trade off

There is an old habit of withholding water before a long drive so the dog will not need to stop. I understand the logic and I would not do it, particularly in an Australian summer. A dog in a warm car with no water has a worse day than one who needs an extra stop.

What works better is portioning. A few mouthfuls at every stop, enough to wet the mouth without filling the stomach. Carry water from home rather than relying on a public tap.

Heat changes the schedule. At thirty five degrees the car is uncomfortable even with the air conditioning running, and a dog in a crate in the back gets less of that air. I plan more stops, in shade, with the dog out of the car for longer. Anything beyond that belongs with your vet.

Setting up the back seat for stops

A trip with six stops wears the back seat. Every time the dog gets out damp or sandy the cover takes another load, and every time they climb back in it shifts.

Two things help. A cover that can be shaken out and refitted in under a minute, so you actually do it at each stop instead of leaving it bunched under the dog. And a flat, predictable surface. Hammock style covers keep the dog out of the footwell, but they make it harder for a stiff dog to step in and out, so a flat cover with a firm edge suits an older dog better.

If you are still choosing, the range of car seat covers splits along that line, and shape matters more than fabric on a stop heavy trip. Check the fastenings at every stop, because straps work loose and a cover that has slid forward is a trip hazard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I feed my dog before a long drive?

A light meal a couple of hours before you leave suits most dogs better than a full bowl at the last minute. Some dogs travel better on a lighter stomach. If yours is sick in the car every time, ask your vet rather than experimenting with timing.

What if my dog will not toilet at a rest stop?

Common, especially at busy highway stops. Take them away from the buildings and other dogs, onto grass, and walk slowly for five minutes rather than standing still. A dog who toilets on one patch at home may need that surface. If nothing happens, try again at the next stop.

Do I need to stop more often with two dogs in the back?

Usually yes. Two dogs take up more room, so neither can stretch out, and they stay alert when the other is awake. Add a stop or lengthen the ones you take, and let them out one at a time so it stays calm rather than a wrestling match beside the car.

Is it better to drive through the night with a dog?

It can be cooler and quieter, and some dogs sleep through it. The trade off is that you are tired, and a tired driver at three in the morning is a bigger risk than a hot afternoon. If you do it, plan the same stops and take them seriously.

How do I know if my dog needs a break early?

Restless standing, repeated circling, a lot of air licking and a dog who cannot settle are the usual signals. Some dogs go very quiet instead, which is easy to miss in a moving car. If you see any of it, stop at the next safe place. A sudden change in how a dog travels is worth raising with your vet.
