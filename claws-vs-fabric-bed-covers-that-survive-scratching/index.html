Claws vs Fabric: Bed Covers That Survive Scratching

By Ruth AldertonSep 3, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: Tight flat weaves such as cotton canvas and denim-weight cotton survive a kneading cat; loose loop pile, chenille and boucle do not. Buy the tightest weave you can find with bound seams and a hidden zip, and keep a spare cover in the cupboard, because covers almost always fail at the seam before the fabric wears through.

Eleven years running a boarding cattery in Sydney, with thirty cats or more in residence, taught me more about fabric than I ever wanted to know. The beds that came back from a peak season in one piece had almost nothing in common with the ones that looked best on the shelf. In one block of eight pens I had four identical canvas covers and three cheap plush ones. By the end of the Christmas rush the plush covers had pulled threads long enough to wrap around a finger, and the canvas covers had gone through the industrial machine at 60 degrees and come out square. That comparison, repeated over eleven years, is the whole of what follows.

What a kneading cat does to a weave

A cat working a bed is not scratching it the way a cat scratches a post. Kneading is a push and release: the claws extend, hook whatever is under them, and pull back as the paw closes. On a tight flat weave the claw skids across the surface and loses purchase. On a loop pile, a chenille or a boucle, the claw catches a single yarn loop and drags it out of the structure, and that loop becomes the start of a run. Owners often describe this as a cat destroying the bed on purpose. It is not. It is a comfort behaviour that happens to test the weave every morning. The practical question is not whether a cat will knead. It is which fabric lets the claw slide and which fabric gives it something to hold.

Weaves that survive a determined kneader

Ranked by what I actually saw survive, it goes cotton canvas first, then denim-weight cotton and heavy twill, then tightly woven microfibre. All three share the same quality, which is that the yarns sit so close together that a claw cannot get underneath them. Below that come the velvet and suede finishes, which feel lovely and mark quickly, though they tend to pill and scuff rather than run.

Below those sit loop pile, chenille, boucle and anything sold as a chunky knit, and those are the covers that come apart in strips. Weight is a rough guide but not a reliable one, because a heavy loose knit is still a loose knit. Rub the cover with a fingernail before you buy it. If your nail catches and lifts a thread, a cat's claw will find the same thread within a week.

Seams and zips fail before the fabric

Nearly every cover I binned had sound fabric and a failed seam. The stress of a cat walking across a bed collects at the corners and along the top edge, and a single row of stitching on a plain seam will let go there first. What held up was double stitching, bound or taped edges, and a zip that sits under a flap rather than across the side. An exposed zip gets chewed, catches fur in the teeth and splits at the slider.

If you are choosing between two covers at a similar price, take the one with the heavier stitching and the recessed zip every time, even if the fabric feels thinner. It is also worth checking whether the cover comes off without turning the whole bed inside out, because a cover you avoid removing is a cover you will not wash.

  • Rub the cover with a fingernail and reject anything that lifts a thread.
  • Choose cotton canvas, denim-weight cotton or heavy twill over chunky knits.
  • Check for double stitching, bound edges and a zip hidden under a flap.
  • Keep a second cover per bed so a soiled one is never a crisis.
  • Secure a pulled loop with two small stitches before it becomes a run.

Covers that keep up with washing

Boarding washing is not gentle, and a household machine is not much kinder. Covers went through at 60 degrees with a commercial detergent, then a hot dryer or a line in the sun, week after week. Most cotton canvas coped and softened a little at the edges while holding its size, provided it was washed inside a pillowcase to protect the seams. Anything with a glued backing, a printed panel or a heavy pile lining broke down far sooner.

Poly-cotton blends dried quickly and held their shape but felt slick and picked up static in dry weather. Pure wool covers are lovely in winter and a nuisance in a shared laundry, because they hold water, dry slowly and shrink if anyone turns the dial up. It is worth reading the pet bed guides for covers built to be taken off and washed, because roughly monthly is the real rhythm in a busy household.

Repairs worth making on a sound cover

If the fabric has not run, most of what fails is worth fixing. A pulled loop should be pushed back through with a blunt needle and secured with two small stitches, not cut, because cutting it starts the run you are trying to avoid. A split seam along a corner can be re-stitched by hand in about ten minutes with doubled thread and a backstitch, and it will usually outlast the original machine stitching.

In a boarding laundry I patched the same canvas cover in three places over two winters and washed it between every repair. What is not worth saving is a cover gone thin across the middle, where the cat's weight sits. New stitching only tears out of fabric that has no strength left, and the same applies to any cover with a bubble of unattached backing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a cat scratch through a cover sold as scratch resistant?

A determined kneader will eventually work through anything, because no fabric is claw proof. What scratch resistant usually means is a tight weave that resists a pulling claw, and those genuinely last longer. In my experience the failure point is the seam or the zip rather than the fabric itself. Judge a cover on the weave and the stitching.

Is a thicker cover always stronger?

No, and this catches a lot of buyers. Thickness usually means more filling or a loftier pile, and a lofty pile is exactly what a kneading claw hooks. A heavy loose knit can pull apart faster than a thin tight canvas. Feel the surface rather than the bulk and press a fingernail into it. If a thread lifts, weight will not save it.

How often should I wash a cat bed cover?

In a busy household, once a month is a realistic rhythm, with a spot wash in between for accidents, hairballs and food. If a cat sleeps on the bed every night and sheds heavily, fortnightly is better. Wash the cover inside a pillowcase or a mesh bag to protect the seams, and dry it fully before it goes back on. Ask your vet if a skin problem is driving the washing.

Are cheap plush covers ever a reasonable buy?

They can be, if you treat them as short life items. Plush is soft and warm, cats like the feel of it, and it will look good for a season or two. What it will not do is survive a kneader or repeated hot washing. If you buy one, buy two so a clean one is always ready, and do not spend much on either.

My cover has small holes already. Should I patch or replace it?

Patch it if the fabric around the hole still feels firm and the yarn does not pull away when you tug gently. A hole in sound cloth is a ten minute repair. Replace the cover if the cloth has gone thin and soft across the sleeping area, or if the hole grows when you stitch it. That means the weave has lost its structure.
