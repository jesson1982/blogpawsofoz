Emergency Egress: Getting a Dog Out of the Car Fast

By Rebecca HalloranSep 1, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers7 min read

Short answer: switch the engine off, choose the door away from moving traffic, and release the dog at the clip rather than the buckle. A tether, a crate latch and a carrier zip each have one fast release point, and you want to know yours before you are standing in a hot car park working it out.

I finish every Gold Coast course with the same five minutes in the car park behind the hall. Someone brings their wagon or ute, we put a dog in the back, and I ask the owner to get it out as though the cabin were filling with smoke. Almost nobody is slow because they panic. They are slow because they reach for the buckle instead of the clip, or because a child lock set two years ago is still on and the only working door now faces the traffic. A car has two or three ways out and most owners have only ever used one.

Decide the exit before you open a door

The order matters more than the speed. Stop, handbrake on, engine off, keys in your pocket rather than in the console where a dog can stand on the lock button. Hazards on. Then look at the traffic before you look at the dog, because the door you pick decides which side of the car you will be standing on with your back turned.

On a suburban street the kerb side is almost always the answer. On a highway shoulder it is the side away from the lane, even if you have to climb through from the other door. If the dog is alone in the back and the only working door faces moving traffic, do not stand in the lane to reach it. Open the far door, call the dog across the seat, and clip a lead on before it can jump out over you. The car seat covers guides will help you sort out the back seat itself, but no cover choice makes up for a door you cannot open.

Child locks, deadlocks and doors that will not open

Most rear doors have a small lever or slot on the door edge, visible only with the door open, and once it is set the inside handle does nothing. Owners set it when the kids were small and forget it for years. Check both rear doors this week, then check again after a service or a detail, because a workshop can move them and will not mention it.

If a door will not open, work through the car rather than against it. Front door first, then reach back and use the outside handle. Failing that, the tailgate or boot release, the folding rear seat in a wagon, or a window that still winds down. Keep the manual key blade where you can find it in the dark. A keyless car with a flat battery is a sealed box, and the blade hides inside the fob.

What a tether does to your timing

A harness tether usually ends in one of two places: a clip on the harness D-ring, or a loop through the seatbelt buckle. The clip is the fast release. The buckle is not, especially with a dog leaning back against it, and it is the part most people reach for because it is the part they touch every day.

Teach your hands to go to the clip instead. One hand on the harness handle, one on the clip, then lift rather than pull. If the tether jams, a seatbelt cutter in the door pocket will go through webbing in a couple of seconds, so store it where the driver can reach it without undoing their own belt. Cut away from the dog, keep the blade flat, and expect the dog to lurch forward the moment the tension goes.

  • Keep a seatbelt cutter within reach of the driver, not in the boot.
  • Park nose out so the kerb side stays your working side.
  • Set child locks on purpose and test them once a season.
  • Name the clip you will release before you touch a door handle.
  • Rehearse with a calm dog on a driveway, not a hot car park.

Crates, carriers and the boot latch

A crate changes the job from releasing a dog to releasing a box. Learn the latch with your eyes closed: the catch on a wire crate, the two zips on a soft carrier, the sliding bolt on a car-specific crate. Soft carriers fail in a hurry, because a zip sat on for two years can split along the seam, so open the end facing you and support the base as you slide the dog out.

In a wagon or a four-wheel drive the tailgate is usually the cleanest exit, provided the crate is strapped down with quick-release ends. If it is jammed, fold a rear seat and work forward. Never drag a crate with a dog inside. One person keeps a hand on the crate door, the other works the latch, and nobody opens anything until both of you are ready to move.

The practice run on a normal Tuesday

Pick a driveway, not a shopping centre, and a calm dog, not a hot one. Load the dog as usual, walk around the car once, then get it out by your planned route and time yourself. Most owners take 40 to 60 seconds on the first attempt and closer to 15 once the hands know the way. Use the doors you would really have, with the child locks set as normal.

Then repeat it with a second person, because the roles change: one holds the lead and the door, the other releases. Say the release point out loud, clip not buckle, until it stops being a decision. Do it each season and after any change of car, crate or cover. If your dog panics in the car, speak to a trainer or your vet before you rehearse anything at speed.

Frequently Asked Questions

If someone else in the car is hurt, does the dog still come out first?

No. People first, every time. Switch off the engine, put the hazards on and call 000, then deal with the dog once the humans are safe or help is close. If it is loose and in the way, clip a lead on behind a closed door before you open anything wide. That order is triage, not cruelty.

Is cutting the tether faster than unclipping it?

Usually not, so try the clip first. The cutter is for a buckle jammed under load or webbing you cannot reach. If you do cut, keep the blade flat against the strap and away from the dog, then replace the tether before the next trip. Cut webbing is not worth reusing, even when the cut looks neat.

My dog only wears a collar in the car. What changes?

You lose the handle and the spread of load, so the lift becomes a two-hand job: forearm under the chest, arm around the hindquarters, head clear of the door frame as you turn. A collar gives you nothing safe to grip for the whole weight. Ask your vet or a trainer about a travel harness before your next long trip.

What if the doors will not unlock after a crash?

Work outward from the cabin. Try a front door from the inside, then the tailgate or boot release, the folding rear seat in a wagon, or a window that still winds down. The manual blade in the fob opens the driver's door on most cars when the battery is dead. Do not break glass over a dog, and tell 000 a dog is inside.

Can I rehearse this in a supermarket car park?

Do it at home. A quiet driveway, a cool part of the morning and a tired dog gives you clean repetitions without an audience or moving cars. Keep sessions under five minutes and stop while the dog is still relaxed. If it freezes, pants or tries to climb into the front, get a trainer involved before adding speed.
