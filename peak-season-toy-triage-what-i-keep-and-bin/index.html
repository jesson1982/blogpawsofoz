Peak Season Toy Triage: What I Keep, Mend and Bin

By Ruth AldertonSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys8 min read

Short answer: After a full boarding season I sort every toy into three piles, keep, mend and bin, and I judge each one on hygiene and structure rather than how much the cats played with it. A toy with sound seams and a clean surface goes back to the shelf. A toy that is chewed through or holds odour is retired. Ask your vet about anything a pet with a health issue mouthed.

Every January the cattery quietened and the toy bins told the real story of the season. I would tip them out on the laundry floor, thirty cats worth of mice, springs and feathers, and sort the lot before a toy went back on a shelf. At home with my two cats I do the same each season, on a smaller pile. The habit came from protecting the next boarder from a toy that had seen better days. I learned to look at a toy with cold eyes, not the warm feeling of watching a cat chase it. Some favourites were the first to go. The triage is quick once you know the signs, and it keeps the toy box honest.

The three piles every season produces

The keep pile is for toys that came through the season intact, clean, and still safe to mouth. The mend pile holds toys with one fixable fault, a loose bell, a popped seam, a feather that slipped its stitch. The bin pile is everything else, the chewed through mice, the limp springs, the fabric that holds odour after a wash. In the cattery I sorted by pen so a toy never travelled between cats without a wash first. At home I sort by toy, not by cat, because my two share the box. The three piles keep the decision simple. You are not judging the toy's history, you are judging its future. If it cannot be trusted next season, it does not earn a place. A clear pile stops you hoarding broken toys out of guilt.

Judging a toy on structure not affection

It is easy to keep a toy because a cat loved it, but affection is not a safety check. I look at the seams first, then the filling, then the small parts. A mouse with a sound body but a missing eye is a mend. A mouse with a split seam and loose stuffing is a bin, because the cat will pull the filling and swallow it. Structure tells you what the toy will do next season, not what it did last season. The cats do not care about a toy's past, they care that it moves and makes a noise now. A flat, silent toy with broken structure will sit untouched anyway, so keeping it helps no one. Judge the object, and the decision gets easier every time.

What earns a clean and a return

A toy returns to the shelf when the surface wipes or washes clean, the seams hold, and no small part is loose. Hard plastic and rubber toys get a soak in warm soapy water and a scrub, then a rinse and air dry. Fabric toys go in a gentle wash with plain detergent, no softener, and dry flat. I check the squeaker and the bell, because a dead squeak means the cat ignores it and a loose bell is a choke risk. In the cattery every returned toy passed a structure check before it touched a pen again. At home the bar is the same. A clean toy that still works is worth keeping. A clean toy that falls apart in your hands is not, no matter how bright it looked on the shelf. For the ones worth keeping, our cat toy range stocks the sturdy styles I reach for.

  • Sort every toy into keep, mend and bin before you clean anything.
  • Judge seams and small parts first, not how much a cat liked it.
  • Wash fabric toys on a gentle cycle with plain detergent, no softener.
  • Retire any toy with loose filling or a missing small part.
  • Return only toys that pass a structure check after the wash.

How I mend a toy worth keeping

Mending is worth it only when the toy is sound apart from one fault. I keep a small sewing kit by the laundry and pet safe fabric glue for the bits glue handles better than thread. A loose bell gets restitched into its pocket, a popped seam gets a close whip stitch so a claw cannot reopen it. Feathers that slipped their tie go back with firm knots. I do not mend toys with stuffing showing, because the fix never holds and the cat finds the gap. In the cattery I mended the sturdy favourites through a season and binned the rest. At home the same rule saves a toy or two a year.

Why the liked toy is not always the keeper

The toy a cat chased hardest is often the one closest to failure. A favourite mouse gets bitten, shaken, and dragged under the sofa more than any other, so its seams wear first. I have binned more well loved toys than ignored ones, because the cats showed me which to test to destruction. Affection is a poor guide to safety. The quiet toy in the corner, barely played with, is often the soundest, and it deserves a place because it survived. When you triage, separate the memory of the play from the state of the object. A toy that gave joy but is now unsafe has done its job. Thank it, and let it go.

What goes in the bin without guilt

Some toys simply reach the end, and binning them is the right call, not a failure. Anything with exposed stuffing goes, because no stitch holds once a cat has found the gap. Any toy with a missing eye, bell or bead gets retired, since those parts turn up on the floor where a pet or a child can swallow them. Fabric that holds odour after a proper wash has reached the end of its life. In the cattery I binned toys the moment they failed the structure check, because the next boarder deserved better than a hazard. At home the rule is softer but the line is firm. A toy you cannot trust next season is not worth the shelf space. Let it go, and the toy box stays honest and safe.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I triage my pet's toys?

Once a season is enough for most homes, with a quick pull of anything obviously broken in between. After a boarding spell, a house move, or a long holiday is also a good moment. Sort before you clean, because you waste effort washing toys you will bin. A season clear out keeps the toy box safe and stops broken toys building up unnoticed.

Can I wash cat toys in the washing machine?

Fabric toys yes, on a gentle cycle with plain detergent and no softener, then dry flat. Hard rubber and plastic toys prefer a warm soapy soak and a scrub, because the machine can crack them. Check the squeaker and bell afterwards, since a loose small part is a choke risk. Air dry everything, and only return a toy that passes a structure check once it is clean.

Is it safe to mend a toy my cat chewed?

Only if the body is sound apart from one small fault, like a loose bell or a popped seam. Stitch it closed firmly so a claw cannot reopen it, or use pet safe fabric glue for tiny parts. Do not mend toys with stuffing showing, because the fix will not hold and the cat will find the gap. When in doubt, bin it.

Why bin a toy my cat still plays with?

Because a loved toy is often the most worn, and worn seams fail fast. A cat that bites and shakes a favourite mouse will open it quicker than a toy it ignores. Safety comes from the object's structure, not the cat's enjoyment. If the seams are split or a small part is loose, the toy is a risk no matter how much it gets played with. Retire it.

What should I do with toys from a sick pet?

Keep them separate from the shared box until you know the condition is not contagious. Wash what survives a structure check on a warm cycle, and bin anything with exposed filling or a missing part. Do not let a well pet mouth a toy from a sick one. Ask your vet before reusing or sharing toys after an illness, since some conditions pass between pets on shared objects.
