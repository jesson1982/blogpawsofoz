600D Oxford, EVA Backing, PP Cotton: What Dog Seat Cover Materials Actually Mean

By Mick O'DonnellAug 15, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers8 min read

Mick from the Bush — dog car-travel & ute gear

Out here on the property, a dog seat cover cops a fair hiding. Banjo is a working Blue Heeler, fifteen years young, and he rides shotgun in the ute more often than he rides in the cab of the SUV. He brings mud, he brings sand from the beach, and on a hot day he brings a worrying amount of drool. So when I went looking for a proper 600D oxford dog seat cover, I did what I always do: I ignored the marketing and read what the thing was actually made of. The numbers and the fabric names tell you more in five seconds than the product photos do in five minutes.

Most folks reckon a seat cover is a seat cover. It is not. The material decides whether the thing lasts a season or five years, whether it slips around on a sharp corner, and whether you can chuck it in the wash after a trip to the coast. Let me walk you through the four materials you will actually see on the labels, in plain language.

What 600D Oxford actually means

The "D" stands for denier, which is the weight of the thread, not the thickness of the cloth. A 600D oxford dog seat cover uses a polyester weave with 600 denier yarn. That is a sturdy, tightly woven fabric that resists the kind of scrabbling a Heeler gets up to when he spots a kangaroo. It is the same family of cloth used for decent backpacks and tool bags, so it is built for a beating. A 300D cover will wear through quicker, and a 1680D one is overkill and costs a packet. For the average dog and the average ute, 600D hits the sweet spot: tough enough, light enough, cheap enough.

Oxford is the weave pattern, by the way, not the place. It is a basket-style weave that gives the fabric some give without losing its shape. That matters because a cover has to fold over the seat, tuck down the gap, and still sit flat. Cheap non-woven fabric cracks and goes stiff in the sun. Oxford cloth stays supple, which is why it dominates the better covers on the Australian market.

The backing is the part nobody talks about

Here is the bit the sellers skip. The top fabric keeps the claws off your seat, but the backing is what stops the whole cover sliding into the footwell every time you brake. A proper non slip dog car cover uses an EVA backing, which is a soft rubbery foam layer that grips the seat fabric without leaving a mark. EVA stands for ethylene-vinyl acetate, but you do not need to remember that. You just need to know it is the grippy layer, and it is the difference between a cover that stays put and one that ends up bunched under the dog at the first servo stop.

Some cheaper covers use a dotted silicone grip instead of a full EVA layer. That works, sort of, but it wears off. A continuous EVA backing spreads the grip across the whole cover, so even a big dog shifting his weight on a corner keeps the thing where it belongs. When you are comparing covers, flip it over and look at the back. If it is smooth nylon, it will slide. If it has that rubbery mat feel, you are on the right track.

PP cotton and what it is doing in there

PP cotton is polypropylene fill, the soft padding sewn between the top fabric and the backing. It is there for two reasons. First, it adds a bit of cushion so the dog is not lying on a hard plastic-backed sheet. Second, it helps the cover hold its shape and bounce back after you fold it. Banjo does not care about luxury, but on a long drive to the coast the padding stops him sliding sideways into the door, which keeps him calmer and keeps me from hearing the claws tick against the trim.

The catch with PP cotton is that it holds water if the cover is not also treated for water resistance. That is why the good covers pair the cotton layer with a waterproof top film. You want the softness of the fill and the protection of a waterproof skin, not one at the expense of the other. A cover that is all cotton and no barrier will soak through to your seat the moment the dog shakes off after a swim.

PU leather, the fancy cousin

Then there is PU leather, short for polyurethane-coated synthetic leather. You see it on the pricier covers, the ones that look like they belong in a showroom. It wipes clean faster than anything else and it shrugs off water completely, which is brilliant if your dog is a salivator or you ferry kids and a dog in the same trip. The downside is heat. On a 40-degree day in the paddock, a leather-style cover gets hot, and a Heeler will tell you about it. It also costs more, and the coating can crack if you fold it hard or leave it baking on the dashboard.

For my money, PU leather suits the town car or the beach-run SUV where you want it to look tidy. For the working ute, the 600D Oxford with EVA backing is the honest choice. It is the one I keep on the seat, and the one Banjo has not managed to destroy yet.

The wash question, answered properly

Folks ask me whether a cover survives the washing machine, and the honest answer is: the fabric ones do, the leather ones do not. A machine washable dog car cover is almost always the 600D Oxford type, and you want to wash it cold, gentle cycle, and hang it on the line. Skip the dryer. The heat cooks the EVA backing and the PP cotton goes lumpy. I wash Banjo's cover about once a fortnight in summer, less in winter, and it still looks the part after two years.

PU leather you wipe with a damp cloth and a splash of mild soap. That is quicker, I will give it that, but you lose the deep clean you get from a proper wash. If your dog is a swimmer or a mud-lover, the washable Oxford cover will serve you better in the long run.

Which material for your rig

If you run a ute and a working dog, get the 600D Oxford with EVA backing and a bit of PP cotton fill. If you want the cabin to stay presentable for the in-laws and the dog only rides on weekends, a PU leather cover is a fair call. And if you are buying for a puppy that will chew first and learn later, do not spend up — a cheaper Oxford cover you can replace beats a wrecked expensive one.

At the end of the day, the label tells the story. Read the materials, match them to how your dog actually travels, and you will not be back on the servo shelf in three months wondering where it all went wrong.

Frequently asked questions

What does 600D mean on a dog seat cover?

Denier is the weight of the yarn. A 600D Oxford cover uses a 600-denier polyester weave, which is tough enough for claws and daily use without being heavy or expensive. It is the standard you want for a working dog.

Is EVA backing the same as rubber?

EVA is a rubbery foam layer, ethylene-vinyl acetate, that grips the seat so the cover does not slide. It is softer and lighter than solid rubber but does the same job. A full EVA layer grips better than dotted silicone dots.

Can I machine wash a 600D Oxford dog seat cover?

Yes. Wash it cold on a gentle cycle and hang it to dry. Do not tumble dry, because the heat damages the EVA backing and clumps the PP cotton fill. Most fabric covers are fine; leather-style ones are not.

Is PU leather better than Oxford cloth?

It wipes clean faster and is fully waterproof, which suits a tidy town car. But it gets hot in the sun and costs more. For a working ute, Oxford with EVA backing lasts better and washes properly.

What is PP cotton for in a seat cover?

PP cotton is the soft padding between the top fabric and the backing. It adds comfort and helps the cover hold its shape. The trade-off is that it holds water if the top is not waterproof, so pair it with a water-shedding surface.

Which cover should I buy for a muddy working dog?

A 600D Oxford cover with EVA non-slip backing and a waterproof top. It shrugs off mud, washes in the machine, and stays put. Save the leather-style cover for the weekend beach SUV.
