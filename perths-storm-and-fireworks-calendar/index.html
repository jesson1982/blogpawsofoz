Perth's Storm and Fireworks Calendar: Planning Ahead for Anxious Dogs

By Maddie SorensenSep 1, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

In Perth the noise doesn't land on one tidy date, it spreads across the year, so planning ahead matters more than reacting well. I'm Maddie, a local groomer, and the rough pattern is storm season building through spring and summer, plus fireworks around New Year and a scatter of community events on top. Knowing the windows lets you start desensitising and set up a den weeks early. The dogs who cope best are the ones whose owners prepared in October rather than December.

Perth's storm pattern through the year

Our cold fronts and summer thunderstorms peak from late spring into summer, roughly November through February. Afternoon sea breezes can build into dramatic cells that roll in off the Indian Ocean, and the coastal plain tends to cop more of them than the hills. It's a long window rather than a single night, which catches a lot of new owners out.

That matters because a one-week prep plan misses most of the season. You want your dog settled before the first big roll of thunder arrives, not halfway through it. Storm season here is closer to a four-month stretch, and your plan needs to last that long too, so build the den once and keep it standing the whole time.

Fireworks dates that catch owners out

Everyone expects New Year's Eve, but Perth also has community displays, sporting finals and the occasional riverside show that send dogs diving under beds. These pop up with very little warning, which is why a permanent safe space beats a plan built around one date. A den that's always there covers the surprise event as well as the famous one.

I tell clients to assume at least three or four noisy nights between December and February, not just the one. Treat the whole summer as fireworks season and you'll stop being caught out by a display two suburbs over that nobody thought to advertise. The dogs that cope are the ones whose den was already built in November.

The prep timeline, in plain terms

Rather than memorising dates, work through four milestones and let the calendar look after itself. The order matters, because each step builds on the one before it, and skipping ahead is the most common reason plans fall over. Start with the space, then the skill, then the product, then the standby.

  • October: build the den and let the dog claim it.
  • November: teach a settle cue and start sound practice.
  • December: add a collar, restock the diffuser.
  • January to February: keep the den ready for surprise events.
  • March: wind down, but keep the den standing.

Reading the coastal plain vs the hills

If you're near the coast from Fremantle up to Joondalup, expect more cells and more dramatic afternoons, because the sea breeze collision fuels them. Hills folk around Mundaring see fewer but the ones that arrive can still rattle a sensitive dog. Either way the prep is the same; only the frequency changes, and the coastal crew simply needs the den up for longer.

This is why I push the October start so hard. The coastal plain's first proper storm can land in November, and an owner who started in December has already missed the gentle build that makes desensitisation work. Early prep is a Perth-specific advantage, because our season is long and loud and thoroughly predictable if you watch it.

I'd also remind coastal owners that the sea breeze doesn't keep a tidy schedule, so a front can build on an otherwise calm Saturday and catch a dog who was fine on Friday. That's another reason the den should be a permanent fixture rather than something you erect when the Bureau warns you, because by the time the warning lands the sensitive dog has often already heard the first rumble. Perth's weather is reliable in pattern and unreliable in timing, and the prep has to cover both.

If you're new to the city, ask a neighbour who's been here a few summers what their street is like when the breeze turns, because local knowledge fills the gaps the forecast leaves. The dog owners who've weathered five Perth storm seasons are the ones who'll tell you the den was worth building in October, and that quiet advice is free in a way the products on the shelf never are.

What to do on the actual noisy night

When the forecast shows a front or an event, walk the dog early, settle the den with the radio on, and keep your own voice light. Don't make a production of it, because the dog reads your tension faster than the thunder. A calm routine plus the den he already loves is the whole game, and by February it should be second nature for both of you.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does Perth storm season actually start?

It builds through late spring, with the real peak from roughly November through February, so October is the month to start prep before the first proper cell. The coastal plain tends to cop more dramatic afternoons than the hills, but the window is long either way. Treat it as a four-month stretch, not a single bad night, and your plan will cover the season instead of missing most of it.

How many fireworks nights should I plan for?

Assume at least three or four between December and February, not just New Year's Eve, because Perth dots in community displays, finals and riverside shows with little warning. A permanent den covers all of them, which is why I'd rather owners build one space and leave it than chase each date. The surprise event two suburbs over is the one that catches the unprepared dog out.

Does the hills area get fewer storms?

Generally yes, the hills see fewer cells than the coastal plain, but the ones that arrive can still rattle a noise-sensitive dog, so the prep is the same, just for a shorter season. Coastal owners from Fremantle to Joondalup need the den up longer because their afternoons are livelier. Either way, an October start beats a December scramble for both.

What's the first prep step I should take?

Build the safe space in October and let your dog claim it as his own before any noise arrives, because a den he chooses works and one he's forced into doesn't. Then layer in sound practice and a settle cue through November, add the collar in December, and keep the den standing all summer. That order is what makes the actual noisy night manageable rather than a crisis.

Should I keep the den up year-round?

You don't need it standing in July, but I'd keep it loosely available rather than packing it away completely, because a surprise storm can land off-season and a dog who's lost the habit takes longer to settle. A crate in the corner with the radio nearby is enough; you don't need the full fort in winter. Come October, freshen it up and you're ahead of the season again.
