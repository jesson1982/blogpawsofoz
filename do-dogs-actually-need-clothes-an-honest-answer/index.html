Do Dogs Actually Need Clothes? An Honest Answer

By Maddie SorensenMar 5, 2026Pet Apparel8 min read

Short answer: most dogs do not need clothes, and a fair number would rather not wear them. That is an odd line to hold from inside a ute full of fleece, and odder still from the owner of a whippet with a nineteen-item wardrobe, but it is what the fittings keep telling me. The dogs who genuinely benefit are thin-coated, small, elderly or recently clipped; the rest are wearing it for us. Bottom line, watch for the freeze.

I fit dog coats for a living. I drive around Perth in a converted ute full of fleece and rain shells, and I get asked this constantly, usually by someone whose border collie is standing next to them in a jacket. So let me answer it straight, at the cost of some sales: most dogs do not need clothes, and a meaningful number genuinely prefer not to wear them. I say that as the person who owns a whippet with a nineteen-item wardrobe.

The honest answer sits between two bad ones. The pet industry wants you to believe every dog needs a wardrobe. A certain kind of online commenter wants you to believe that putting a jumper on an animal is inherently cruel. Both positions ignore what's actually in front of you, which is a specific dog, with a specific coat, in a specific climate, telling you something with its body.

Which dogs genuinely get something out of it

Start with coat type, because it predicts most of the answer. A single-coated dog — whippets, greyhounds, vizslas, boxers, staffies, most of the short-haired terrier types — has one thin layer of hair and very little undercoat. Olive is the textbook case: skin you can practically read a newspaper through, a coat about as insulating as a tea towel, and a habit of vibrating on a July morning in Perth. She is visibly, measurably more comfortable in a fleece, and she will walk to the door and wait when I pick it up.

Then the lean and the elderly. Very old dogs often carry less muscle and less body fat, and they feel the cold more than they did at five. Very young puppies don't regulate their temperature as well as adults. Hairless breeds — Chinese crested, the sphynx cats I sometimes fit, my own Noodle — have essentially no insulation at all, and for them a layer is closer to practical than optional, particularly in front of a window in full summer sun.

Geography counts too. Do dogs need clothes in Australia is a strange question because Australia isn't one climate. A kelpie in Darwin needs nothing. A greyhound in Ballarat in July is living somewhere genuinely cold, and a coat makes an outdoor life possible for a dog who'd otherwise be on the couch. I've fitted coats for people in the Dandenongs heading out at five in the afternoon at four degrees, and that isn't a fashion decision.

At the other end of the thermometer, extreme summer sun matters for the thin-coated and the pale-skinned. That's not a cooling conversation, it's a shade conversation — but a light layer can reduce direct sun on a pink-skinned dog on a beach walk. Shade still does more than any garment.

Which dogs almost certainly don't

Double-coated breeds. Your husky, your malamute, your border collie, your kelpie, your golden retriever, your German shepherd. These dogs have a dense undercoat that traps air and does the insulation job better than anything I sell. Putting a quilted coat on a husky in Perth is like wearing a puffer jacket inside a doona, and worse, it flattens the undercoat and stops it lofting the way it's meant to.

I've had this argument in a driveway in Scarborough with a woman whose husky was panting in a jacket in fifteen-degree weather. She'd bought it because it was on sale. That dog was not cold; it was dressed.

And any dog who tells you no. Which brings me to the part people don't want to hear.

The freeze, and why pushing through it makes it worse

Why does my dog freeze in clothes? Because it is a stress response, not an attitude problem. A dog who stands completely still, head lowered, tail tucked, ears back, refusing to take a treat or move a foot, has gone into what behaviour people call shutdown. It isn't sulking and it isn't "getting used to it." The animal has decided that moving is unsafe and staying still is the safest available option.

The instinct most owners have is to wait it out, or hold the dog still, or put the coat on and let them sort it out. That reliably backfires. Every minute spent frozen becomes more evidence that the garment predicts something bad, and the next session starts from a worse place. I've met dogs who took six months to undo what three forced sessions did.

Are dog clothes cruel? A well-fitted garment on a dog who is comfortable in it, taken off after a sensible interval — no, and I'd defend it. A too-tight costume strapped onto a dog who is frozen, panting and trying to escape, for a photo — yes, actually. The difference is entirely about consent and fit, and the dog's body tells you which one you're doing.

How long is long enough

How long can a dog wear a coat? For most dogs, a few hours is plenty. That covers a walk, a cafe stop, an evening in the backyard in July. Longer than that and you're into territory where the risks — rubbing under the armpits, a damp band sitting against skin, a dog who needs to toilet and can't be bothered asking — start to outweigh the benefit.

Take it off indoors. Always. Once a dog is inside and out of the weather, a coat is doing nothing except holding warmth it doesn't need to hold. I say the same thing about cooling vests in February: the garment is for outside.

And check underneath. Run your hand along the chest strap line, under both front legs, and along the spine every time it comes off. Any pinkness, any rub, any hot patch means that garment is not the right shape for that dog, and no amount of adjusting will fix a shape problem.

What I'd tell you in the driveway

Do dogs like wearing clothes? Some do, clearly, and you can see it — the dog who brings you the coat, who walks differently with it on, who settles faster outside. Some tolerate it with mild indifference, which is a fine outcome and not a failure. Some genuinely dislike it, and for those dogs the right answer is a shorter walk, a different time of day, or accepting that your whippet is going to shiver for four minutes in June.

My verdict after years of this: buy less, buy better, and let the dog have the casting vote. One well-fitted fleece that comes off indoors beats five novelty outfits. And if your dog freezes — stop, take it off, and go back a step. There's no prize for getting the coat on.

Olive loves hers and asks for it. Noodle, the hairless cat, has rejected every single garment I've ever put on him, including one I made myself, and I've stopped trying. Two animals, two answers, same house. That's really the whole article. — Maddie, Perth, where it is currently eighteen degrees and Olive is wearing nothing at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do dogs need clothes in Australia?

Most do not. Single-coated, lean, very young, very old and hairless dogs can genuinely benefit from a layer in cold southern winters or in extreme sun, while double-coated breeds generally do not. Climate and coat type matter more than anything else.

Are dog clothes cruel?

Not when they are well fitted, appropriate to the conditions and worn by a dog who is comfortable. It becomes a welfare problem when a garment is too tight, worn too long, or forced onto a dog who is frightened or frozen and trying to escape.

Why does my dog freeze when I put clothes on them?

Freezing is a stress response, not stubbornness. A rigid, head-low, tail-tucked dog has gone into shutdown and has decided that staying still is the safest option. Forcing them to wear it or waiting it out makes the problem worse, not better.

How long can a dog wear a coat?

A few hours is plenty for most dogs. Take the garment off as soon as your dog is indoors or out of the weather, and check underneath the straps and around the armpits for any rubbing each time it comes off.

Do dogs like wearing clothes?

Some clearly do, some tolerate it, and some genuinely dislike it. Watch your own dog rather than the internet: a dog who moves freely, takes treats and settles well is comfortable, while a dog who freezes, licks their lips or tries to remove it is not.
