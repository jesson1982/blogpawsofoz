Side Flaps, Anchors and Pockets: Which Features Earn Their Price

By Jess AllenSep 1, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers8 min read

Short answer: side flaps, anchor straps and a backed, wipeable base earn their price. Quilted padding and a row of small pockets usually do not. Buy the flaps if your dog climbs in wet, buy the anchors if the cover slides, and treat pockets as a bonus rather than a reason to pay more.

I fit covers on customer cars in the shop yard most Saturdays, and the same thing happens with almost every buyer. They arrive with a photo of a cover with six pockets, a quilted diamond top and straps running in four directions, and they want to know whether the dearer one is better. Then I open the back door and see a nine kilo kelpie cross with wet feet. The features that matter are rarely the ones in the photo. I have refitted and washed enough covers now to know which extras I would pay for on my own ute, and which I would leave on the shelf.

What side flaps actually protect

Side flaps are the panels that hang off the long edges of a cover and drop over the seat bolsters, usually 20 to 30 cm deep on a wagon. Their job is not to look tidy. When a wet dog launches through the back door, the first thing it touches is that raised edge, and the second is the bolster underneath it. On most cars that bolster is leather, vinyl or a stitched seam, and it is the most expensive square of trim in the back. A flap takes the claw scrape instead.

A single layer of thin polyester will curl within a month and stop hanging straight, which puts the seam back in the firing line. I look for a second layer or a stiffened hem, and I check that the flap sits behind the door seal rather than trapped inside it, because a flap pinched by the rubber drags the cover sideways every time the door shuts.

Anchor straps and the walk problem

Every cover walks. The question is how far and how fast. A cover held only by tuck-in flaps will creep forward 5 to 10 cm over a fortnight of school runs, and once it creeps, the front lip folds under the seat. Anchor straps fix that, but only if there are enough of them and they pull in the right direction. Two adjustable straps around the headrest posts, pulling back and down, plus tuck-in flaps pushed deep into the crease, is the combination that holds. A strap under the seat base is a bonus on a bench seat, not a substitute.

I test this the same way every time: fit the cover, drive a loop of about 20 km on gravel, then check whether the front edge has moved past the seat lip. If it has, the anchors are decorative.

Pockets, seams and what gets used

A pocket sounds harmless until you have owned one for a season. The flat single pocket across the back of a hammock style cover earns its place, because a lead, a roll of bags and a collapsible bowl live there and nothing else does. Deep box pockets are a different animal. They fill with sand, dog hair and grit off a beach carpark, and they are awkward to turn out because the lining is sewn shut at the bottom. Every pocket mouth is also a seam through the top face, and that is where water gets past the coating. Three pockets on a cover that carries a wet dog is a net negative. One flat pocket, or none, with the money going into bar tacks at the corners instead.

Quilted padding under a wet dog

Quilting looks like comfort and behaves like a sponge. The loft between the stitch lines holds water against the seat, and the stitch lines trap grit that is hard to vacuum out. A quilted top also takes far longer to dry on the line than a flat one, which matters when the cover comes out of the machine at nine on a Sunday night and the dog is in the car at seven.

Where quilting earns its price is on a thin, firm seat with an older dog that struggles to grip, or on a ute bench where the vinyl is hot in February. There, 5 to 10 mm of loft does real work. On a normal rear bench with a healthy young dog, a flat backed base dries faster and does not hold the smell of a wet kelpie for three days.

  • Side flaps of 25 cm with a stiffened hem take the claw scrape off the bolster.
  • Two headrest straps plus deep tuck flaps stop a cover walking forward.
  • One flat pocket gets used; three box pockets collect sand and hide grit.
  • Quilting helps an older dog on a firm seat and slows drying everywhere else.
  • Pay for anchors and a backed base before you pay for stitching.

Where the extra money stops making sense

Price bands sit loosely at under about $60 for a basic flat cover, $60 to $120 for one with flaps and anchors, and $120 to $200 for a fitted, backed cover with padding and a shaped base. The jump from the first band to the second buys almost all of the function. The jump from the second to the third buys fit, finish and a base that stays put on leather. Past that you pay for stitching patterns, branded webbing and more pockets, and none of those change how the cover behaves on a wet Tuesday.

My rule is simple. Pay for the parts that touch the car and the dog, and refuse to pay for the parts that only show up in a photo.

Fitting the features to your dog

Fit decides more than features. A cover 5 cm too narrow will lift its flaps off the bolster the moment the dog turns around, no matter how many straps it has. Measure the seat base at its widest point, then at the front lip, and buy to the smaller number if your seats taper.

On a ute with a bench seat, flaps matter less than a strap under the base, because there is no bolster to protect. With two dogs on a rear bench, the centre takes all the traffic, so one wide cover with two headrest anchors beats two narrow ones that each slide toward the middle. Frequently Asked Questions

Do side flaps work with a child seat in the back?

Fit the child seat first and tuck the flap underneath rather than trapping a folded flap beneath the seat base. A bunched flap changes how the restraint sits, and that is not worth any amount of seat protection. Follow the child seat instructions and your vehicle manual, and if the flap interferes, fold it clear.

My headrests are fixed, so how do I anchor a cover?

Look for long tuck-in flaps and a strap that passes behind the seat back, or loops around the seat frame at the base. Push the flaps deep into the crease and pull them taut before the dog gets in. If the front edge still creeps after a week, add a strap under the seat base.

How do I get sand out of the cover pockets?

Turn the pocket inside out while it is dry and brush it out over a bin, because wet sand turns into a paste that works into the stitching. A vacuum with a crevice tool gets the last of it. This is the main reason I steer people toward one flat pocket instead of three deep ones.

Is quilted padding worth it for an older dog?

On a firm, thinly padded rear seat it often is, because the loft gives an older dog something to grip when it turns around. Keep it to 5 to 10 mm and check how long it takes to dry. If your dog is wet most days, a flat cover with a separate washable mat on top is easier.

Can I run a hammock cover with side flaps in a wagon?

Yes, and the flaps are more useful in a wagon than in a sedan because the boot lip sits closer to the rear seat. Check that the flap hangs outside the seat rather than over the boot floor, and that it does not foul the folding mechanism. Measure flap depth against bolster height before you order.
