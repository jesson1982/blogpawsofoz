Why Playpens Get Abandoned After Week Three

By Marcus HaleSep 5, 2026Pet Playpens8 min read

Short answer: Most playpens are abandoned well before they wear out. The usual causes are a pen bought for a smaller puppy than the one now living in it, a position in the busiest part of the house, and a setup that gave the puppy nothing to do inside. Week three is when growth, boredom and inconvenience arrive at the same time.

I read resale listings the way other people read reviews. A secondhand playpen listing is unusually honest, because it is written by someone with no reason to defend the purchase. The phrases repeat with remarkable consistency: used twice, puppy did not like it, too small for our boy, assembled once then packed away. When I started sorting the listings by reason instead of by price, the pattern had less to do with the pens than with the week they were bought. Most had been purchased in the first fortnight of a puppy arriving, before anyone knew the dog's adult size, its sleeping habits or where in the house it would actually settle.

The week-three cliff

The first week is novelty for everyone. The puppy explores the pen, the household sits beside it, and the whole thing feels like good parenting. The third week is when the wheels come off. The puppy has grown, the household has gone back to work or back to school, the pen has stopped being interesting, and the space it occupies in the lounge room has become an obstacle to a vacuum cleaner. Nothing dramatic happens. The pen simply stays folded against a wall one day, and then every day. Nobody decides to stop using it; it just drops out of the morning routine. This is why the abandonment barely shows up in returns data. It is listed, or it goes into the garage, and the household buys a crate instead.

Size catches up faster than buyers expect

Buyers size a pen for the puppy in front of them, which is the smallest the dog will ever be. A puppy that fits comfortably at eight weeks can be cramped at five months, and the difference is not subtle. Panel height matters as much as floor area, because a determined puppy will test the top rail long before it tests the latch. Floor area matters more than most listings suggest, because the pen has to hold a bed, a water bowl and a toilet area without the bed becoming the toilet area. A one metre square pen gives a square metre of floor, minus roughly the footprint of a bed, minus a bowl, and what is left is the space the puppy has to move in for several hours. Add a growing body and the arithmetic stops working.

This is where the six-panel pen sold as a starter kit fails. It is not a bad product. It is a product sized for a fortnight.

The setup problem behind the resale listing

Placement decides more than panel count. A pen in a back bedroom gets abandoned because the puppy cannot see the household and protests, which the household reads as hating the pen. A pen in the middle of a walkway gets abandoned because everyone is stepping over it twenty times a day. A pen with nothing to chew gets abandoned because a bored puppy tests the mesh, and a pen with a mesh panel is a pen that will eventually be chewed through. The setups that last tend to share three things: a position where the puppy can see a doorway, a bed inside that is the dog's actual bed, and something to do that is not the pen itself.

  • Measure the puppy's likely adult length before choosing a panel count.
  • Place the pen where the dog can see the household, not in a spare room.
  • Put the real bed inside, plus a mat if the floor is tile or concrete.
  • Give the puppy a chew inside the pen so the mesh is not the entertainment.
  • Buy modular panels so the pen can grow with the dog instead of being replaced.

What abandoned pens have in common

Sorting the listings by shape rather than by brand, a few things cluster. Six-panel pens with a single door appear constantly, usually described as new or barely used. Soft-sided pens with mesh windows turn up with a note about chewing. Pens sold without a floor appear with complaints about mess, because a puppy on carpet in an open base is a cleaning problem rather than a containment problem. Pens with the door on the long side rather than the short side turn up less often, which fits with them being easier to place against a wall. The most common listing of all is the pen that was bought before the household decided between a pen and a crate.

The resale market as a signal

A healthy secondhand market for playpens is not a bad sign for buyers. It means the object survives well enough to be worth reselling, panels are standard enough to be interchangeable, and a household can try a setup without committing to the full playpen price. It also means there is a large supply of lightly used pens. For a buyer, that is an opportunity: a pen listed as assembled once is often functionally new, and the money saved can go towards the extra panel set that actually solves the size problem. The catches are missing connectors, a bent panel from being stored under something heavy, and mesh that has been chewed at the corners, which is not always visible in a photograph taken from a metre away.

Buying with the exit in mind

The most useful question before buying a pen is not how big the puppy is. It is what happens to the pen when the puppy no longer needs it. A modular set of metal panels can become a gate across a hallway, a barrier around a Christmas tree, a drying pen after a muddy walk, or a safe zone for an older dog that wants to be left alone. A soft-sided pen with a fixed shape has one job and one lifespan. That single difference explains why metal panel sets hold their value and soft pens do not, and it is a better guide to spending than any claim on the box. Buy the panels, keep the box, and count them before you need them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a puppy be left in a playpen each day?

There is no single number, because it depends on age, toilet routine and what is inside the pen. The practical guide is that pen time should include sleep, a chew and a toilet break. If a puppy is distressed rather than resting, the pen is doing containment rather than settling, and that is worth changing.

Is a playpen better than a crate for a new puppy?

They do different jobs. A crate is for sleeping and short confinement, and it works because it is small and enclosed. A pen is for a puppy that needs room to move, play and toilet away from its bed. Many households end up with both, using the pen during the day and the crate at night.

Can I add panels to a playpen I already own?

Usually, if it is a standard panel set with bar-and-post connectors. Check the panel height and the connector type before buying extra panels, because a mismatched height creates a step a puppy will test. Adding panels is almost always cheaper than replacing the pen, and it is the fix for the most common abandonment reason, which is size.

What floor surface works best under a playpen?

Something washable and not too cold. Tiles and concrete are easy to clean but poor for settling, so a mat or a pen with a base panel helps. A rubber-backed mat under a pen with a wipeable base is the setup that survives a puppy and a hose-down without moving the pen every week.

How do I stop a puppy chewing the playpen itself?

Remove the opportunity rather than correcting the behaviour. Mesh is the main target, so metal bars are the simpler answer. For bar pens, keep a chew inside the pen and rotate it, and position the pen so the puppy is not working at a corner out of sight. A puppy that chews a pen is usually bored, not naughty.
