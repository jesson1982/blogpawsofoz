Lead Clips, Swivels and Wear: When to Replace One

By Nina CarusoSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear8 min read

Short answer: Replace a lead the moment the clip will not snap shut with a firm click, the swivel will not turn, or the webbing shows a single frayed spot under load. Most leads fail at one point, not all over, and that one point is what a thirty-second check before each walk will catch. A lead that opens at the park is a lead that should have been retired last week.

Two weeks ago a caller from the Central Coast told me his Labrador's lead clip popped open as the dog hit the end of the line at the beach. No damage done, but the look on his face in the photo he sent said it all. The clip had been loose for a month, he said, but it always closed again so he kept using it. That is the call I dread, because the fix was a five-dollar clip swapped six weeks earlier. On the support desk the leads that come back tell a clear story: they do not wear out evenly. They fail at one spot, the clip, the swivel, or a single worn patch in the webbing, and the warning signs are there long before the failure. Knowing what to look for turns a scary moment into a calm Saturday chore.

The clip is the first thing to fail

The trigger snap is where most leads give up, and it is the part people trust without thinking. A healthy clip snaps shut with a clean click and stays put when you tug it. When it starts to feel soft, closes halfway, or needs a second push, the spring inside is on its way out. Salt water speeds this up, which is why beach-walking owners call us most. Test the clip every time you clip on: snap it, pull it, listen for the click, because a clip that opens under a sudden lunge is the easiest failure to spot early.

Swivels that seize

The small barrel where the lead turns is easy to ignore until it stops turning. A free swivel keeps the lead from twisting into a tight coil when a dog spins, and a seized one lets the webbing wind up instead. You feel it first as a lead that kinks and stays kinked. Sand and salt are the usual cause, working into the joint and grinding it stiff over a few months. Rinse the swivel after salt walks and work it with your fingers to keep it moving. When it will not rotate no matter how you work it, the lead is past a rinse and a seized swivel will not mend. The constant twist it allows shortens the life of the webbing beside it, so replace rather than wait for the coil to fail.

The single frayed spot

Webbing rarely wears evenly. It frays at one point, almost always where it rubs a buckle, passes through a ring, or takes the load when the dog hits the end of the line. A lead that looks fine everywhere else can have a threadbare patch an inch long, and that patch is the whole story. Run your thumb along the lead, slowly, feeling for a thin spot. If you can see the inner core or separate a strand with a light pull, the lead has done its time. Leads come back with one frayed section and the rest like new, which tells you the failure was local. Check the spot you least expect.

  • Snap the clip and tug it before every walk, listen for the click.
  • A swivel that will not turn by hand has reached the end of its life.
  • Run a thumb along the webbing to feel for one thin, frayed patch.
  • Retire a lead the day a strand separates, not after the next walk.
  • Rinse salt and sand off after beach walks to slow the joint wear.

The thirty-second check

You can inspect a lead in the time it takes to put your shoes on, and it is worth building into the same routine. Hold the lead up, give the clip a snap and a pull, twist the swivel with your fingers, then run your thumb from the handle to the clip feeling for thin spots. That is the whole check. Do it before the walk, not after, because after is too late to act. I suggest people do it at the door so it becomes muscle memory, the way you check the latch on a ute tray before a trip. The owners who call us least are the ones with this habit, because they retire a lead on a quiet Tuesday, not on a busy Saturday when the dog is already pulling at the gate.

What the returns shelf shows

The leads that come back to us follow a pattern, and it is useful before you buy rather than after. The largest group is clips that failed, then swivels that seized, then webbing with one frayed section. Almost none come back worn out everywhere at once, which tells you a lead's life is set by its weakest point, not its overall condition. Cheap clips and stiff webbing show it first, but even good leads fail local when they are used hard near salt water. The lesson is to spend five minutes on a check and a few dollars on a replacement clip when needed. Most leads we see could have lasted longer with one small part swapped in time.

Keeping a spare on the hook

The cheapest insurance against a failed lead is a second one on the hook, so a retired lead never becomes a missed walk. When the thirty-second check flags a clip or swivel, swap to the spare and order the part or the lead at leisure, instead of standing at the door with a dog who wants out. I keep a plain backup lead in the ute for exactly this. The support desk gets fewer panic calls from owners who already have a plan for the day a lead fails. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I replace my dog's lead?

There is no fixed date, because it depends on use, salt exposure and the dog's pull. A lead walked daily on the beach may need a clip swap within a season, while a suburban lead lasts far longer. The honest answer is to replace the part that fails, not the whole lead on a schedule. Make the thirty-second check a habit and retire the lead the moment one weak point shows.

Can a lead clip be repaired instead of replaced?

Often yes, and it is the fix we suggest most. A worn trigger snap can be cut off and a new clip crimped or spliced on for a few dollars, which keeps a good lead out of the bin. If the swivel or the webbing itself has failed, though, a full replacement is the safer call, so ask at a tackle or saddlery counter.

What causes a lead swivel to stop turning?

Sand, salt and grit work into the joint and grind it stiff over months of use, especially after beach and river walks where the lead is never rinsed. Once the barrel seizes it will not free up with oil alone, because the wear is mechanical. Rinse the swivel in fresh water after salt walks and turn it by hand to keep it moving. When it stays locked, replace the lead, since the twist it allows will fatigue the webbing beside it.

Is frayed webbing safe for one more walk?

No, and this is the one I am firm on. A frayed patch is a weak point under load, and a dog hitting the end of the line puts the full jolt there. One more walk is one lunge from a failure at the park. If you can separate a strand or see the inner core, retire the lead before the next outing, because a frayed lead is past the point of a careful repair.

Are retractable leads more likely to fail at the clip?

The failure mode is different rather than simply more common. Retractables tend to fail in the mechanism or the thin tape under load, not the clip alone, and a jammed reel is a separate problem from a worn snap. For everyday walks a fixed lead with a sound clip and swivel is easier to check in thirty seconds. If you use a retractable, test the lock and the recoiling action as part of the same routine.
