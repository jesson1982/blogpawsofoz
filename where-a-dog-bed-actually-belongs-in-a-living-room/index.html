Where a Dog Bed Actually Belongs in a Living Room

By Sofia MarchettiSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

Short answer: watch your dog for a week before you move anything, then put the bed where they already choose to lie. Keep it within about two metres of the household but out of the walking line, check where the sun lands at nap time, and match the bed's base to the floor underneath it. Most beds that go unused were put where there was space rather than warmth and a view.

Almost every living room I walk into has the same problem, and it is not the dog. The bed is in the wrong place. It sits in a corner that looked empty on the plan, or beside the television because that is where the wall socket is, or in a hallway because it was in the way anywhere else. The dog uses it for twenty minutes a day and sleeps on the couch instead. Moving the bed is free, and it changes more than any purchase in this category. Here is how I decide placement, and what I look at before I move anything.

Start with the dog, not the room

Before I move a single thing, I watch the dog for a week. Where do they already choose to lie? Most dogs have picked their spots long before we arrived: the patch of afternoon sun, the cold tile beside the laundry door, the spot at the end of the hall where they can see both bedrooms at once. Those choices are not random. They are a dog's reading of warmth, surveillance and escape routes, and a good bed placement works with them rather than against them. If your dog already flops in a particular corner every evening, that corner is the answer, and the job is to make the bed fit there rather than to find a tidier spot and hope.

Traffic lanes and the two-metre rule

A bed wants to be adjacent to life, not in the middle of it. In practice that means keeping the bed out of the walking line between the kitchen, the couch and the back door, but well within sight of it. My working rule is two metres: close enough that the dog can watch the household and be touched by a passing hand, far enough that nobody has to step around them carrying a hot drink. Beds placed on a main walking route get bumped all evening, and a bed that is bumped is a bed a dog learns to avoid. If the only free floor is on the route, a flatter mattress-style bed that sits below foot traffic works better than a tall bolster everyone has to navigate.

Sun, draughts and the air-conditioning vent

Warmth is the single strongest predictor of whether a bed gets used, and it is the easiest thing to test: stand in the room at the time of day your dog naps and feel where the sun lands. A bed moved a metre into the sun patch will be used twice as much. The opposite is just as important. Look up before you place anything, because a bed directly under an air-conditioning vent or in a draught between an open door and a window is a bed a dog will abandon in summer and winter alike. In a Queensland living room, the ideal is a spot that catches morning sun and stays out of the direct blast of the afternoon cool, which usually means a north or east wall rather than a west one.

Floor surface changes everything underneath

The floor decides what the bed needs to be. Tiles and polished timber stay cool, which dogs seek out in January, but they also strip warmth in July and they transmit sound, so a bed on a hard floor wants a dense base rather than a loose filling that compresses flat in a fortnight. Carpet is more forgiving but it holds hair, so a washable cover earns its keep. Rugs complicate placement in a useful way: a bed placed half on and half off a rug will drift, because the dog's weight shifts it against the pile. Either commit the bed fully to the rug or fully to the hard floor, and if you have an older dog on tiles, add a runner between the bed and the door so the walk to it is not a skating rink.

The four placements I move clients away from

First, directly in front of a television, which is loud, bright and busy for the eight hours a day nobody is watching it. Second, in a dead-end corner behind a door, because a dog who cannot see the room will not settle in it. Third, against a heater or a fireplace surround, which is a fire risk first and an overheating risk second. Fourth, on a raised rug in the middle of the room as a styling statement, because it is the most-bumped position in the house and the dog knows it. Every one of these placements looks good in a photograph. None of them is where a dog chooses to sleep.

Making the placement stick

Once the spot is right, spend a fortnight making it the obvious choice. Put a worn t-shirt of yours under the cover for the first week, feed a couple of meals beside the bed rather than in the bowl's usual place, and drop a treat there whenever the dog wanders over unprompted. Do not pick the dog up and place them on it, which teaches the opposite of what you want. Then watch: if the bed is still empty after two weeks in a spot that has sun, sightlines and no draught, the placement is wrong and not the bed. Move it a metre and try again. Frequently Asked Questions

Should a dog bed go in the bedroom or the living room?

Whichever room the dog already settles in when you are home. Most dogs want proximity to the household during the evening, which makes the living room the usual answer, and then a second flat bed in the bedroom if they sleep there too. Two cheaper beds in the right two places beats one expensive bed in a room nobody uses after eight.

My dog ignores the bed and sleeps on the tiles. Should I move the bed onto the tiles?

Yes, and I would try it before buying anything. Hard floors stay cool in an Australian summer, and a dog choosing tile is telling you exactly where they want to be. Put a low, breathable mat or an elevated cot on that tile and leave the cushioned bed for winter. Fighting a dog's temperature preference with a thicker bed never works.

Is it bad to put a dog bed directly under a window?

It is one of the best spots in the house, with two caveats. Check the sun at the hours your dog actually naps, because a west-facing window turns that patch into an oven by mid-afternoon. And check for a vent or a draught above it, since a bed under blowing air conditioning is the one dogs walk away from most often.

How do I stop a heavier dog sliding the bed across the floor?

Put it on a rug rather than half on and half off one, or use a non-slip underlay cut to the bed's footprint. Dense base beds with a firm, flat underside grip better than soft ones with a loose filling, which shift and bunch. If the bed moves every time the dog gets on it, the dog will stop using it, so this is worth solving.

Can a dog bed look like part of the room rather than pet gear?

Absolutely, and it is mostly about colour and profile. Choose a neutral tone that sits close to your floor or your rug, pick a low-profile shape without a high printed bolster, and keep the cover washed so it reads as textile rather than equipment. A well-chosen bed in the right position stops registering as a dog bed at all, which is usually the goal.
