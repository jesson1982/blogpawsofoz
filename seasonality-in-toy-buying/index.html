Seasonality in Toy Buying: What Peaks and When

By Marcus HaleSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys8 min read

Short answer: Toy demand climbs through late spring into Christmas, holds up over summer, and lifts again around school holidays. It goes quiet through late autumn and mid-winter. Buying a month or two ahead of a peak usually gets you better choice at a softer price, especially in the durable and puzzle end of the category.

Every January I rebuild the same chart and every January it has the same shape. Not the same figures, but the same outline: a long flat stretch through autumn, a climb from roughly September, a spike in the fortnight before Christmas that is bigger than anything else in the year, then a slow slide through January and February. The interesting part was never the spike. It was what happened to choice at each point on that curve, and how differently the same toy is priced in June and in December. Once I started tracking availability alongside demand, the timing advice for buyers became obvious and slightly counterintuitive.

The shape of a toy year

Two things are happening at once in this category, and they overlap badly. One is replacement: a toy wears out, gets lost under a couch, or is destroyed by a dog that has found a new hobby, and the household buys a substitute within days. That demand is steady all year and it is largely insensitive to season. The other is occasion: a new puppy, a birthday, a Christmas present, a rainy fortnight with children at home. That demand is spiky and it is almost entirely about timing. The chart above is mostly occasion demand sitting on top of steady replacement demand, which is why the quiet months still move stock. A category that looked purely seasonal would be unusual, and a buyer who treats toys as a December purchase pays December prices for something the dog needed in July.

What drives the peaks

The Christmas peak is the easiest to explain and the least interesting. Gifts get bought in volume, often by people who do not own the dog, which is why gift-oriented packaging and smaller plush items dominate the shelves in November. The summer lift that follows is different: dogs are outside more, households are home more, and toys get left in the yard and ruined by sun and sprinklers. The school holiday lift is subtler and is about supervision. A household with children at home plays with the dog more often, and a bored dog with children in the house is a household that buys enrichment. Wet weather does the same job in reverse for indoor play, which is why puzzle feeders and sniff mats move steadily through winter rather than sitting still.

The quiet months are not empty

Late autumn and mid-winter look flat on a demand chart, but the composition changes. Durable rubber and puzzle items sell steadily because the households buying them are replacing a known quantity rather than shopping for a gift. Consumable toys, the plush and rope end, slow down. What the quiet months do offer is depth of range: colours, sizes and the less popular shapes are all on the shelf, and the shelves are not being stripped by gift buyers. That matters more than most buyers realise, because the thing that ruins a toy purchase is not usually price. It is arriving at the shop in the third week of December and finding only the size that does not suit your dog.

  • Separate replacement demand from occasion demand before you judge a toy price.
  • Buy durables and puzzles in autumn, when range is deep and shelves are quiet.
  • Buy plush and rope before November, not during the gift rush.
  • Expect summer to destroy outdoor toys faster than winter does.
  • Treat school holidays as a demand signal, not a calendar fact.

Buying against the season

Buying out of season works best for anything in the toy range that does not degrade in a cupboard. Hard rubber, nylon, puzzle feeders, treat-dispenser balls, flirt poles and most cat toys will sit in a box for six months without any loss, which makes a February purchase for the following summer a reasonable trade. Soft plush, rope and anything with a squeaker are a different matter: they store fine but they are the first things a household reaches for, so buying them early tends to mean using them early. The other limit is the dog. A puppy bought in autumn will be a different size and a different chewer by December, so buying a year of toys for a young dog is a waste. Buying a year of toys for an adult dog with settled habits is not.

Choice thins before price moves

This is the part that changes how I shop. In the lead-up to a peak, the first thing that goes is breadth: the extra sizes, the odd colours, the specialist shapes. Price is stickier, because demand is doing the work and there is no reason to discount. By the time a genuine markdown appears, it is usually after the peak, when stock is being cleared and the remaining range is whatever did not sell. So the buyer who waits for a discount in a toy category often gets a narrow choice at a modest saving, while the buyer who shops a month early gets the size that fits at close to the same money. The exception is the January clearance on gift stock, which is real but limited to whatever was over-ordered.

Planning a toy year

A workable plan for one dog is smaller than it sounds. In autumn, buy the durable core: two or three hard toys, one puzzle, a long line for the park. In late winter, replace whatever was destroyed over summer and add one outdoor item before the shelves fill with gift stock. Keep a small box of toys rotated out of reach so the dog does not see the whole collection at once, which extends the life of everything in it. Then in December, buy nothing unless the dog actually needs it. The pattern has held for as long as I have been charting it, and it is one of the few pieces of timing advice in this industry that costs nothing to follow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to buy dog toys after Christmas?

Sometimes, on gift stock that was over-ordered. The discount is real but narrow, and by then the range has been picked over, so the size or shape your dog needs may be gone. For a specific purchase, buying a few weeks before the peak is usually the better trade. For a general restock of durable items, the post-Christmas clearance can be worth watching.

Do cats follow the same seasonal pattern as dogs?

Less so. Most cat play happens indoors, so weather moves cat-toy demand far less than it moves dog-toy demand. There is still a gift lift in December and a smaller lift around school holidays, but the flat stretch through winter is much flatter. If you are buying for a cat, timing matters mostly for price and range, not for when the cat will use it.

How many toys should a dog have at once?

Fewer than most households keep out. A small rotation of four or five items, with the rest stored away and swapped every week or two, keeps novelty higher than a basket of twenty. The rotation also makes it obvious what is actually being used, which is the only reliable guide to what to buy again. Toys that never get chosen are a signal, not a bargain.

Do toys go stale if I store them for months?

Hard rubber, nylon and most puzzle items store indefinitely. Plush and rope are fine if they stay dry, though elastic and squeakers can perish in a hot garage. Anything with a food scent or a treat compartment should be used fresh. Store toys in a dry cupboard rather than a car boot, and check squeakers and stitching before handing an old toy back to a strong chewer.

Why does the same toy cost more in December?

Because demand is doing the work and there is no commercial reason to discount. December prices are not usually a deliberate hike so much as the absence of the markdowns that appear in quieter months. The larger effect is on choice: the popular sizes are gone, so buyers pay full price for a less suitable item and often do not notice until the dog ignores it.
