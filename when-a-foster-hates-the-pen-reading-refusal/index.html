When a Foster Hates the Pen: Reading Refusal

By Fiona EllerySep 5, 2026Pet Playpens8 min read

Short answer: some fosters never accept a pen, and a few should never be penned at all. I give every new arrival a two-week window, make two changes to the setup, and watch whether distress settles within ten minutes of the door closing. Constant panic, panels rattled until they bend, or total shutdown means the pen goes and we manage the room differently.

Whippy, a whippet cross who arrived in Ballarat last winter, taught me the difference between a pen a dog hates and a pen a dog is still deciding about. She protested for forty minutes the first night. By night four she was walking in on her own. But I have had three fosters in nine years where no setup, no schedule and no amount of gentle practice changed anything, and every one of those dogs was calmer within a day of the pen going back in the shed. Learning to tell the two apart is the most useful skill in my fostering notebook, and it is mostly watching rather than buying equipment.

What normal pen adjustment looks like

A normal first week has rough edges but a direction of travel. Night one might bring whining, pacing, a paw lifted against the mesh, or a dog who refuses dinner until you sit nearby. What convinces me is improvement measured in days: shorter episodes of fussing, faster settling once the lights go off, a dog who chooses to take a chew into the pen when the door is open.

The morning matters more than the night. A foster who has whined for ten minutes but then sleeps through, eats breakfast and greets you with a loose body has had a normal night. I keep notes in a cheap notebook beside the pen, one line per session, because memory lies and patterns show up on paper within three days. Most dogs I have fostered land inside this first category.

What genuine refusal looks like

Genuine refusal is flat and unremitting. The dog panics within seconds rather than minutes: drooling heavily, throwing themselves at the panels, digging at the floor seams, or going completely still and refusing to lie down for the whole session. There is no arc across the week. Night four looks like night one, and the dog starts bracing the moment they spot the pen from across the room.

Shutdown worries me more than noise. A foster who stands frozen in the corner of the pen for two hours, not touching the blanket or the water, is not learning to settle; they are enduring. That dog needs a different arrangement, not more practice. If I am unsure, I ask my vet or the rescue's behaviour contact to look at what I am seeing rather than guessing my way through it.

Changes I try before giving up

My first change is location. A pen in a cold hallway with nothing to look at is a lonely place, so I shift it into whatever room the household actually uses. Dogs settle when they can hear ordinary life around them. Second, I put the pen against a wall rather than out on open floor, because two exposed sides feel safer than four.

Then I change what happens in the pen. Every meal gets fed in there, door open. The best chews of the day happen in there, door open, me in the room. Sessions shrink to short stretches that end before fussing starts, then lengthen slowly. I had one mastiff cross who only relaxed once I covered three sides with an old sheet; he went from an hour of pacing to sleeping through the afternoon in a single day.

  • Give a new foster two weeks before deciding the pen has failed.
  • Improvement across days is adjustment; identical nights are refusal.
  • Move the pen into a lived-in room against a wall.
  • Feed every meal in the pen with the door open first.
  • Treat silent shutdown as a stop signal, not progress.

Placement and timing of pen sessions

Timing does more than any gadget I have bought. I pen a new foster when they are already tired and fed, never straight after arriving and never when the house is full of visitors. A short session that ends well teaches more than a long one that ends in barking, because the last minute of a session is the part the dog remembers.

I also match sessions to the household rhythm. Pen time while I cook dinner works well, because the dog can see and smell everything without having to make decisions about it. Pen time after everyone has gone to bed usually fails, since a dark quiet house leaves a nervous dog alone with every sound. If the only slot available is the bad one, I skip the pen that day.

Choosing a different pen for the dog

Sometimes the animal is fine and the equipment is wrong. Wire panels with wide gaps frustrate dogs who want to poke a nose through, and soft fabric pens fall apart under a dog who tests seams with their front paws. For a pusher and digger I use heavier panels with narrow spacing and a solid floor tray, and when a tray cracks I replace it through the usual playpens range. For a watcher who wants sightlines I use open mesh and skip any cover.

Size cuts both ways. A pen too small for an adult to stretch out flat breeds frustration within days, while an enormous pen in the middle of a room can leave an anxious dog feeling exposed. I start most fosters with a mid-size setup against a wall and adjust from there once I have seen a week of sessions.

Managing the room without a pen

Going pen-free is not giving up; it is changing tools. For a dog who cannot do pens, I puppy-proof one room properly: bins out, cords lifted or covered, anything chewable gone, and a baby gate in the doorway if I need to limit access. A dog-proofed laundry or spare room gives the same containment with none of the pressure, and most dogs settle in it within a night.

The trade-off is honest: a pen-free setup takes more preparation and leaves less flexibility for short-notice arrivals, which is why my car kit still includes panels. But the pen is a means of keeping an animal safe while they learn the house, not a test they must pass. When a foster is calmer without it, the right call is obvious by the second night.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I keep trying before giving up on the pen?

I use a two-week window with two deliberate changes along the way, usually location and routine. If session four looks exactly like session one, or the dog starts bracing at the sight of the pen, the outlook is poor. Genuine refusal rarely improves after that point, and my three pen-free fosters were all clearly failing by day ten.

Is some whining in the pen normal at first?

A little, yes. Ten minutes of protest on the first night or two is common and usually fades once the routine is predictable. I judge whining by its direction rather than its volume: shorter each session is fine, identical or worse every night is not. Pair the pen with meals and good chews so the association builds itself.

Can a pen be too big for a dog?

It can. A huge pen placed on open floor leaves an anxious dog with too much space to patrol and nowhere to feel tucked away. I start most fosters with a mid-size pen against a wall, which gives room to stretch and turn without turning the space into a corridor. If a dog settles in the corner only, I make it smaller.

What do I use instead of a pen for a foster who refuses?

A properly dog-proofed room works for most of them. I clear the chewing risks, lift the cords, remove bins and cover anything I would hate to lose, then use a baby gate for the doorway. The dog gets containment without confinement, and I get a room I can walk into at any hour. Every pen-free foster I have had settled faster this way.

Should the pen ever be used as a punishment?

Never. If the pen becomes where a dog lands after something frightening or after you are cross, it turns into a place to avoid and you lose your best settling tool. I keep good things happening in there: meals, chews, quiet company. Timeouts belong somewhere else entirely, not in the one space that is meant to feel safe.
