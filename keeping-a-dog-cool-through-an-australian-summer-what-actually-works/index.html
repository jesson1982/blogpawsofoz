Keeping a Dog Cool Through an Australian Summer: What Actually Works

By Maddie SorensenMar 5, 2026Pet Apparel7 min read

Short answer: shade, water, airflow and walking at the right hour do almost all of the work, and the gear does the rest. Olive proved that on a forty-one degree afternoon in Perth, when twenty minutes of a four o'clock walk had her lying down on someone's nature strip and me carrying her home. If you only change one habit this summer, move the walk to before seven in the morning.

It was forty-one degrees in Perth and I'd made the rookie mistake of walking Olive at four in the afternoon because that's when I finished work. She's a whippet: fifteen kilos of muscle, skin you can see through, and a coat about as insulating as a tea towel. Twenty minutes in she stopped, lay down in someone's nature strip, and refused to move. I carried her home. That's how I learned the difference between a dog who is warm and a dog who is in trouble.

She was fine by the evening, but I've spent the years since then working out what actually keeps a dog cool, and most of it isn't what people buy. Here's the honest version, from someone who fits cooling gear for a living and has also got it wrong.

Dogs don't sweat the way we do

This is the fact everything else hangs off. Dogs have a few sweat glands in their paw pads and nowhere else worth mentioning. They cool themselves almost entirely by panting — moving air across the moist surfaces of the mouth and upper airway, and losing heat through evaporation. On a hot, humid day that system works badly. On a hot, dry Perth day it works, but it costs them a lot of water and a lot of effort.

Which is why the flat-faced breeds struggle so much. A bulldog or a pug has the same cooling hardware crammed into a much shorter airway. They're not being dramatic when they stop on a walk; they're telling you the maths isn't working.

The four things that actually help

Shade comes first and it isn't close. Moving a dog out of direct sun does more in thirty seconds than any product does in an hour. Second is water, available constantly and offered often, not just at the end. Third is timing — early morning and after sunset, before seven and after seven, which is the advice every vet in Australia gives and the advice most people ignore on their way out the door at lunchtime.

Fourth is evaporative cooling, and that's where a cooling vest belongs. You wet it, wring it out, and put it on. As the water evaporates it draws heat off the body, which is exactly the mechanism your dog is already using with their tongue, just spread across a much larger surface. It's not refrigeration and it's not magic. It's assisting the process they're already running.

What it doesn't do

I want to be direct here because the marketing in this category is genuinely misleading. A cooling vest does not prevent heatstroke. Nothing you can buy does. Heatstroke is a veterinary emergency that kills dogs in Australian summers every year, and the things that prevent it are shade, water, sensible timing and not leaving an animal in a car.

What a vest does is make a twenty-minute walk tolerable for a dog who would otherwise be struggling by minute ten. Olive recovers faster, pants less, and is willing to keep moving. That's the whole pitch, and I'd rather it be an honest one than a dramatic one.

Also worth knowing: in very humid conditions evaporative cooling works poorly, because the air is already carrying as much water as it can hold. In Darwin in the build-up, a wet vest is a wet vest. In Perth, Adelaide or inland NSW, it does real work.

Recognising trouble early

Early signs: heavy noisy panting, bright red gums, thick ropey saliva, refusing to continue, seeking shade on their own. Later signs are the frightening ones and they don't always look like distress — sometimes panting goes quiet and shallow, the gums turn pale or grey, the dog becomes wobbly or collapses.

If you see the later signs, this is not a hose-and-wait situation. Cool the dog with tepid (not ice-cold) water, get air moving over them, and call a vet on the way. Ice water sounds logical and it's harmful, because it drives blood away from the skin and slows cooling.

Using the gear properly

Fit matters more than people expect. A cooling vest needs to sit against the body to work, so measure the chest at its widest point and the back from the base of the neck to the base of the tail, and check the brand's chart rather than guessing from weight. If your dog falls between two sizes, go up — a vest that's too tight restricts the chest and won't sit flat.

Re-wet it when it dries. On a hot dry afternoon that can be every half hour, and a dry cooling vest is just a slightly damp piece of polyester doing nothing. Keep a bottle in the car for exactly this.

And take it off inside. Once your dog is in air conditioning, the vest isn't helping and a damp band sitting against warm skin is how you get a rub or a hot spot.

Olive wears one on every walk from November through March and she's a different dog for it — but she also gets walked at six in the morning now, and that change mattered more than the gear. Shade, water, timing, then the vest. In that order. — Maddie, Perth, writing this at five-thirty in the morning like a sensible person.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do cooling vests actually work for dogs?

Yes, in dry heat. They work by evaporation: wet the vest, wring it out, and as the water evaporates it draws heat off the body. They assist the panting your dog is already doing rather than replacing it. In very humid conditions they work much less well.

Can a cooling vest prevent heatstroke?

No. Nothing you can buy prevents heatstroke. Shade, constant water, walking in the cool parts of the day and never leaving a dog in a car are what prevent it. A vest makes a walk more comfortable; it is not a safety guarantee.

What are the signs of heatstroke in dogs?

Early signs include heavy noisy panting, bright red gums, thick saliva and refusing to walk. Later and more serious signs include quiet shallow panting, pale or grey gums, wobbliness, vomiting and collapse. The later signs are an emergency — cool with tepid water and get to a vet immediately.

How do I size a cooling vest?

Measure the chest at its widest point behind the front legs and the back length from the base of the neck to the base of the tail. Match those to the brand's chart rather than guessing from weight. If your dog is between sizes, choose the larger one.

How often do I re-wet it?

Whenever it dries. On a hot dry day that can be every twenty to thirty minutes, so carry a bottle of water specifically for the vest. A dry cooling vest provides no cooling at all.
