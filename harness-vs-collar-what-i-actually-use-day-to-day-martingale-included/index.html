Harness vs Collar: What I Actually Use Day to Day (Martingale Included)

By Daniel WhitlockJan 15, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear7 min read

Short answer: a simple collar still earns its place for ID tags and letterbox trips, but a harness does the daily work, and a martingale covers any dog that slips out. The truth is most homes are better off with both within reach, because the collar is the backup if the harness slips at the beach. My hook holds a reflective one beside a padded one, and the martingale proved itself after a dog escaped two flat ones.

I used to think the collar-versus-harness debate was one of those internet arguments that does not matter in real life. Then I owned a dog that slipped every collar I bought and a different dog that pulled like a freight train, and suddenly the gear on my hook became a genuine daily decision. Here is what I actually reach for, and why a dog collar and lead set still earns a place in my rotation even though I am mostly a harness person now.

The case for the humble collar

For quick trips to the letterbox, backyard hangs, and ID tags, nothing beats a simple collar. My everyday one is a reflective dog collar Australia residents would appreciate on a dark winter evening. The reflective stitching means I can spot my boy from the car headlights when he wanders to the fence, and that small detail has genuinely stopped a couple of near-misses on the street.

But a flat collar is not for walking a puller. The moment a dog hits the end of a flat collar at speed, all that force lands on the throat. That is where I started looking at alternatives, and where the martingale entered my life.

How a martingale collar actually works

People hear "martingale" and picture a choke chain, which it is not. A martingale collar how it works explanation is simple: it has a main loop that goes around the neck and a smaller control loop the lead attaches to. When the dog pulls, the collar tightens just to the size of the neck and then stops. It cannot close further, so it gives a gentle correction without the horror-show tightening of a slip chain.

I use a martingale for my sighthound-mix because his head is narrower than his neck and he backs out of buckles like a magician. The martingale tightens under tension and loosens the second he relaxes, so he stays put without me wrenching on him. It is the one collar I trust for dogs that treat escapes like a hobby. The martingale collar how it works logic is what makes it safe: it can never close past the neck measurement, so there is no risk of the choking a slip chain invites.

When I switch to a harness

For actual exercise walks, I almost always grab a padded dog harness. The padding matters more than you would think. A thin strap across the chest rubs raw on a long walk, and a padded dog lead for pulling keeps my own hands from getting chewed up when the big lad decides a skateboard is a personal enemy. Padded gear is the difference between a pleasant stroll and a blistery regret.

On hot days, the breathable mesh dog harness comes out. A mesh dog harness summer Australia owners need is not a luxury here, it is survival. My older boy overheats fast, and the mesh lets air move through instead of trapping a sweaty sauna against his coat. We went from cutting walks short to actually finishing them.

Big dogs need serious hardware

My neighbour's mastiff-cross is the reason I researched a large dog harness strong enough to hold a small horse. Thin clips and flimsy webbing are a joke at that size. The one we landed on has reinforced stitching and a belly strap that will not pop when he spots a cat. For the truly determined, a large breed dog harness no escape design with both a front and back clip plus a belly band is the only thing that keeps him contained.

I borrowed that harness on a holiday dogsit and finally understood the appeal. The dual clip lets you attach a lead at the back for relaxed walks and the front for redirection, and the belly strap stops the wriggle-out that plagues escape artists. It is bulkier, but for a strong dog it is peace of mind.

Why I keep both within reach

The honest answer is I do not pick one. A padded dog harness lives by the door for walks, and a martingale hangs on the hook for the dogs that need it. The reflective dog collar Australia stays on everyone for ID even when the harness is doing the work, because a harness has no tag ring I trust at the off-leash beach. Layering the two sounds fussy until the day your dog slips a harness and the collar still has the tag with your number on it. Even my dog collar and lead set gets a weekly wipe, because a stiff, crusty collar is the fastest way to make a dog dodge the lead.

Fit checks I no longer skip

Gear fails when it fits badly, not when it is cheap. I run the two-finger test on every strap monthly, because a large dog harness strong enough to hold a horse is useless if the belly strap rides up. The mesh dog harness summer Australia also stretches a little with washing, so I re-tighten after the first few cycles. A padded dog lead for pulling wears at the handle long before the clip, and I replace it the moment the padding thins rather than waiting for a snap on a busy road.

My rule of thumb

So what do I actually use? Collar for ID and quick yard trips, martingale for the escape-prone walker, padded mesh harness for hot exercise days, and a heavy no-escape rig for the strong puller. There is no single winner, only the right tool for the dog in front of you. The gear is supposed to serve the dog, not the other way around.

Match the tool to the dog, and the walk gets easier. From one very opinionated gear-hook organiser.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a collar or harness better for walking?

For exercise walks with a puller, a padded harness is kinder because it spreads pressure across the chest. A flat collar is fine for ID and quick yard trips but not for walking a dog that pulls.

How does a martingale collar work?

A martingale has a main loop around the neck and a smaller control loop for the lead. When the dog pulls it tightens only to neck size and stops, giving a gentle correction without the choking of a slip chain.

When should I use a padded harness?

Use a padded harness for actual walks, especially long ones, because the padding stops chest rub and saves your hands on a pulling dog. On hot days swap to a breathable mesh version.

What harness does a large strong dog need?

A large dog needs reinforced stitching, a rated buckle, and ideally a no-escape design with front and back clips plus a belly strap. That hardware is what keeps a strong puller contained.

Do I need both a collar and a harness?

Many owners keep both: a harness by the door for walks and a martingale or reflective collar for ID and escape-prone dogs. A collar with a tag is still the backup if a harness slips at the beach.
