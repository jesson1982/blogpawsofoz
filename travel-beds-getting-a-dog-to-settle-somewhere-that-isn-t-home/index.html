Travel Beds: Getting a Dog to Settle Somewhere That Isn't Home

By Tessa NgataApr 10, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: a travel bed only works if you put your dog's own unwashed bedding inside it, because the point is carrying home into an unfamiliar place, not comfort. The first time I took Pip away she just circled the spare room and would not settle. What fixed it on the second trip was her actual cushion from home, unwashed, tucked into the shell. The truth is the familiar smell matters more than the padded insert, so pack the old thing and treat the shell as a holder.

The first time I took Pip to a friend's house for a weekend, she spent the entire first evening circling. She wouldn't lie down, she wouldn't eat, and she kept returning to the front door. I'd bought her a travel bed and set it up in the corner of the spare room, and she walked straight past it. What fixed it wasn't the bed. It was the fact that on the second trip I brought her actual bedding from home, unwashed, and put it inside the travel bed.

That's the whole principle, and it's worth understanding properly before you buy anything: a travel bed is not really about comfort. It's about carrying a piece of home into an unfamiliar space.

Why a familiar bed works at all

Smell is doing most of the work. A dog's sense of smell is somewhere in the order of ten thousand to a hundred thousand times more sensitive than ours, and their own scent on their own bedding is a strong signal that a place is theirs. In a strange room with strange sounds and strange smells, that one familiar object does a surprising amount of calming.

It also defines a territory, which matters more than owners realise. In a holiday house or a hotel room, the whole space is unfamiliar and slightly overwhelming. A bed gives them a defined patch that is unambiguously theirs, somewhere to retreat to when the front door opens or the kids get loud.

And it gives them a job at bedtime. Dogs settle far faster when there's a clear cue that sleep is coming, and their own bed in the corner of a strange room is about as clear as it gets.

In the car

Safety first, and it isn't negotiable: a dog should never travel loose in a car, and a bed must never become a projectile in a sudden stop. A non-slip mat in the footwell or a secured crate with a mat inside are the sensible options. Don't put a loose bed on the back seat and assume it's fine.

Beyond that, a non-slip base matters because a bed that slides around on every corner makes a dog reluctant to settle on it at all. Something low-profile works better than something deep — a thick bed in a footwell takes up the space they need to lie comfortably. And take it out of the car when you park, because a car in an Australian summer reaches dangerous temperatures in minutes and a bed sitting in one doesn't change that.

In accommodation

Set it up the moment you arrive, before you unpack anything else. Put it in a quiet corner, ideally away from the door and away from any air conditioner that cycles on noisily through the night. Then leave it alone — a dog who is circling and sniffing is doing exactly what they should be doing, and hovering over them makes it worse.

Keep the bedtime routine as close to home as you can. Same time, same order, same few words. It sounds precious and it works, particularly on the first night.

Cats are a different exercise entirely

Most cats do not want an open travel bed. They want somewhere to hide. The single most useful thing you can bring is their carrier, left open on the floor with familiar bedding inside it — which turns the thing they associate with terror into a den they can retreat into. It works remarkably well.

A covered bed can also work, positioned where they'd choose to hide anyway, which is usually under something. Then the rule everyone breaks: leave them alone for the first day. Cats explore a new space on their own timetable, and pulling them out of a hiding spot to reassure them undoes a day of settling. Food, water, litter tray and their scent in the room, then patience.

What to pack

An unwashed item from their bed at home — a small blanket or a cover is ideal and takes almost no room. A bed that folds or packs flat and dries quickly. Something you can wash at the other end, because a travel bed gets dirtier faster than a home bed. And if you're camping, something that keeps them off cold or damp ground, since the ground is where all the temperature goes.

That's the list. Most people overpack for pets and under-think the smell, and it's the other way round.

Bring something that smells like home, set it up before you unpack, and put it where they'd choose rather than where you'd like it. The bed is just the carrier for the familiar thing. Pip now sleeps through the first night at a friend's place, which took three trips and one unwashed blanket to achieve. — Tessa, Hobart, packing light since 2019.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do travel beds actually help dogs settle away from home?

Yes, mostly because of scent rather than comfort. Bring an unwashed item from their bed at home and put it inside the travel bed. That familiar smell is what does most of the settling work in an unfamiliar room.

How should my dog travel in the car?

Never loose. Use a secured crate with a non-slip mat inside, or a properly fitted harness restraint. A loose bed on a seat is not a restraint and can become a projectile in a sudden stop.

Where should I put the bed when we arrive?

In a quiet corner, away from the door and from any noisy air conditioner, set up before you unpack anything else. Then leave your dog to investigate it in their own time.

What about travelling with a cat?

Bring their carrier, left open with familiar bedding inside it. Most cats want somewhere to hide rather than an open bed. Set up food, water and a litter tray in one room, then leave them alone for the first day.

Can I leave a bed in a parked car?

Take it with you. A car in the sun reaches dangerous temperatures within minutes, and a bed inside one makes no difference. If your dog can't come with you, leave them at home.
