Styling a Window Perch Around Blinds, Curtains and Flyscreens

By Sofia MarchettiSep 2, 2026Cat Window Perches8 min read

Short answer: plan the window in layers from the glass outwards before you buy anything. Sheers and full curtains usually mean mounting on an uncovered pane or moving to a freestanding tower, timber venetians and vertical blinds are best served by a tower beside the glass, and a fixed security screen is the easy case. Measure the glass, the frame depth and every layer of window furniture, then decide where the perch stops and the covering starts.

Every perch decision in an Australian home comes down to the same question, and it is not about suction cups: what else is on that window? Blinds, sheers, timber venetians, security screens and flyscreens all occupy the same few centimetres of glass and frame that a perch wants. Get this wrong and you end up with a functional perch and a daily annoyance, which is how most perch regret starts. Here is the order I plan a window in, and the clashes worth solving before you buy anything.

Plan the window in layers, from glass outwards

Work from the glass towards the room and you will not miss anything. Layer one is the glass itself, which is what a suction mount grips. Layer two is the frame, which is what an over-frame hammock hooks onto and what a pole system presses against. Layer three is the window furniture: blind track, curtain rod, sheer, screen. Layer four is the furniture in the room, which decides the sightline and the height you want. The mistakes all happen when somebody buys at layer one and discovers layer three afterwards, and the perch is already on the glass before they notice the sheer hangs exactly where the cat's head will be.

  • Layer one, the glass: what a suction mount actually grips.
  • Layer two, the frame: what carries an over-frame hammock or a tension pole.
  • Layer three, the window furniture: blind track, curtain rod, sheer and screen.
  • Layer four, the room: the sightline and the height you want the perch at.

Curtains, sheers and roller blinds

Curtains are the most common clash and the easiest to design around. A sheer or a full curtain drops straight down the glass, and a perch mounted on the glass means the curtain hangs over the cat or is permanently pulled aside, which ruins the drape of the fabric and looks unresolved. Two workable answers: mount the perch on a pane the curtain does not cover, or choose a window with a rail wide enough that the curtain sits outside the glass entirely. Roller blinds are more forgiving because they travel vertically and can stop above the perch head height, so a roller blind window takes a perch comfortably if you set the blind's resting point deliberately rather than leaving it to memory. And note the ratio: a perch wants roughly the bottom half of the pane, while a blind's natural rest position is usually at the top, so the two coexist easily once you have decided where each stops.

Timber venetians and vertical blinds

These are the awkward ones. Timber venetians need to tilt, and a perch sitting in front of them blocks a slat run, which means the cat's favourite window becomes the one blind in the house that never fully closes. Vertical blinds have the same problem in a different direction, and the slats catch on a perched cat's tail as it turns. Neither is a reason to give up on the window. My answer is usually a freestanding tower placed beside the glass rather than mounted on it, or a perch on the fixed pane of a window where the blinds are stacked open. There is a small seasonal trick here too: in winter, a cat on a perch with the blinds partly open gets the sun and warms the room less effectively, so tenants who care about heating find that a perch works better on a north window with a roller blind than on a west window with venetians.

Flyscreens, security screens and the open-window question

Most Australian windows have a screen, and it changes what an open window means. A perch mounted on the glass puts the cat on the room side of the screen, which is fine, but you need to plan how the window opens for air and whether the screen is fixed or removable. A fixed security screen is the easier case, because the cat can be on the glass with the window open and nothing else moves. Removable flyscreens are the trap: people take the screen out for airflow and forget that a mounted perch now sits between a cat and an open window. Check that the screen latches, that the window has a restrictor, or that the perch position lets you keep the window closed to a safe opening. It is a two-minute check that prevents the one thing nobody wants to think about.

Heat, sun fade and west-facing glass

Two problems arrive with an Australian summer. The first is heat: a perch on west-facing glass in January gets hot enough to soften suction rubber, and a cup that creeps is a cup that lets go. If the window takes the afternoon sun, either choose a mount that does not rely on suction, or plan to re-seat the cups monthly through summer. The second is fade: a fabric pad or hammock left in direct sun all season will fade noticeably faster than the same fabric in a shaded room, and a faded pad is what makes an otherwise tidy perch look tired. Choose removable, washable covers in a colour that fades gracefully, and rotate or store the pad during the hottest months if the window is a full-sun one.

The order I would actually buy in

Measure the glass, note the frame depth, identify every layer of window furniture, and decide where the perch stops and the curtain or blind starts. Then pick a mount that suits the surface you actually have, not the surface in the product photos. Then, and only then, think about the pad, the colour and the styling. Most people do this in reverse and end up with a carefully styled perch that fights their blinds. If the window turns out to be unsuitable, a freestanding tower beside the glass costs more floor space and delivers most of the same benefit, and our cat window perch range includes the mount styles worth comparing before you commit to drilling-free suction on a window that was never going to suit it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a window perch if I have sheers across the whole window?

Yes, but not on the glass. Either choose a perch that mounts on the frame above the sheer and lets the fabric hang behind it, or move to a freestanding tower beside the window. Mounting on the glass usually means the sheer drapes over the cat, which looks unresolved and damages the fabric over time.

Do I need to remove a flyscreen to fit a perch?

Usually not, because a suction mount sits on the glass on the room side of the screen. The thing to plan is airflow: if you open that window for summer air, make sure the screen stays latched or the window has a restrictor, so an open window and a perched cat never line up with an unsecured gap.

Will a perch damage curtains or blinds?

A mounted perch does not touch them, but daily friction does cause wear: a cat squeezing past a sheer, or a platform pressing a slat run out of line. Choose a window where the perch and the covering occupy different parts of the glass, and the two stop interacting entirely. That single decision prevents most of the damage people report.

Is a west-facing window too hot for a suction perch in summer?

It is the hardest case for suction. Afternoon sun heats the glass, the air pocket inside the cup expands and the seal creeps. If a west window is your cat's only option, re-seat the cups monthly through summer, clean the glass properly each time, and consider a frame-mounted or freestanding alternative for the hottest months.

How do I keep the perch pad from fading in the sun?

Treat the pad as a cover rather than part of the perch: choose a removable, washable one in a colour that fades gracefully, and consider storing it during the peak-sun weeks if the window gets full afternoon light. Neutral and mid-tone covers age into a soft look, while bright prints go patchy and make the whole window look neglected.
