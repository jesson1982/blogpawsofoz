A Year of Toys: What Miso and Wally Actually Reached For

By Sophie BennettSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys5 min read

Short answer: most of what I bought for Miso the kitten and Wally the Cavoodle sat ignored within a fortnight. After twelve months and roughly thirty purchases, only about a dozen things became genuine favourites, and the pattern was clear. Cheap crinkle toys and a wobble feeder did the heavy lifting, while anything battery powered or oddly scented barely got a look in. If you are staring at a exploding toy drawer wondering where the money went, the audit below is the honest version of what earned its place.

How I ran the year-long toy audit

I kept every receipt and, once a month, tipped the lot onto the lounge room floor. Whatever was still getting played with stayed; whatever had been ignored for two months straight went to the op shop or the bin. It felt ruthless at first, but it saved me a fortune by month nine.

The only rule was honesty. A toy that Miso batted once during the photo did not count as a win. I wanted things that survived the boredom curve of everyday apartment life.

  • Toys played with at least weekly for a month stayed on the shelf.
  • Toys ignored for two months straight were culled.
  • Anything that fell apart or smelled off went straight in the bin.

What Miso the kitten actually reached for

Miso is a rescue kitten with a short attention span and a love of small, moveable things. Her winners were a felt mouse, a ribbon wand I dragged along the skirting board, and a cardboard scratch pad with a dangly bit. The catnip banana I was so proud of? Ignored after day three.

The surprise hit was a $4 crinkle ball from the supermarket. She still chases it under the fridge at least twice a week, a year on. Expensive does not mean played with.

What Wally the Cavoodle actually reached for

Wally is a soft Cavoodle who chews gently and carries toys like a security blanket. A rubber wobble feeder, a rope knot, and a flat plush he can squeak-and-cuddle both landed in the keeper pile. The loud electronic fetch ball I bought on a whim lasted one afternoon before he lost interest.

His clear favourite is a lick mat smeared with yoghurt on hot evenings. It is not a toy in the traditional sense, but it keeps him busy longer than any squeaky thing I have bought.

The things that flopped, and why

Battery toys frightened Wally and bored Miso. A puzzle globe marketed for cats confused Miso so much she gave up and ate around it. Scented toys lost their smell within weeks, which told me the novelty was the smell, not the play.

I also learned that anything too big for a small apartment gets tripped over and resented. Space beats spectacle when you rent.

What this cost me, and what I'd buy again

Across the year I spent roughly three hundred dollars on toys that did not work out, plus about two hundred on the keepers. The maths stung, but the repeat buys are all cheap and simple. A wand, a crinkle ball, a wobble feeder and a lick mat would cover both pets happily.

If I could restart, I would buy three good things and a rotation plan instead of a trolley of possibilities. You can see how a simple rotation keeps interest up in our toy category.

The audit also changed how I store toys. A clear tub under the laundry bench keeps the rotation dry through a Perth summer, and pulling it out feels like a small re opening night each fortnight. I started photographing the favourites so I could spot a slow fade in interest before it became a bored cat scratching the couch.

One thing the year made obvious was that Miso and Wally play on completely different clocks. She wants three short bursts, he wants one long settle with a lick mat. Buying for both from one basket meant accepting that some toys serve only one of them, and that is fine. The audit is not about equal shares, it is about honest use.

If you are starting your own, begin with three things and watch for a month before adding more. The temptation is always a bigger trolley, but the pets do not notice the quantity, they notice what is in front of them today. A smaller, honest shelf beats a stuffed drawer nobody sorts.

The other lesson was about gifts. Well meaning friends buy novelty toys that flop, and that is okay, they become donation stock. The keeper pile is what I rebuy, and it is refreshingly short once the noise is stripped away.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many toys does a cat and dog actually need?

In my experience, about six to eight good ones beat thirty average ones. Miso and Wally share a small shelf of favourites and rotate through them. Fewer, well chosen toys get played with more often than a drawer nobody sorts through.

Should I throw out toys my pet stopped using?

Not immediately. Pets cycle through interests, so a two week break is normal. If something has been ignored for two months and is intact, store it and retry later. If it is broken, smelly or unsafe, bin it rather than risk a swallowed part.

Why did my expensive toy flop?

Price rarely predicts play. Battery and electronic toys can frighten noise shy pets, and scented toys fade fast. Simple textures, movement you control, and food based toys tend to win because the pet, not the gadget, drives the fun.

How do I audit toys without wasting money?

Buy one or two of a type, watch for a month, then commit. Keep receipts and avoid bulk sets of untested shapes. A monthly floor tip-out audit takes five minutes and shows you exactly what is earning its place.

Can kittens and dogs share toys?

Sometimes, with care. Supervised, size appropriate toys like a wand or a snuffle mat can be shared, but chew strength differs hugely. A Cavoodle toy may be too tough for a kitten, and small parts are a choking risk. Match the toy to the pet, not the household.
