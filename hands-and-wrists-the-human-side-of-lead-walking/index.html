Hands and Wrists: The Human Side of Lead Walking

By Hannah WrightSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear8 min read

Short answer: Most lead strain lands on the hand, wrist and forearm, and it comes from three things: a closed fist around a thin handle, a lead held at an angle across the body, and a dog who hits the end of it. A wider handle, a looser grip and a lead held on the dog's side take most of that load off.

Last winter a woman came in for a routine check and spent most of the appointment talking about her wrist. She had a forty kilogram bull arab cross who was lovely on the lead until a cat appeared, and she had been walking him with the loop wrapped twice around her hand for two years. The brace on her wrist was new. I am not the person to assess a human wrist and I told her so, but I could see the handling problem from across the room. Most of what makes lead walking hard on the hands is fixable with gear and a few habits.

Where the strain actually lands

The lead is a lever, and the load arrives at the smallest joint in the chain. When a dog lunges, force runs from the clip, up the lead, into the handle and then into the fingers, wrist and forearm. The further your hand sits from the line of the lead, the more of that force your wrist absorbs.

Three things make it worse. A thin handle digging into the fingers makes you grip harder. A closed fist with the thumb hooked over the top means the wrist cannot move with the dog. And a lead held across the body, right hand walking a dog on the left, puts the wrist at an angle it was not built to resist.

None of this is about strength. A relaxed hand on a wide handle absorbs more than a tense fist on a cord.

The grip most people default to

Watch ten people walk past a cafe and you will see the same grip nine times: the loop over the wrist, the lead across the palm, the thumb hooked over the top. It feels secure, and it is the grip that hurts most after an hour.

The loop over the wrist is the first thing I would drop. If the dog bolts and your hand is threaded through, the loop tightens and you cannot let go when you need to. Holding the handle in the hand, thumb alongside rather than hooked over, gives you a grip you can release.

The other default is the death grip. A dog pulls, the hand clenches, the forearm tightens, and by the end of the walk the whole arm is tired. A hand that opens and closes as the lead slackens is doing less work, not more.

Leads that make the hand work harder

Some designs put more load on the hand, and it is not always the expensive ones that are kind.

A flat nylon lead with a thin loop is hardest on fingers, because nothing spreads the pressure. A rope with a padded handle is more comfortable but heavier. Leather with a wide stitched handle is what I would pick for a strong dog, because it keeps its shape and does not compress into a cord under load. Bungee sections soften a jolt but can teach a dog to lean into the give.

Hands free belts move the load to the hips, which suits some walkers and not others, especially anyone with a sore back. Retractable handles ask for a fixed fist and a thumb on a button, which is a lot of tension for an hour. If you are replacing one, the dog leads range divides along those lines anyway.

  • Carry the loop in your hand rather than over your wrist so you can let go.
  • Walk the dog on the same side as the hand holding the lead.
  • Choose a wide, padded handle for a dog over about twenty five kilograms.
  • Let the lead slacken between pulls instead of holding one fixed grip.
  • Check the clip and the stitching before every walk, not once a season.

Wrist position and the long walk home

A neutral wrist, roughly in line with the forearm, is the position that lasts. It happens naturally when the lead runs straight from your hand down to a dog walking at your side.

It stops happening when the dog is ahead and to one side, or when you are holding a phone, a bag or a second lead in the same hand. The wrist bends, the forearm rotates, and the small muscles that hold the position tire first. That is usually the ache people describe at the end of a walk.

The fix is boring. Shorten the lead so the dog is beside you rather than two metres ahead. Change hands when one gets tired. Put the phone away for the first ten minutes, when most of the pulling happens.

Two hands, one dog, and when to switch

Two hands on one lead sounds safer and often is not. It locks the shoulders, shortens your reach and makes it harder to move with the dog.

Use one hand as the default and bring the second in when you need to hold something steady, at a road crossing or when a dog is reacting to something across the street. Take the slack with the second hand rather than gripping further down, so the lead stays straight.

Switching hands is worth practising at home, on a quiet footpath, before you need it. A dog who has only been walked on the left will lean and cross when you change sides, and ten minutes on a familiar street sorts most of that out.

Small changes that take load off the hand

None of this needs new gear to start. Shorten the lead. Hold it in the hand rather than on the wrist. Keep the dog on the same side. Move your hand with the dog instead of bracing against the pull, letting the arm swing rather than locking the elbow.

Then look at the dog's end of the lead. A dog who pulls constantly will wear out any hand, and the gear that helps most is often a front attach harness rather than a different lead. That is training territory, worth one session with someone who can watch you both walk.

If your hand, wrist or shoulder is sore for more than a day or two after a walk, that is a question for your GP or physio. I can tell you what a lead does to a hand under load. I cannot tell you what is happening inside yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a padded handle worth the extra money?

For a dog over about twenty kilograms, usually yes. Padding spreads pressure across the palm instead of concentrating it on a cord, and a handle that holds its shape is easier to release. On a small dog, a plain flat lead is fine. Width matters more than padding.

Should I wrap the lead around my wrist for security?

I would not. A loop around the wrist tightens under load, puts the pull across the joint, and stops you letting go in a hurry. Holding the handle in a closed hand with the thumb alongside is secure enough, and kinder to the wrist over an hour of walking.

Does a bungee lead help with a dog that lunges?

It softens the first jolt, which your arm will notice. It also stretches, and a dog can learn to lean into that give, which means more sustained tension. Some walkers like them, some find them vague. Try one on a quiet walk before you commit to it.

Which hand should hold the lead?

The hand on the same side as the dog, so the lead runs straight down rather than across your body. If the dog walks on your left, that is your left hand. For most people the real answer is both, alternating, so neither arm takes the whole walk every day.

Can I use a waist belt with a dog that pulls?

A belt moves the load to your hips and lower back, which is better for the hands and worse for the spine if the dog hits the end hard. I would only use one with a dog who walks on a loose lead most of the time, and keep a hand on the lead as well. Ask your physio if you have a back problem.
