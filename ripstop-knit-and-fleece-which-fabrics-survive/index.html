Ripstop, Knit and Fleece: Which Fabrics Survive a Dog

By Jess AllenSep 3, 2026Pet Apparel7 min read

Short answer: ripstop nylon handles claws and scrub best, a tight polyester knit is the most comfortable under a harness, and fleece is warm but collects burrs and pills. Most dog clothing fails at the seams and the velcro rather than the main panel, so pay for construction before you pay for a fancy face fabric.

Every spring I sort a tub of returned dog coats on the shop counter and lay them out by failure rather than by brand. A fleece jumper with the pile matted into one solid patch. A ripstop shell with a 4 cm tear that never grew. A knit shirt with a run down one side where a harness strap crossed it. Sorting them that way taught me more about fabric than any label did, because the label tells you what the panel is and the return tells you what happened after three months on a real dog in real weather.

How ripstop behaves against claws

Ripstop is a plain weave with a heavier yarn running every 5 to 8 mm in both directions, which gives it the faint grid you can see against a window. The grid does one thing very well. When a claw or a blackberry thorn starts a tear, the split runs to the next thick yarn and stops. I have watched a torn ripstop panel hold a 3 cm hole through a whole winter without it becoming a 20 cm one, and that is the argument for the fabric.

The trade-offs are noise and stiffness. A crisp nylon ripstop rustles against itself, and it does not stretch, so a coat cut from it has to be shaped properly around the shoulders or it binds as the dog reaches forward. On scrub and wire fence lines it is the panel I would choose first.

Knit fabrics and the stretch trade-off

A knit is loops rather than woven threads, which is why it moves. A tight polyester or nylon knit will stretch over a dog's shoulders, sit flat under a harness and come back into shape after a wash, and that makes it the best everyday choice for a dog that tolerates clothing. The weakness shows up wherever a strap crosses the fabric. Every rub point pills, and a pull in a knit can run the length of the panel like a ladder in a stocking.

Look at the weight before you buy. A light jersey at around 150 gsm will not last a season on an active dog, while a 250 to 300 gsm interlock with some body to it holds up far better and still moves. Knits also need a closer fit, because a loose knit catches on everything it passes.

Fleece and the burr problem

Fleece is a brushed knit, and the brushing is exactly what makes it warm, quiet and soft against an older dog's skin. It is also why fleece and Australian paddocks are a poor match. Burrs, grass seed and bidgee-widgee lock into the pile and will not shake out, and pulling them free takes fibres with them, so the patch goes bald. A fleece coat soaked in rain also becomes heavy and stays wet for hours, which makes it a dry cold fabric rather than a wet one.

Where fleece wins is in the car, in a crate, on a dog that sleeps on a cold floor and on short walks on a cold dry morning. If your dog works cover, choose a smooth woven outer and keep fleece for the layer underneath, where nothing can grab it.

Where the failures actually happen

When a dog coat comes back to the shop, the face fabric is usually intact. The failure is at a seam, a closure or a binding. Velcro is the worst offender. The hook side is aggressive and will eat the loops of any knit it touches, including the sleeve of your own jacket in the wash, and once the loops are gone the tab will not hold. Stitch density matters too, and a single row of stitching at a leg opening will give way long before the panel does.

I pull on every join before I hang a coat, and I look for bar tacks at strap anchors, bound edges rather than raw overlocked hems, and a zip with a guard behind it so hair cannot jam the pull. A cheap fabric with good construction outlasts an expensive fabric sewn badly, every time I have checked.

  • Ripstop resists a claw tear because the grid stops the split travelling.
  • A tight knit moves with the dog but pills where a harness strap sits.
  • Fleece is warm and quiet but holds burrs and water like a sponge.
  • Velcro hooks destroy knit loops, so check what each closure mates with.
  • Bar tacks and bound edges outlast any claim made about the face fabric.

Washing without wrecking the face fabric

Almost every coat failure I see after a winter began in the laundry. Wash at 30 degrees on a short cycle, close every velcro tab so the hooks cannot grab anything, and put a coated coat in a mesh bag. Skip fabric softener, because it coats the fibres and kills the wicking that makes a knit bearable under a harness. Air dry in shade, and hang fleece so it drains rather than lying in a puddle of its own water.

A tumble dryer will ruin the coating on a ripstop or softshell panel in one cycle and fuse fleece pills into hard knots. Fleece also sheds a lot of fibre, so wash it with other fleece rather than with your good jumpers.

Matching the fabric to the dog

Start with the dog, not the colour. A short coated, active dog in scrub country needs a smooth woven outer and very little else. A thin older dog that feels the cold indoors needs fleece or a fleece lined knit, cut close so it does not drag. A dog that wears a harness daily needs a knit or a softshell with a smooth face, because webbing rubbing on fleece will pill a patch through in about a month.

A dog that panics in clothing needs the quietest fabric you can find, which usually means knit or fleece, plus a week of short, food rewarded sessions before it ever goes outside in the thing. Frequently Asked Questions

Is ripstop too noisy for a nervous dog?

It can be, and the noise is worse when the coat is slightly too big and the fabric rubs against itself as the dog walks. Try the coat on indoors for two minutes before you cut the tags off. If the dog startles at every step, swap to a knit or a brushed fleece.

Can dog coats go in the dryer?

Not the coated ones, and I would not tumble fleece either. Heat breaks down the waterproof coating on a ripstop or softshell panel, and it welds fleece pills into hard lumps that cannot be brushed out. Line dry in shade instead, and hang the coat by its back rather than by a leg.

Why does fleece pill so quickly?

Pilling is loose fibre working its way to the surface and tangling, and it happens fastest where something rubs, which on a dog coat means under a harness, under a collar and between the back legs. A tighter, denser fleece pills less than a fluffy one. Shaving the pills off slows it but will not stop it.

Which fabric is best under a harness?

A smooth faced softshell or a tight knit, because both let the webbing slide instead of grabbing. Fleece is the worst choice here, since the webbing grips the pile and rubs a bald patch into it within weeks. If you need warmth under a harness, look for a woven outer with fleece only on the inside.

Does a coated fabric stay waterproof after washing?

A clean coating usually keeps working, but detergent residue and heat both shorten its life, and a coating that has started to crack at the folds will leak along those lines. Wash sparingly, rinse well, and accept that a coated panel is a two or three season item on a dog worn daily in the rain.
