Big Cat, Big Backpack: Finding a Carrier for My 12kg Maine Coon

By Ella TranJan 15, 2026Pet Carriers & Backpacks7 min read

Short answer: ignore the weight rating and measure the floor instead. Basil is 12kg and rated for most carriers on the shelf, but he could not turn around in any of them. You'll want roughly 45cm of usable length, not whatever number is printed on the box. A rigid base mattered just as much, because soft-sided carriers sag and drop the whole weight onto his spine.

There is a particular kind of panic you feel the first time you try to zip your cat into a carrier and realise the zip simply will not close. One of the big cats I work with, Basil, is a Maine Coon, and at his last vet weigh-in he tipped the scales at 12 kilograms. He is not fat, he is just a big, glorious floof of a cat. But that size turned what I thought would be a quick online purchase into a three-week rabbit hole of measurements, return labels, and a slowly growing pile of carriers in my hallway.

If you have ever searched for a carrier built for large cats, you will know the listings lie a little. They show a happy cat peeking out, but the fine print says "suitable for pets up to 5kg". My 12kg boy would have been a folded-up pretzel. So I want to share what I actually learned, the hard way, so your hallway does not end up like mine.

The day my old carrier finally gave up

Basil's original soft crate was a hand-me-down from a friend with a dainty tabby. For two years it limped along, but as he grew, the seams started to groan. On a trip to the vet last winter, I lifted the bag and felt the bottom stitching go. He dropped two inches and let out a noise I have never heard from him before. No one was hurt, but I promised him (and myself) that the next carrier would be chosen with math, not hope.

Why the weight limit is the first thing I check now

When you are shopping, the single number that matters most is the rated load. I needed a carrier rated for at least 12kg, and honestly I wanted a little headroom so the seams were not living at their breaking point. A carrier rated exactly at your cat's weight is a bad idea; fabrics stretch, zips wear, and a nervous cat shifts its weight around. I ruled out anything under 15kg rated capacity, which immediately deleted about 70% of what was for sale.

People forget that the weight limit is not just about the fabric holding. It is about the shoulder straps, the buckles, and your own back. A 12kg cat plus a 2kg bag is like carrying a small toddler on one shoulder. The harness system needs to spread that load, or you will regret it by the time you reach the car.

Interior length matters more than you'd think

Here is the thing nobody tells you: cats do not curl up neatly on command. Basil likes to lie flat with his tail stretched out, and a carrier that is tall but short simply does not work. When I finally found a proper Maine Coon model, the spec that sold me was interior length, not height. He needed roughly 45cm of lying-down space, and most "large" bags top out around 35cm.

I measured him asleep on the rug with a tape measure, because that is the kind of person this journey made me. Knowing his real stretched-out length meant I could ignore every listing photo and shop by numbers instead. If you are in Australia and your cat is a big breed, look for the interior dimensions in centimetres and measure your own pet first. The photos will always look bigger than reality.

A rigid base made all the difference

The breakthrough was a rigid, reinforced base. Soft-bottom bags sag under a heavy cat, and a sagging floor means your cat feels every step you take through the bag. With Basil's weight, that sag was uncomfortable for him and painful for my lower back. A large carrier built for the Australian market needs a hard insert or a structured baseboard so the load stays stable.

When I found one with a proper base, the change was immediate. He stopped shifting and crying on short walks. The rigid floor meant his weight was distributed instead of sloshing around, and I could actually stand up straight. It is one of those details you only appreciate once you have carried a sad, sliding cat for twenty minutes.

What I ended up with, and what I'd tell you

After all the returns, I settled on a structured backpack with a 15kg rating, a 47cm interior length, and a hard base. It is not the cheapest thing I own, but Basil rides in it calmly now, and the vet trips have gone from a wrestling match to a quiet walk out the door. If you are hunting for the best option for big cats, do not start with colour or style. Start with the three numbers: rated weight, interior length, and whether the base is rigid.

I also learned to read reviews from other big-breed owners specifically. A five-star review from someone with a 4kg kitten tells you nothing about your 12kg problem child. Search the comments for "Maine Coon", "ragdoll", or "large cat" and see what those people say. They are the only ones who have truly tested the limits.

One more thing worth mentioning: the shoulder padding. I almost skipped it as a comfort extra, but with this much weight it is a safety feature in disguise. Thin straps dug into me within minutes and made me walk crooked, which jostled Basil and made him fussy. Wide, padded straps and a chest buckle that pulls the load in close turned a miserable haul into something I can do for half an hour without complaining. If you are carrying a cat this size, treat the harness on your own body with as much care as the one inside the bag.

— Written by someone who has carried a 12kg cat more times than she'd like to admit. Basil says thanks (and demands treats).

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose a backpack for a large cat like a Maine Coon?

Start with three numbers: the rated weight, the interior length, and whether the base is rigid. A Maine Coon needs a carrier rated above his actual weight, with enough stretched-out length to turn around, and a hard floor so the load stays stable instead of sagging.

Why does interior length matter more than height?

Cats lie flat with their tail stretched out rather than standing tall, so a tall but short bag leaves them folded. Interior length is what lets a big cat turn, lie down, and settle. Most "large" bags are too short, so shop by the centimetre measurement, not the photo.

What weight rating should I look for?

Pick a carrier rated above your cat's actual weight, not exactly at it. Fabrics stretch and zips wear, so a 12kg cat should be in something rated at least 15kg. That headroom keeps the seams off their breaking point and protects your own back on the walk.

Why is a rigid base important for heavy cats?

A soft bottom sags under a heavy cat, so he feels every step and the floor presses on his spine. A reinforced base spreads his weight and keeps the bag stable, which calms the cat and saves your lower back from a miserable, sliding haul.

How do I check if a carrier will fit my big cat?

Measure your cat asleep, stretched from nose to tail base, then compare that to the interior length. Read reviews from other owners of the same breed, because a five-star review from a kitten owner tells you nothing about a 12kg problem child.
