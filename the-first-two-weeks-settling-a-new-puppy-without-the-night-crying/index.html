The First Two Weeks: Settling a New Puppy Without the Night Crying

By Nadia BrooksFeb 10, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

Short answer: most puppies stop crying within four nights, so ride out the first two weeks without reinforcing the noise, because staying calm teaches them crying brings no reward. I brought home an eight-week cavoodle called Pip and still sat on the laundry floor with my hand through the bars. She was quiet within four nights, and the collar waited until she was twelve weeks and the routine was set. You'll want the crate, the breeder's blanket and a fixed clock more than any product.

I've helped hundreds of families through this and I still got my own puppy's first night wrong. We brought home an eight-week-old cavoodle whose name, by the time she was ours, was Pip. I'd read everything, I had the crate, the clock, the blanket from the breeder, and a plan. At half past eleven she started crying. By one in the morning I was sitting on the laundry floor with my hand through the crate bars, which is precisely the thing I tell every client never to do.

She was quiet within four nights. Most puppies are, and knowing that would have saved me three sleepless nights of feeling like I was damaging her for life. I wasn't. I was just teaching her that crying brought me running, and I had to undo it over the following week.

The three-night pattern

Almost every puppy follows the same arc and nobody warns you about it. Night one they're too exhausted to do much. Night two is the worst, because the novelty has worn off and they've worked out that they're alone. Night three is noticeably better, and by night four or five most are sleeping through until five in the morning.

What matters is that you don't turn night two into a rehearsal for night thirty. When she cried, I waited for a gap of three or four seconds of quiet rather than going to her mid-wail. Then I'd say nothing at all, let her out for a completely boring toilet trip with no talking and no playing, and put her straight back. The boredom part is the whole technique, and it's much harder to do at two in the morning than it sounds.

What actually helped more than anything I bought

The blanket the breeder sent home, unwashed, smelling of her mother and littermates, went straight in the crate and mattered more than every product combined. A hot water bottle wrapped in a tea towel did the next most work, because she'd been sleeping in a pile of warm puppies and the sudden solitude is genuinely the strangest part of the whole experience for them.

Then the routine. Same dinner time, same toilet spot, same lights-out, same boring voice, for two solid weeks. Puppies settle when their world becomes predictable far faster than they settle because of anything you put on them. I moved the crate into our room for the first four nights and then walked it back out to the laundry over the following week, one room at a time.

Why we waited until twelve weeks for the collar

I get asked about calming collars for puppies constantly, and my answer is that age matters more than most people realise. Most manufacturers set a minimum age, commonly around twelve weeks, and I stuck to that even though I had a drawer full of them. A very young puppy chews everything, including the collar on their own neck, and their skin is more sensitive than an adult dog's.

We put one on at twelve weeks, ahead of her first vaccination visit and the start of socialisation outings, because that was when she actually needed support. I measured her neck with a soft tape, cut the length down so it sat properly on a puppy rather than hanging loose, and checked it daily for any redness. Two fingers of slack, checked every few days, because puppies grow at a genuinely absurd rate.

Socialisation, and the bit people get backwards

The other thing worth saying plainly: the goal in those first weeks is not to keep a puppy calm at all costs, it's to help them feel safe while they learn the world is fine. Those aren't the same thing. I wanted Pip a little bit brave, not sedate. We did short, positive exposures — five minutes in a car park watching traffic, a different surface every day, one new person at a time — and then came home to a quiet crate to process it.

A calming collar supports that cycle by helping her settle afterwards rather than by blunting her curiosity during it. If your puppy is hiding, refusing to eat, or showing fear that's getting worse rather than better over a week, that's a vet conversation, not a product one.

Pip is two now and sleeps on the end of my bed with her feet in the air, which is a long way from that first night on the laundry floor. If you're in the middle of night two right now: it passes, usually faster than you think, and the boring toilet trip is doing more work than any product on this page. — Nadia, Adelaide, currently being used as a footrest.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do puppies cry at night?

Most follow a three-night pattern: quiet on the first night from exhaustion, worst on the second, and noticeably better by the third or fourth. If crying is still going strong after a week, it's worth checking for a medical cause with your vet.

Should I go to my puppy when they cry?

Wait for three or four seconds of quiet rather than responding mid-cry, then take them out for a completely boring toilet trip with no talking or play, and put them straight back. Responding to the crying itself teaches them that noise brings company.

At what age can a puppy wear a calming collar?

Most products set a minimum age of around twelve weeks, and it's worth respecting that. Younger puppies chew everything and have more sensitive skin. Check the specific product label, and check the fit every few days because puppies grow quickly.

What helps most on the first night?

An unwashed blanket from the breeder that smells of their mother and littermates, a wrapped hot water bottle for warmth, the crate in your room for the first few nights, and a completely predictable routine. None of it is expensive and all of it works.

When should I worry?

If your puppy is refusing food, hiding continuously, has diarrhoea, or seems more fearful rather than less after a week, book a vet check. Sudden behaviour changes in a young puppy are worth ruling out medically before assuming it's settling nerves.
