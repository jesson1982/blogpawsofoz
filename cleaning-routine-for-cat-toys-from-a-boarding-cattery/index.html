The Cleaning Routine for Cat Toys, From a Boarding Cattery

By Ruth AldertonSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys7 min read

Short answer: keep three toys out, rotate weekly, and wash whatever comes back to the box before it goes in. That single rule spreads the wash load across the week and stops anything going stale, because cats avoid a toy that smells wrong long before a human notices. Fabric with no glued parts takes a hot machine wash in a laundry bag, while plastics and springs get warm soapy water and a full dry.

Nobody thinks about toy hygiene until they have kept thirty cats' worth of toys in one building for a summer. Then it becomes a system, because there is no alternative: toys that are not washed become unpleasant, toys that cannot be washed get thrown out, and toys that are washed badly fall apart. The routine I ended up with is simple, it is fast, and it costs less than replacing a couple of toys a month. Here it is, translated from a boarding operation to a home with however many cats you have.

Why toy hygiene matters more than people think

A cat toy collects fur, saliva and whatever the cat has walked through. That is normal and mostly harmless, but two things happen when it accumulates. First, the toy starts to smell, and cats are scent-driven creatures: a toy that has gone stale gets avoided, which quietly removes enrichment from your cat's day. Second, in a multi-cat house, scent on shared objects becomes part of the social negotiation, and a toy carrying an unfamiliar or stressed smell changes who plays and who withdraws. Washing toys is therefore not about visible dirt. It is about keeping the objects useful and keeping the household calm.

The rotation-and-wash system

In the cattery, every toy spent a set number of days in a room and then went through a cycle: out, washed, dried, boxed, and back in service a week later. At home the same loop works with far less effort. Keep three toys out, rotate weekly, and wash whatever comes back to the box before it goes in. That single rule means the wash load is spread across the week instead of becoming a Saturday chore, and it means nothing sits in a cupboard carrying old scent for months. The box itself gets a wipe out monthly, because fur and crumbs collect in the corners.

Fabric toys: what can actually take a hot wash

Fabric toys are the simplest and the most fragile at once. Anything with no glued components and no internal rattle can go through a hot machine wash in a laundry bag, and it comes out clean and usable. Anything glued, or with a bell or rattle sealed inside, should be hand-washed in warm water instead, because heat and agitation open glued seams. Feathers should not be washed at all; they go in the bin and get replaced. Dry everything outdoors in the shade if you can, since sun on plush fabric helps with freshness, and skip the dryer on anything rubbery or elastic, which degrades fast.

Plastic, springs and the disinfectant question

Hard toys get a warm wash with a mild dish soap, a rinse, and a full dry. I do not use heavy chemical products on anything a cat mouths, and the cattery never needed them: soap, warm water, thorough drying and rotation did the job. What matters more than the cleaning method is that everything dries completely before it goes back into service, because a toy put away damp is the one that comes out smelling wrong three days later. Springs and hard balls are the easiest category: a bowl of warm soapy water, a rinse, a towel and ten minutes in the air.

Shared toys in a multi-cat household

The higher the cat count, the shorter the wash interval should be. My own four cats get their rotating toys washed weekly rather than fortnightly, and anything that has been used by a cat who is unwell goes into the wash immediately and separately. That is a hygiene habit rather than a medical claim: smell and residue on shared objects affect behaviour in multi-cat houses, and it costs nothing to be tidy about it. If you have just added a cat to the household, wash everything before it goes back into circulation and let a fresh set of scents establish on a clean slate.

The wash kit I still keep at home

A mesh laundry bag, a mild dish soap, two microfibre cloths, a small bowl for hard toys, and a dedicated spot to air-dry in the shade. That is the whole kit, and it has outlasted every gadget I bought for the purpose. The routine takes fifteen minutes a week in a four-cat house, and the return is toys that stay interesting, a room that does not smell of stale fur, and less money spent replacing things that were fine until they were not. Boilerplate as it sounds, cleanliness and rotation are the two most effective things I know for getting real value out of cat equipment, and I learned both by having no choice.

  • A mesh laundry bag for fabric toys with no glued parts.
  • A mild dish soap, and no heavy chemical products on anything a cat mouths.
  • Two microfibre cloths and a small bowl for hard toys.
  • A dedicated spot to air dry in the shade.
  • Fifteen minutes a week in a four-cat house.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I wash my cat's toys?

Weekly if you run a rotation, monthly at minimum if you do not, and immediately for anything a cat has mouthed while unwell. A toy that stays out permanently and never gets washed will be avoided long before it looks dirty, because scent changes far earlier than appearance. A weekly wash of three active toys is a fifteen-minute job in most households.

Can I wash cat toys with my regular laundry?

Better not, mostly for the toys' sake: detergent residue and fabric softener leave a scent layer that makes toys smell unfamiliar, and softener coats plush fibres so they lose their texture. Wash them in a separate small load or a laundry bag with a mild soap, and skip softener entirely. The toys end up cleaner and more attractive to the cat, which is the point.

What should I use to disinfect cat toys?

Warm water and a mild dish soap, plus thorough drying, and that is genuinely enough for routine cleaning of cat toys. Avoid anything heavily perfumed or chemically aggressive on objects a cat repeatedly mouths, and never use a product not intended for pet-contact surfaces. Rotation matters more than any cleaning product: a clean toy that goes away and comes back is more useful than one that is washed but always present.

My cat's toy smells bad even after washing. What now?

Check whether it dried fully before storage, because a damp toy in a cupboard is the usual answer. If it still smells after a proper wash and dry, the fill or an internal component has absorbed something and the toy should be retired, especially anything with a rattle or glued layers. Fabric toys do reach an end of life, and replacing one is cheaper than persuading a cat to use something that smells wrong.

How do I keep toys clean in a household with several cats?

Shorter intervals and more rotation. My four-cat house washes the active toys weekly, keeps only three out at a time, and washes separately anything used by a cat who has been off colour. It also helps to have duplicate small toys so no shared item is in constant demand. The goal is simple: nothing stale, nothing contested, and nothing sitting in a cupboard carrying the wrong scent.
