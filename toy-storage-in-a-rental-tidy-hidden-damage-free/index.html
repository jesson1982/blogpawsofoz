Toy Storage in a Rental: Tidy, Hidden, Damage-Free

By Ellie NguyenSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys7 min read

Short answer: I store cat toys in two lidded baskets, one shallow tub under the bed and one soft cube on a bookshelf, so nothing is drilled, nothing drags across the floorboards and everything is off the floor when the agent walks through. The rule that makes it work is a hard capacity limit: if a toy does not fit in those containers, it leaves the flat.

Two cats in a one-bedroom flat means toys in places I did not put them. For about eight months I lived with a wand toy under the couch, a crinkle mat behind the television and three ping-pong balls I could hear rolling across the floorboards at 2am. The real problem was not the mess. It was that every storage fix I looked at wanted a drill, a hook or a wall I do not own. I ended up building the system around four containers that sit on the floor or inside furniture, and it has survived two inspections and one cat who can open anything with a loose lid.

Why storage is harder in a rental

A rental takes away most of the usual answers. No hooks in the wall, no shelving, no cabinet that I would have to leave behind at the end of the lease. Anything I bring in has to sit on top of existing furniture, slide under it, or live in a wardrobe. On top of that, my lease asks me not to fix anything to the walls, and the agent notes scuffs on the condition report. So the system had to be free-standing, quiet when it moves and light enough for me to shift alone when the floors get washed. The other constraint is the cats. A lid they can nose open is not storage, it is an afternoon of entertainment with a mess at the end of it.

The four containers that do all the work

One: a lidded fabric basket with a rigid frame, about 40cm across, in the bottom of the wardrobe, holding the soft toys and anything fleece. Two: a shallow plastic tub about 60cm by 40cm that slides under the bed, which takes the long wand toys, the laser pointer and the treat pouches. Three: a soft cube in a bookshelf opening, which looks like the rest of the shelf. Four: a small open tray that stays out on purpose with three or four toys in it, because a toy a cat can see is a toy a cat will play with. Everything else lives in the other three containers. I keep them in neutral colours, close to the tones of the furniture, so at inspection they read as homewares rather than pet gear. Anything that has earned a spot comes from the pet toy range I have tested in this flat.

Nothing that scratches the floor

Floorboards were the second problem. A basket dragged out from under a bed twice a day will dull and mark a timber floor over a lease, and that is a real repair cost at the end of it. I put adhesive felt pads on the base of anything I slide, and I lift the under-bed tub out rather than drag it. Anything with castors stays off timber entirely. Where a container sits in front of a wall, I check underneath it every so often, because one piece of grit under a hard plastic tub will scratch a path into the floor the first time it moves. A cheap rubber mat under the tub removes that problem completely.

  • Lidded baskets beat open boxes if you have a cat that investigates.
  • Felt pads on anything you slide protect timber floors.
  • Keep one small tray of toys out so play stays easy.
  • Choose containers that match the furniture so they disappear.
  • Set a capacity limit and let it decide what stays.

Choosing storage that hides in plain sight

The hiding part is mostly colour and shape. A grey fabric basket in an otherwise empty corner looks like something that belongs there. A bright plastic tub with a pet logo on it does not. I keep lids on, I use the same two or three tones across the flat, and I avoid anything taller than the furniture beside it. Cubby shelves are the easiest win, because a soft cube fills one opening and the rest of the shelf still holds books. The bottom of a wardrobe works the same way. If you have a linen cupboard with spare floor space, that is a spot most agents never open, as long as nothing is blocking the door.

The rule that keeps it manageable

Storage only works if it has a limit. Mine is volume: the wardrobe basket, the under-bed tub, the shelf cube and the tray. If a new toy will not fit, something else goes to a friend with a kitten or into the donation bin. That one rule has done more for this flat than any clever container, because it stops the slow creep that ends with toys in every room. The limit also keeps the daily reset quick. At the end of the day I gather whatever is on the floor, put the toys from the tray back into the tub and bring three or four different ones out. It takes about two minutes.

Making a system I actually keep

The system that survives is the boring one. Every container has a home, every home is a few steps from where the cats play, and nothing is stacked so deep that I have to move two things to reach one. That last point matters more than people expect. If getting a toy out means unpacking a tower of boxes, the toys are back on the floor within a week. I keep a small caddy of cleaning cloths beside the under-bed tub so wiping it out costs no extra trip. Once a month I empty everything onto the bed, check for broken bits and loose bells, and put back only what is intact.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do you do with toys your cats ignore?

They go into the under-bed tub for a month and come back out later, and if they are still ignored after that, they leave. Cats change what they want as they age, so a toy that failed in winter sometimes works in summer. Anything gets a second chance once, because a box of rejects is just clutter with a lid on it.

Can I use an over-door hook or a hanging organiser?

The fabric over-door sort is usually fine, because it sits on top of the door rather than being fixed to anything and it comes off in seconds. I avoid adhesive strips on painted surfaces and I check the top of the door for a mark when I take it down. A door is also somewhere a cat can reach, so keep the pockets above their stretch.

How many toys should be out at once?

Three or four, in one small tray, swapped every few days. That is a rule about my floor rather than about cat welfare. Anything more ends up spread across the flat within an hour and I stop noticing what is actually being used. The rest stay in the containers and come out on rotation. If a cat has stopped playing altogether, ask your vet.

What about toys that get wet or filthy?

I keep a separate small tub for anything that needs washing so it does not sit against the dry toys. Hard plastic and rubber get a warm water rinse and dry on a tea towel before they go back. Soft toys go in with a load of linen on a cool wash once the basket is full. Nothing goes into a container damp, because that is how a basket starts to smell.

Will my agent notice the containers?

Probably not if they look like furniture. My last two inspections did not mention the basket in the wardrobe, the tub under the bed or the cube on the shelf. What does get noticed is a trail of toys across the living room, a scuffed floor or a container pushed out into the middle of the room. Tidy storage is easy to overlook. Mess is not.
