The First Bed: What to Buy for a Puppy (and What to Expect It to Cost You)

By Tessa NgataApr 10, 2026Beds & Mats6 min read

Short answer: spend less on the first puppy bed, not more, because it will likely be chewed to pieces, and that is normal for the first months. I'll say the line sellers hate: teething runs three to six months, and I've nursed more than one pup through surgery after swallowing filling. You should buy a cheap, safe, washable mat you can bin, sized for the eight-week stage rather than the grown dog. Upgrading comes later, once the mouth settles and the body stops doubling.

I'll start with the sentence nobody selling pet beds wants printed: the first bed you buy a puppy will probably be destroyed, and that is normal. Puppies explore with their mouths, they chew more when they're overtired, and teething runs from roughly three to six months. I've nursed more than one puppy through surgery after swallowing a chunk of bed filling, and every single one of those owners said the same thing — they had no idea it could happen.

So this is the one article in this series where my advice is to spend less, not more.

Safety comes before comfort

Loose fibre fill is the real hazard. A puppy can tear a seam in under a minute, and swallowing stuffing can cause a bowel obstruction, which is a surgical emergency and genuinely life-threatening. If your puppy is a shredder — and most are — avoid beds with loose fill entirely for the first few months. A firm crate mat with a tightly bound edge, or a dense foam pad with a fitted cover, is far safer than a fluffy bed that looks lovely in the photos.

Then the small stuff: no dangling tags, no loose threads, no decorative buttons or pom-poms, and no exposed zip pulls they can worry at. Check the bed over daily rather than weekly. And supervise a puppy with any new bed for the first few days, the same way you would with a new toy.

What to actually buy at eight weeks

Something cheap, washable, and the size of the space you want them to sleep in — which is usually the inside of a crate or a pen. At this stage the bed is doing a job: it tells the puppy where sleep happens, and it gives you something you can throw in the machine at eleven at night without feeling bad about it.

A crate mat is the most useful first purchase for most families. It fits the sleeping area snugly, which discourages a puppy from soiling one corner and sleeping in the other, it washes easily, and it's cheap enough to replace. Save the nice bed for later.

Sizing for an animal that doubles in size

The temptation is to buy small so they feel secure, or to buy big so it lasts. Both have problems. Too big and a puppy will use one end as a toilet. Too small and they've outgrown it in six weeks.

Two sensible approaches. Either buy for the size they'll be in two or three months and use a divider in the crate in the meantime, so the sleeping space stays correct even as they grow. Or accept that the first bed is temporary, buy cheap for now, and replace it once teething settles. For most people the second option is less stressful and not much more expensive.

When to upgrade

Once adult teeth are through — usually somewhere between six and nine months depending on the breed — most dogs stop systematically destroying soft items. That's the point to buy something decent. By then you also know what they actually do: whether they curl or stretch, whether they dig before lying down, whether they chew when they're bored or not at all. Buying the good bed after you've watched them for six months is a much better bet than buying it on day one.

Some dogs never stop. If yours is one of them, that's not a training failure, and the answer is a durable bed and management rather than a more expensive one they'll also destroy.

If they swallow any of it

I want this to be the last thing you remember from this page. If you know or strongly suspect your puppy has eaten bed filling, fabric, or foam, call your vet straight away. Don't wait to see whether it passes. Vomiting, lethargy, a swollen or painful belly, straining without producing anything, or loss of appetite are all emergency signs, and the earlier this is caught the better the outcome by a wide margin.

Buy cheap, buy washable, check it daily, and upgrade when the teeth are done. The expensive bed is a reward for getting through the chewing months, not a solution to them. And if any of it ends up inside the puppy, phone the vet first and worry about the bed afterwards. — Tessa, Hobart, who has met too many puppies in the wrong kind of emergency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of bed is best for a puppy?

Something cheap, washable and firm for the first few months. A tightly bound crate mat that fits the sleeping space snugly is the safest and most practical choice. Save a soft, expensive bed until adult teeth are through around six to nine months.

How do I stop my puppy chewing their bed?

Mostly you don't, until teething passes. Use a durable low-value bed, supervise, redirect chewing onto appropriate items, and make sure they're getting enough sleep, since overtired puppies chew more. Avoid loose fibre fill that can be swallowed.

What size bed should I buy for a growing puppy?

Either buy for the size they'll be in two to three months and use a crate divider in the meantime, or buy cheap now and replace it once teething settles. A bed that's too large gives a puppy space to toilet in one corner.

Is swallowed bed filling dangerous?

Yes, it can be. Swallowed stuffing or fabric can cause a bowel obstruction, which is a surgical emergency. If you suspect your puppy has eaten any part of their bed, contact your vet immediately rather than waiting to see whether it passes.

When should I buy a proper bed?

Once adult teeth are through, usually between six and nine months. By then you'll also know how they sleep, whether they dig or stretch, and whether they chew when bored, which makes choosing a good bed far easier.
