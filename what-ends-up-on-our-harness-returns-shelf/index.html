What Ends Up on Our Returns Shelf, and What It Taught Me About Buying

By Nina CarusoSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses7 min read

Short answer: the harness returns I see most come from three habits, and price is not one of them. People buy from a breed chart instead of a tape measure, choose on colour before checking where the straps sit, and skip the fitting routine that keeps a good harness good. Measure the girth, count the clips if your dog is touch-sensitive, and give the harness three or four proper walks rather than a fortnight of hoping it settles.

Every pet store has a returns shelf, and ours is a research department I visit more than my manager would believe. A returned harness tells you exactly where the marketing stopped being true: which size chart misled, which clip failed, which design rubbed a dog raw behind the legs. I handle these conversations all day, so consider this the buyer's version of what I see: the five return reasons, in the order they show up, and the check that prevents each one.

Return reason one: the dog slipped out of it

Escape is our most common harness return and almost never the harness's fault alone. It splits into two causes: a harness that was too loose around the chest, and a dog who has learned to reverse out of anything by pulling backwards. The first is fixed with a tape measure. The second needs a different design, usually one with a third strap or an extended chest panel, because a determined backer-outer treats a two-strap harness as a challenge. The pre-purchase check: measure the girth properly, then look for the harness's stated adjustment range and see whether your dog sits comfortably inside it. Aim for the middle of the range, not the edge.

Return reason two: chafing behind the front legs

Rubbing is the second-most common return and the one I feel worst about, because it means the dog walked in discomfort for a week before someone worked out why. It appears in two places: tight behind the armpits, which is a size issue, and across the shoulder point, which is a shape issue. The tell is whether the harness moves when the dog walks. A sliding harness rubs by definition. The pre-purchase check: compare the chest strap's angle in the product photos to your dog's chest. Deep-chested, narrow dogs need a different geometry, and forcing a general design onto a greyhound shape is how you end up on my messages.

Return reason three: the dog hated having it put on

This one is not a defect, and I still refund it happily, because a harness that turns every walk into a wrestling match is a harness nobody will use. The pattern behind it is almost always step-in designs with a nervous or touch-sensitive dog, or a harness with clips that pull on leg fur. The pre-purchase check: count the clips. A harness that needs two clips and a lifted paw is harder than one that goes over the head and closes once. If your dog is already wary of handling, start with the simplest design you can find and let a trainer help with the routine rather than buying three harnesses in a fortnight, which is a pattern I recognise instantly in the order history.

Return reason four: chewed in the first week

We refund these less often, and I understand why customers find that unfair, so here is the honest version: webbing chewed through is not a manufacturing fault, and the chewing almost always happens in the first fortnight when the harness is new and interesting. The pre-purchase check is behavioural rather than dimensional: if your dog chews leads, collars or bedding, buy the cheapest harness that fits properly for the trial period and keep it out of reach when it is not on the dog. Webbing left on a floor overnight is the single most common cause of this return, and it is the easiest one to prevent.

Return reason five: bought two sizes, kept one

This is a healthy return, which is why I have put it last. People order the medium and the large, fit both in the loungeroom, keep one and send the other back. It works, and if your dog is between sizes it is genuinely the right strategy, with two caveats: check the return window before you order, and keep the rejected harness clean and off the floor so it comes back in resalable condition. The alternative, guessing, costs more in postage and disappointment than it saves, and it uses up my goodwill budget for the awkward cases.

What the shelf would tell you if it could talk

Most returns come from three habits: buying from breed charts instead of measurements, choosing by colour and price before checking strap geometry, and skipping the fitting routine that makes a good harness stay good. The fourth habit is quieter: not asking the seller anything. The team on the other end of the message has seen your dog's shape and your problem a hundred times this year. Ask before you order, describe your dog honestly, and the returns shelf stays empty, which is a result everybody wants and almost nobody thinks to ask for.

  • Choosing on colour and price before checking strap geometry.
  • Skipping the fitting routine that makes a good harness stay good.
  • Not asking the seller anything before the order goes out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do stores mind when customers order two sizes and return one?

Not when the return window and condition rules are respected, and for between-sized dogs it is the sensible move. What frustrates the team is not the second harness, it is the harness coming back muddy, hair-covered and past the window. Keep it clean, keep the tag, send it back inside the deadline, and nobody on my side of the counter will think twice.

Can I return a harness my dog has already worn outside?

Usually yes within the change-of-mind window, provided it is clean and undamaged, and most fit problems only appear once the dog is walking properly outside. Check the specific policy before ordering, because it varies between stores, and photograph the harness if you are worried about proving its condition. A quick photo of a clean harness on the day it arrived has settled more than one disagreement.

How long should I give a new harness before deciding?

Three or four proper walks across a week covers almost every fit issue we see: slipping, chafing and resistance all show themselves early. What does not help is leaving it on the dog for a fortnight hoping it improves. Harnesses do not break in the way shoes do. If it is rubbing on day two, it will still be rubbing on day twelve, and the sooner you tell us, the more options we have.

My harness clip came loose on a walk. Is that covered?

Contact the seller immediately and describe exactly what happened, including where and how the clip released, because hardware failures are taken seriously and are usually covered. Take a photo of the clip and the strap it was attached to. If it is a hardware defect rather than a fitting issue, that is a warranty conversation, and a clear description speeds up the replacement enormously.

What is the single most common mistake you see in harness orders?

Choosing the size from the dog's weight or breed rather than a tape measure, and it is not close. The second is ordering the wrong shape for a deep-chested dog, which produces a correct-size harness that still rubs. Both are fixable in a two-minute message before the order goes out, and both cost the customer a fortnight of walking in the wrong gear if they are not.
