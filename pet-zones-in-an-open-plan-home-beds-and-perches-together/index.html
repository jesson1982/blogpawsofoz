Pet Zones in an Open-Plan Home: Beds and Perches Together

By Sofia MarchettiSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: Treat the bed and the perch as one zone rather than two separate purchases. Anchor both to a fixed point such as a window wall, keep them clear of the walking line between doorways, and repeat one colour and one material across the pair so the room reads as styled rather than furnished by accident.

Last winter I laid out a New Farm apartment with a single open room about nine metres long. The dog bed had been sitting on the main run between the kitchen and the balcony doors, and the cat perch was on the only window, both of them stepped over daily. Nothing in that room was broken. The pieces simply had no relationship to each other or to the way people moved through the space. We moved the bed behind the sofa arm, put the perch on the window at the end of that same run, and repeated the cushion colour across both. Same furniture, different room, and the dog stopped following me into the kitchen.

Why open plan unsettles pets

Enclosed rooms give an animal three things without anyone planning for them: a boundary, a sightline and somewhere to retreat to. An open plan gives none of that. A bed placed in the middle of a long room sits in a corridor that was never meant to be a corridor, so the dog gets stepped over and the older cat stops using the floor altogether. The usual result is a pet who follows you from kitchen to lounge, not because they are needy but because there is nowhere that is clearly theirs. Styling an open room for pets is really about creating the cues that walls would normally provide. A rug edge, a change in flooring, the side of a sofa or the base of a window all do that job, as long as you use them deliberately rather than by accident.

Anchor the zone to a fixed point

Pick one fixed element and build the zone around it. A window wall is the best anchor in most Brisbane homes, because it already gives the cat height and the dog something to watch. A structural column, a joinery return, the end of a sofa or the outside corner of a kitchen island also work. The test is whether both pieces can touch or sit within a metre of that anchor without crowding it. When the bed and the perch share an anchor, the eye reads them as one arrangement rather than two objects parked in a room, and the animal gets a defined area with a boundary on two or three sides.

Anchors also keep a room flexible. If the sofa moves, the zone moves with it, which is worth planning for in a rental or a room you rearrange each summer. Decide the anchor before you buy anything and the sizes tend to sort themselves out.

Keep both pieces off the walking line

In an open room, the walking line is whatever path people actually take, and it is rarely the one you would draw on a plan. Leave about 900 mm clear along that path, then set the bed back so its front edge sits roughly 400 mm outside the run. Watch door swings, the arc of a dishwasher, the stool you pull out at the island and the line from the lounge to the back door. A bed that has to be stepped around gets moved, and a bed that gets moved gets abandoned. Cats need the same courtesy in the vertical plane: a perch above head height over a busy path is fine, while a low perch on the same run is a trip hazard and a hiding spot nobody enjoys.

  • Pick one anchor, usually a window wall, and build both pieces around it.
  • Leave 900 mm of clear path and set the bed 400 mm outside it.
  • Repeat one colour and one metal finish across the bed and perch.
  • Give the cat height over the traffic, not a low perch in it.
  • Live with the layout for a week before you buy anything new.

Make the pair read as one piece

Two pieces look intentional when they repeat something. Use the same metal finish on the bed frame and the perch brackets, or keep metal out of sight on both. Repeat the cushion fabric, or a close tonal match, so the high perch and the low bed connect across the room. Vary the scale rather than the colour: a low, wide bed beside a compact perch is easier on the eye than two pieces of similar bulk. If you are starting from scratch, work from a shortlist of pet beds that share a cover fabric, then choose the perch to match it. Keep the palette to two tones plus one texture and the zone holds together even when the toys are out.

Where the perch sits above the back of the sofa, treat it as part of the window composition and keep the sill clear. Where the bed is the lower piece, it should be the quieter one.

What goes under both pieces

The floor is the fastest way to pull a zone together, and it does most of the practical work as well. A flatweave rug or a washable cotton mat under the bed defines the area, catches hair and grit, and stops the bed sliding on tiles. In Brisbane, a breathable cotton or flatweave wool is cooler under a dog than a thick synthetic pile, and it can go through the machine at 30 degrees. Choose a size that extends at least 30 cm past the bed on the open sides so the area reads as a zone rather than a coaster. Avoid loose fringing anywhere near a perch, because cats will pull it. A rubber-backed mat in a tone close to the grout also keeps a tiled floor quiet.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far apart should the bed and the perch be?

Close enough to read as one area, far enough that the perch is not directly above the bed. Around a metre to a metre and a half works in most rooms. If the perch is directly overhead, hair, a knocked water bowl and the occasional misjudged jump all land on the bed, which nobody enjoys.

Can a dog bed and a cat perch share the same corner?

They can, and it often works well, provided the cat has an exit that does not pass the dog. Height is the advantage: a perch at least 1.2 metres up gives the cat a position out of the dog's reach. Give the cat a second route out of the corner, and separate the two for a while if the dog guards the space.

Should the bed go against a wall in an open plan room?

Where you can, yes. A wall gives the bed a boundary and makes it feel more like a den. If there is no free wall, use the back of a sofa or a joinery return instead. What you want is a solid edge on at least one side, with the open sides facing the room so the animal can see who is coming.

Does the perch need to be near the bed?

Not always, but in one large room it helps both the pets and the styling. Cats often prefer to be high and slightly apart from the dog, so a low bed on one side of the anchor and a perch up on the other is close to ideal. If the cat is avoiding the area entirely, move the perch first.

How do I stop the zone looking like a pet corner?

Limit it to one colour family, one metal finish and one repeated fabric, and keep the number of visible pieces low. Two items that match softly read as part of the room. Three or four mismatched pieces in bright colours read as a pet corner. Store toys elsewhere so the zone itself stays calm.
