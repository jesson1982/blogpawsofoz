The Three Weeks I Desensitised Our Cavoodle to Thunder

By Sophie BennettSep 1, 2026Calming & Anxiety7 min read

Short version: it took about three weeks to get Wally, our Cavoodle, from trembling under the bed to dozing through a Perth summer storm. The part that worked was gradual sound exposure paired with a predictable safe spot, not the pile of gadgets I'd stockpiled beforehand. A calming collar took the edge off but was never the whole answer on its own. The training itself cost nothing except about ten minutes a day and a steady supply of chicken. If you've got a noise-sensitive dog and storm season rolling in, start the prep now rather than the night the sky cracks open.

Why Wally fell to pieces at the first rumble

Wally is a six-kilo Cavoodle with the confidence of a much bigger dog, right up until the barometer drops. The first big storm we sat through together, he pressed himself against the bathroom tiles and would not move for the better part of an hour. It wasn't naughtiness. His heart was racing, his pupils were pinned, and he turned down roast chicken, which for Wally is the surest sign that something is properly wrong.

That told me the usual advice of just ignore it was not going to help. Noise fear tends to build rather than fade, and the longer it runs the more it spreads, so plenty of dogs that start with thunder end up startled by the bin truck or a car backfiring. I decided to treat it as training rather than a personality flaw, and that reframing mattered because it turned a helpless situation into a set of steps I could actually take.

What I tried first, and what it cost me

Before I understood any of this, I bought things. A herbal dropper that promised calm in a bottle cost about forty dollars and achieved nothing across a fortnight. A storm prediction app notified me about every cell within fifty kilometres, which mostly served to wind me up, and Wally read that tension straight off me within seconds.

I'm not against products on principle. I'm against buying three of them before doing the free bit. Sound desensitisation is the free bit, and it is the bit that actually moves the needle. The gadgets are the garnish, if they help at all. Looking back, the forty dollars would have been better spent on a decent mat and a pouch of chicken.

The three-week sound plan, step by step

I started with a free recording of thunder played so quietly Wally didn't react at all. We sat on the lounge, I tossed chicken for any calm glance, and the volume crept up only when he stayed relaxed. Over about three weeks the volume rose from barely audible to roughly normal TV level, always paired with something good to eat.

The rhythm mattered more than the volume. Two short sessions most days beat one long one, and we never pushed past a little worry. If his ears flicked or he stood up, that was the ceiling for the day, and we finished on a calm note. Most nights it was ten minutes, sometimes less, which is the kind of routine a renter with a full-time job can actually keep. For more ideas on the wider approach, our calming hub covers the same basics from a trainer's side.

Building the safe spot he'd choose

The second half of the plan was a spot he could retreat to that wasn't the bathroom tiles. I dragged his bed into the walk-in robe, hung a thick doona over the doorway, and left a radio on low talk so the sharp cracks from outside got blurred. He found it himself within a couple of days, which told me it was working.

The point was that the spot was his idea, not a shove. On storm nights now he heads there before I've even noticed the wind, and I leave him to it. A safe space a dog volunteers for is worth more than a crate he's been forced into, because the choice is the whole reassurance.

The collar that took the sharp edge off

Midway through the three weeks I added a pheromone collar, mostly because I'd already bought it and felt silly leaving it in the drawer. It didn't transform him, but on the first proper storm afterward he stayed in the robe instead of the bathroom, which for Wally was a genuine step. I'd call it a helper, not a fix.

If I were starting again I'd still buy it, but last, not first. The order that worked was sound practice, then the safe spot, then the collar as a top-up. Spend your energy on the two free things before you spend money, and you'll know whether the collar is doing anything at all.

What I'd tell a fellow Perth renter

The lesson that stuck with me is that storm season here is a four-month stretch from late spring, not a single bad night, so a one-week panic plan misses most of it. Start in October, keep sessions short, and let the dog set the pace. If your pet is suddenly fearful or off their food, that's a vet conversation first, not a training one.

  • Start sound practice at a volume your dog ignores, not notices.
  • Pair every session with food your dog loves, not just likes.
  • Build the safe spot in calm weather, well before the first storm.
  • Add a collar only after the free work is underway.
  • Finish every session on a calm note, never a worried one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it really take to desensitise a dog to thunder?

In our case it was roughly three weeks of short daily sessions before Wally stopped panicking at normal volume. Every dog is different, and a cavoodle who is only mildly noise-shy may shift faster, while a dog with years of fear behind them can need months. The honest answer is that you plan for weeks, not days, and you measure progress in calmer body language rather than a single perfect storm night.

Can a calming collar replace training?

No, and that was the clearest lesson from our three weeks. The collar took a small edge off once Wally already had a safe spot and some sound practice, but on its own it did nothing for the shaking. Treat it as a top-up you add after the free work, not a shortcut that saves you the ten minutes a day of actual desensitisation that does the heavy lifting.

What if my dog is scared of the recording itself?

Then the volume is too high, so drop it until they genuinely don't react, even if that means a phone speaker hidden under a cushion. The whole method relies on the dog staying under threshold, where they can eat and glance around calmly. If they won't take chicken, you've gone too far, and backing off a notch is the correct move rather than pushing through it.

Should I comfort my dog during a storm?

Comforting isn't the same as rewarding fear, so a calm hand or a quiet word won't make the phobia worse. The mistake is getting wound up yourself, because dogs read that instantly. Keep your own voice even, guide them to the safe spot if they'll follow, and let them choose where to be. Your steadiness is more reassuring than any product you could buy.

When should I skip the home plan and call a vet?

If the fear appeared suddenly, or comes with not eating, limping, hiding that won't lift, or any physical sign, book a check before you train. New anxiety in an older pet especially deserves a health look, because pain and illness often show up as fear first. Training is for known, gradual noise sensitivity, not for a symptom that might have a medical cause underneath it.
