The Second-Hand Harness: What to Check Before You Buy Used

By Nina CarusoSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses8 min read

Short answer: check the webbing behind the front D-ring, the stitching at every load point and the plastic adjusters before anything else. A used harness is worth buying when those three areas are sound, and worth leaving on the table when the stitching has pulled or the webbing has gone soft and furry.

A woman brought a harness into our Newcastle counter last month, bought secondhand at a market for fifteen dollars, and asked whether we could fit it. The webbing looked fine at a glance. Then I turned it over and saw the stitching at the front ring had been cut and re-sewn by hand, with thread that was already fraying. She had a strong dog and a lead that clipped straight to that ring. We did not sell her anything that day. I wrote down every check I ran on the harness, and they are the same ones I will walk you through here.

The three places used harnesses fail

Most used harnesses I inspect fail in the same three places, and none of them are the places a seller photographs. The first is the webbing directly behind the front D-ring, where the metal rubs the strap with every step the dog takes. The second is the stitching at the side seams and the adjuster loops, because thread takes the load before the webbing does. The third is the plastic hardware: side-release buckles, sliders and the small keeper loops that hold strap ends flat, which are usually the first thing to tear.

A seller will show you the padded chest panel and the colour. Ask for a photo of the inside, the back of the front ring and both buckles before any money changes hands.

Reading the webbing and the stitching

New webbing is firm, with an edge you can run a thumbnail along and feel it resist. Webbing that has done hard miles goes soft and slightly furry on the surface, and it folds too easily. If a 25mm strap collapses between two fingers without any pressure, it has lost its body and it will stretch under load. Hold it up to a window as well: light coming through the weave in patches means abrasion has thinned the fibres rather than just marked them.

Then look at the thread. Compare its colour with the webbing, because faded thread beside bright webbing usually means the harness has been washed hard. Loose loops, grit trapped along a seam and ends standing up all tell you the same thing, which is that the stitching has been working and is close to letting go.

Damage that is only cosmetic

Plenty of what looks bad on a used harness costs nothing. Faded dye, mud tide marks, scuffed metal, a missing brand tag and a chewed corner on a padded panel that carries no load are all cosmetic. None of them change how the harness holds a dog. Chewing on webbing is a different matter. A dog's teeth crush the weave even when the outside still looks closed, and a strap that has been mouthed repeatedly will be thinner through the middle than at the edges.

Bend it and look for a pale line where the fibres have broken. If the harness came from a household with a young dog that chews, ask directly what was chewed, then check that strap yourself rather than taking the answer at face value.

Hardware, clips and adjusters

Side-release buckles crack at the flex point of the tab, usually on the side that gets pressed. Close each one and press it with your thumb; a buckle that pops open under light pressure is finished, whatever it looks like. Check the D-rings for a groove worn where a clip has sat, because a groove will wear through the gate of a lead clip over months of walking. Aluminium rings are light and groove faster than steel, and corrosion around a rivet matters because the rivet is what holds the strap to the ring.

Adjusters should still grip. Slide the strap through and pull it firmly, and if it creeps back the harness will not hold its fit on a walk.

  • Run a thumbnail along the webbing behind the front ring for soft patches.
  • Ask for photos of the inside seam rather than the padded outside.
  • Press every plastic buckle shut and check that it stays shut.
  • Slide each adjuster and pull hard to see whether it holds.
  • Decide the most you will pay before you see the harness.

Trying it on before money changes hands

Fit is where a used harness earns or loses its money, and you cannot judge it from a photo. Measure your dog's girth behind the front legs with a soft tape, then set the harness to that measurement before it goes on. Once it is on, you should be able to slide two fingers under the chest strap but not a fist. The strap should sit behind the shoulder blade rather than in the armpit, and the front panel should sit low on the chest instead of across the throat.

Look at the adjusters next. If they are already at the end of their range, the harness is the wrong size for your dog, not a bargain. Watch the dog walk, turn and shake, and see whether the harness shifts. The dog harnesses we sell are fitted this way at the counter, and the method works the same in a car park.

When to walk away from a bargain

There are faults I will not argue about at any price. Hand re-sewn stitching at a load point, webbing that has been cut and melted back together, a buckle held shut with tape or a cable tie, or a missing adjuster that lets a strap run free. All of those are repairs to the parts that hold a dog, and none of them can be verified by eye at a market stall.

I also walk away when the seller cannot say why the harness is spare. Sometimes the honest answer is that the dog slipped it, and that is useful information about both the harness and the dog. If you cannot try it on the dog, and the seller will not take it back, the saving is not worth the risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a fair price for a used harness?

From what I see at markets and in buy-swap groups, a used harness changes hands for somewhere between a fifth and half of its new price. Ten to thirty dollars is common for basic nylon. Above half price I would rather buy new, because new comes with a returns window, a warranty and a second size to try.

Can I wash a second-hand harness before my dog wears it?

Yes, and I would. Hand wash in cool water with a mild soap, work the straps through your hands, rinse until the water runs clear and dry it in shade rather than full sun. Check the stitching once it is dry, because washing can lift a seam that was already failing. A padded panel needs an extra day to dry right through.

Does a faded harness mean the webbing is weak?

Not on its own. Dye fades before nylon or polyester loses strength, so a harness can look tired and still be sound. Judge the webbing by feel instead: soft, furry, thin patches or a strap that folds without resistance are the signs that matter. If a faded strap also feels limp behind the front ring, treat it as worn out.

The harness has no brand tag. Does that matter?

It matters for two reasons. Without a brand you cannot check the size chart, order a replacement buckle or find out what the harness was rated for. It also makes fit harder to judge, because you have no reference for where the straps should sit on a dog of your dog's build. Measure the harness flat and compare it with your dog's girth.

Is a used harness a sensible buy for a puppy?

Often it is, because puppies grow out of sizes quickly and a cheap second-hand harness covers the awkward months. Check the fit every fortnight and expect to change size at least once before the dog is fully grown. Do not buy one a long way too big in the hope it will last, because a loose harness rubs. Ask your vet about any coat or skin issue.
