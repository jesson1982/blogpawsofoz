How Often Owners Replace a Harness: A Replacement Cycle

By Marcus HaleSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses8 min read

Short answer: Most owners keep a harness for two to three years, and the gear is usually past its best long before that. A year or two of daily walking is a more honest service life. The end is rarely dramatic: a strap frays at an adjuster, the padding packs down, and the fit quietly stops being right.

I keep a strip of harness records for fitting questions: purchase date, the dog's weight at the time, size bought, and why the household came back. What surprised me was not the failure rate. It was how few owners could tell me when they had bought the harness the dog was wearing. Most guessed about a year. When I matched the records, a good share were closer to two years and some were past three, still walking daily on webbing that had gone soft. Nobody was being careless. A harness simply does not announce that it is finished, and a dog cannot say that the strap under its chest feels different this week.

The calendar nobody keeps

A harness is the only piece of walking kit most households buy once and then never think about again. The lead gets replaced when the handle frays, the collar when the tag wears through. The harness sits outside all of that because it is still doing its job in the only sense the owner can see: the dog goes out, the dog comes back, nobody has escaped. That is a low bar for a piece of load-bearing equipment. In the records I work with, the purchase date and the replacement date are usually two to three years apart, and most of that gap is the household not noticing. Owners are not replacing on a schedule. They are replacing after something has already gone wrong, and the file only shows the second date.

What actually ends a harness

The failures I see in return reasons are boring and predictable. Webbing fuzzes at the point where it folds through an adjuster, then thins, then starts to look furry along one edge. Padding compresses flat under the chest and stops spreading load, which shows up as rubbing rather than as a break. Buckles crack at the tongue, usually after being dropped on concrete or stood on in the car boot. Salt water and sand do quiet damage to metal hardware, and a harness left on a hot verandah or in a car loses strength in the webbing long before it looks worn. Weight change is the other common end.

Catastrophic failure, where a strap lets go mid-walk, is rare. What is common is a slow drift into a bad fit, which is harder to notice and harder to date.

The inspection gap

Almost every owner inspects a harness at the wrong moment, which is when it looks wrong. By then the household is already dealing with a rub mark or a dog that has started backing out of the car. A thirty-second check once a month catches most of it. Run a thumb along the webbing at each adjuster and feel for the thinning you cannot see. Look at the stitching where the D-ring attaches and where the front clip is sewn in. Squeeze the lining under the chest to see whether the padding still has height.

  • Feel the webbing at every adjuster; fuzzing means the fibres are already going.
  • Check stitching at the D-ring and front clip, not just the straps you can see.
  • Weigh the dog every few months; a kilo changes the fit more than owners expect.
  • Rinse salt water out and dry the harness in shade, never on a hot car dash.
  • Write the purchase month inside the strap so the next decision has a date.

Why cheap harnesses are not always the short-lived ones

Price and lifespan do not line up the way buyers assume. The twenty-five dollar flat webbing harness is often still sound after two years, because there is little to fail: one layer of webbing, two adjusters, a simple buckle. The ninety dollar padded vest can be the one that goes first, because thick foam compresses, the lining wears through at the armpit, and there are more seams to rub. What separates a harness that lasts from one that does not is usually the quality of the webbing and the stitching at the load points, not the amount of padding or the brand on the box. Cheap hardware is the real risk. A stamped buckle that bends under a hard lunge fails without warning.

Fit change is the hidden trigger

Weight is the trigger owners miss, and it moves both ways. A dog that slims down over summer ends up in a harness that now needs to be tightened past its adjustment range, which puts the joiners in the wrong place and the load on the wrong part of the chest. A dog that thickens up over winter ends up with a harness that will not close comfortably at all. Coat makes a difference too: a harness fitted over a full winter coat can be too loose once the dog is clipped. Puppies are the extreme case, which is why a first harness is almost always a short-term purchase. Measure the girth every few months rather than trusting the size on the tag, and treat any harness that is already at the end of its adjustment range as one size too small.

Keeping a replacement log

The fix for all of this is unglamorous. Write the month and year on the inside of the strap with a laundry marker, or photograph the harness on the day you buy it and let the phone hold the date. Note the dog's weight at the time. When the harness starts to feel loose or the dog starts to hesitate at the car, you have a number to work from instead of a feeling. If you replace, measure again before you go back to the dog harness range, because sizing drifts between brands and a dog's shape changes more than its weight suggests. Keep the old one as a spare for wet days. It has already done its service life, and a spare in the boot is why many households get an extra season out of the new one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a worn harness be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes. A saddler or repair service can restitch a D-ring or replace a buckle, and that is often worth doing on an expensive harness with sound webbing. It is not worth doing where the webbing itself has thinned or gone furry. If the damage sits at a load point, replace rather than repair.

How can I tell if the harness has become too small?

The clearest sign is adjustment. If every strap is near the end of its range, the harness is effectively a size down. After that, look for hair rubbed thin at the armpit or shoulder, and a chest strap that sits higher than it did when the harness was new. None of those are emergencies, but all of them mean the fit has moved.

Does washing a harness shorten its life?

Washing is fine, heat is not. A mild detergent in cool water, a soft brush on the lining, a thorough rinse and drying in shade will do far less damage than leaving salt and grit in the webbing. Hot water, a tumble dryer or a radiator will soften nylon and shrink foam.

Should I replace a harness after the dog chews it once?

It depends where the teeth went in. A nibbled edge on a non load-bearing strap is a cosmetic problem you can watch. Any damage within a few centimetres of a D-ring, a front clip, a buckle or a stitched join is a replacement, because that is where the load concentrates and a partial cut will keep propagating. When in doubt, replace it.

Do harnesses degrade if they are hardly used?

They do, just slowly and invisibly. Foam loses rebound, elastic goes slack, and webbing left in sunlight or in a humid garage loses strength without changing appearance much. A harness that has hung in a cupboard for four years is not the same harness it was when new. Check stitching and hardware before trusting it on a strong dog.
