How Long Does a Calming Collar Last? Storage, Water and Replacement

By Nadia BrooksFeb 10, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

A client rang me in February, mildly furious, because the collar she'd put on her dog in early December had apparently stopped working by mid-January — comfortably short of the sixty days on the packet. I asked where she kept the spares. In the glove box. That's the answer. A South Australian summer in a closed car is easily sixty degrees inside, and the collar had been quietly emptying itself in there before she ever opened the pouch.

The duration claim on these products is roughly honest, but it's measured from the moment you break the seal, and it assumes reasonable storage and ordinary use. Both of those are more fragile than most people realise.

What sixty days actually means

These collars are slow-release reservoirs. The blend sits inside a flexible material and moves off the surface steadily, warmed by body heat. Sixty days is a reasonable average for how long that reservoir lasts at normal temperatures with continuous wear.

Two things shorten it. Heat speeds up evaporation considerably, so a collar on a dog running along a beach in January will not last as long as one on a cat asleep indoors. And time starts at opening, not at fitting — a collar taken out of its pouch and left on the bench for a fortnight has already spent a fortnight.

How I store spares

Sealed, in the original pouch, in a cupboard, out of the sun. Not in the car, not in the laundry where the dryer runs, and not in a shed. It sounds fussy and it takes no effort at all. If you buy a multipack, this is the thing that determines whether the fourth collar still works when you get to it.

I also write the date I opened each one on the pouch with a marker, which takes four seconds and removes all guesswork about when it's due for replacement.

Water, baths and swimming

Most collars in this category are described as waterproof or water-resistant, and in practice that means rain, a quick creek crossing and the odd swim won't destroy them. Water resistance is about the material not falling apart; it doesn't make the release mechanism immune.

What I do is take it off for an actual bath, dry the dog thoroughly afterwards, and put it back on. Not because a twenty-minute soak ruins it, but because a wet collar sitting against a damp coat for hours is exactly how you get skin irritation under it, and that's a real problem rather than a theoretical one.

When to replace it

The honest test is your nose. When you can no longer smell anything on the collar, there's nothing left coming off it, and that's the time to change it. For most products that lands somewhere near the eight-week mark, which is close enough to the claim to be credible.

I also replace on a schedule rather than waiting, because I don't want to discover it's finished on the afternoon of a storm. Diary reminder every eight weeks from the date written on the pouch. It's the least interesting advice on this page and it's the reason the whole system works.

Puppies, kittens and minimum ages

Most products specify a minimum age of around twelve weeks, and it's worth respecting rather than treating as a suggestion. Young animals chew everything, their skin is more delicate, and they're still developing. For a puppy under that age, the things that actually help are free: a predictable routine, a worn t-shirt with littermate scent on it, boring toilet breaks at night, and handling practice with food.

Once they do reach the age, fit it properly and check it every few days, because puppies grow at a rate that will surprise you. A collar fitted perfectly at twelve weeks can be tight by fourteen.

And for cats, the same rules with one addition

Everything above applies to cats as well: sealed storage, out of the sun, replace when the scent goes, off for baths though you'll rarely need that. The addition is ventilation. A cat in a small, unventilated room with a strongly scented collar can end up over-exposed, so keep the space aired and watch for drooling, wobbling or hiding more than usual, which means it comes off immediately.

Cats also lack a liver enzyme most mammals use to clear many plant compounds, which is why the species statement on the packet matters more than the duration claim. A sixty-day dog collar is not a bargain on a cat if the formulation isn't appropriate.

Glove box, car, laundry shelf, direct sun — those are the four places a spare collar goes to die. Sealed in a cupboard, dated with a marker, replaced on a reminder. Boring, and it's the difference between a collar that works in February and one that doesn't. — Nadia, Adelaide, with a marker pen permanently in the kitchen.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a calming collar last?

Most release for around sixty days, counted from the moment you open the sealed pouch rather than from when you fit it. Heat and prolonged sun exposure shorten that considerably, as does leaving it out of the pouch unused.

How should I store spare collars?

Keep them sealed in the original pouch, in a cupboard, away from sunlight and heat. Never store them in a car, a laundry or a shed. Write the opening date on the pouch so you know when each one is due for replacement.

Can my dog swim or have a bath with it on?

Most are water-resistant enough for rain and swimming. Take it off for a proper bath, dry the coat thoroughly, and put it back on afterwards, since a damp collar sitting against the skin is a common cause of irritation.

How do I know when to replace it?

When you can no longer smell anything on it, it has finished. That usually lands near the eight-week mark. Set a reminder from the date you opened it rather than waiting to notice, so it doesn't run out during an event.

Can puppies and kittens wear a calming collar?

Most products set a minimum age of around twelve weeks, and it's worth respecting. Young animals chew everything and have more delicate skin. Check the fit every few days, because they grow quickly.
