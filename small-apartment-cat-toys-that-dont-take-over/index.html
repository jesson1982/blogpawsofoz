Small-Apartment Cat Toys That Don't Take Over the Lounge Room

By Ellie NguyenSep 5, 2026Cat & Dog Toys7 min read

Short answer: in a small flat a toy has to earn its floor space, so the winners are the ones that have a home when they are not in play. A wand on a hook, a laser in a drawer, small balls back in a box and a puzzle feeder that replaces the food bowl all pass the test, while a basket of soft toys does not. Three toys out and the rest in a lidded tub, rotated weekly, replaces the novelty that buying more toys is meant to solve.

Living in a one-bedroom flat with two cats taught me that the real question about a cat toy is not whether it is fun, it is where it lives when nobody is playing. Everything I own passes through a floor-space audit, and the toys that survive are the ones that either fold down, hang up, or keep the cats busy without a single square metre of loungeroom committed to them. This is that list, plus the things I bought, believed in, and eventually gave away.

The one-bedroom test: what survives a tiny floor plan

My rule is that any toy occupying permanent floor space has to be earning it daily. A scratching post earns it. A cat tree earns it, though only if it is tall rather than wide. A toy box does not earn it unless it is out of the way, and the basket of soft toys I had for a year was, on reflection, storage I was doing for toys nobody used. What survives instead: wand toys that hang on a hook, lasers that live in a drawer, small balls that go back in a box, and a puzzle feeder that sits where the food bowl already was. The trick is not owning fewer toys, it is owning the kind that have a home when they are not in play.

  • A scratching post earns its space, and a cat tree only if it is tall rather than wide.
  • Wand toys earn it if they hang on a hook rather than living in a cupboard.
  • Lasers and small balls earn it because they store in a drawer or a box.
  • A puzzle feeder earns it by replacing the food bowl rather than adding to it.
  • A basket of soft toys does not earn it.

Box toys and the packaging your cats will prefer

Every cat owner learns this eventually: the box is the toy. That is genuinely useful in a small flat, because a cardboard box can be flattened and stored, swapped out weekly for novelty, and recycled when it dies instead of being another object in the corner. My version is two boxes, one cut with holes, one open, rotated weekly, and I have never seen my cats lose interest in that rotation. It costs nothing, takes no storage, and beats three of the soft toys I once spent forty dollars on. If you want the version that lasts, a fabric tunnel is the closest thing to a box that folds flat when it is done.

Wand toys: storage is the whole design brief

A wand toy is the best value in cat play, and the storage problem is the only thing that keeps people from using them. I hang mine on a hook beside the bookcase, which I accept is an aesthetic compromise, and the alternative I tried first, keeping it in the cupboard, meant I stopped playing with the cats entirely. The lesson was that a toy out of sight is a toy unused. Buy a wand with replaceable heads so the wand itself stays and only the small parts need storing, and buy one with a wand that is long enough that your cat never accidentally grabs your hand instead of the toy.

Puzzle feeders in a small kitchen

Puzzle feeders replace the food bowl rather than adding to it, which makes them a free inclusion in a small space. Mine sits where the bowl sat, holds a meal, and buys me fifteen quiet minutes. The buying advice is short: get one that is easy to wash by hand and has no crevices you cannot see, because food residue in a plastic maze is the sort of thing you find out about three days later. For two cats, feed in separate rooms or use two feeders at separate ends of the flat, because a food puzzle is a resource and resources get guarded.

The rotation box, and what I stopped buying

Everything I own lives in one lidded tub the size of a shoebox. Three toys are out, the rest are in the tub, and I rotate weekly so the cats meet something they have not seen in a fortnight. Rotation replaces novelty, and novelty is what cats actually want; buying more toys is the expensive way to solve a storage problem. What I stopped buying: plush toys with catnip that end up flat and ignored, balls that roll under furniture I cannot reach behind, anything with feathers glued on that sheds onto the carpet, and any toy that needed batteries and made a noise I could not turn off at 5am. My cats are happy, my loungeroom is not a pet shop, and the tub fits under the bed, which is the whole achievement.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many cat toys does an indoor cat actually need?

Fewer than people buy, and more variety than a single toy provides. Mine do best with three active toys and a weekly rotation, which gives novelty without clutter. What matters more than quantity is interactive play: two ten-minute sessions a day with a wand does more for a flat-bound cat than a cupboard full of things it can bat around alone.

Are laser pointers bad for cats?

They are good exercise and a poor finish, because the hunt never ends and some cats get frustrated by that. Use the laser for the chase, then land it on a physical toy the cat can catch and bite, every time. Done that way it is one of the best small-space games going, and it is the only toy in my kit that needs zero floor space at all.

How do I store cat toys in a small flat without clutter?

One lidded container, sized to fit somewhere out of sight, and a rule that only three toys are out at once. The container enforces the limit; the rule enforces the rotation. Anything that does not fit in the container has to displace something, which turns every toy purchase into a real decision about space rather than another object in the corner.

My cats ignore every toy I buy. What am I doing wrong?

Usually the toy is fine and the timing is wrong. Cats hunt at dawn and dusk, so a wand session at 8pm will outperform the same toy at 2pm in nearly every household. Try the toy at the cat's active hours, move it like prey rather than waving it around, and put it away after five minutes. There is also a chance you have a cat who prefers boxes, paper and shadows, which is a perfectly good answer.

What about catnip toys — are they worth it in a small flat?

For cats who respond to catnip, yes, and they are small enough to store easily. The catch is that roughly a third of cats do not react to it at all, and responses fade with constant exposure. Keep the catnip toys in the rotation tub rather than leaving them out, and they will stay interesting for months. Silvervine is worth trying if catnip does nothing in your house.
