Skin marks, rubbing and coat wear: when harness fit is wrong

By Hannah WrightSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses6 min read

Plain answer: when a harness fits wrong, the first signs are usually coat wear and skin marks that trace the strap exactly, a thinning line of fur, a red groove, or bare skin where webbing rubbed. Those marks are a fit problem, not an allergy or itch. As a veterinary nurse I look for the pattern, because a line that matches the harness tells you more than the dog can. I am not diagnosing, but I can help you read what the skin is saying and know when to adjust at home versus book a vet. The shape of the mark is the clue.

How to read a strap mark

A harness rub leaves a tell-tale shape. The mark follows the strap, appears on both sides where a loop crosses, and shows up after walks rather than overnight. An itch or allergy tends to be patchy and not strap-shaped.

  • A red groove means the strap sat too tight or twisted against skin.
  • Thinning fur along a line means repeated low-grade rubbing over weeks.
  • Bare skin at the armpit means the strap pinched with every step.

Matching the mark to the strap is the fastest way to confirm the cause. If the line ends where the webbing ends, the gear did it, and the fix is fit, not medicine.

Coat wear versus a real sore

Coat wear is often cosmetic at first, a dull or rubbed patch that grows if nothing changes. A real sore is hotter, possibly weeping, and the dog may guard the spot. The difference matters for what you do next.

If it is coat wear with no redness, fixing the fit usually lets the fur grow back. If there is a broken surface or the dog is clearly sore, that moves beyond home adjustment and needs a vet's eye. The cost of guessing is a wound that takes weeks to heal, so when in doubt, ask.

What a wrong fit looks like

Most marks come from three fixable issues. A too-tight girth presses a groove; a slipped harness rotates the straps into the armpit; a twisted webbing concentrates force on one point.

All three are visible if you look before the walk. I tell owners to lift the strap and check the skin underneath at least twice a week, not just when the dog seems off. The dog will not tell you it is warm until it is sore, and by then the fur is already thin.

Fixing it at home first

If the skin is intact, the first move is to loosen or reroute the strap, then recheck with the two-finger rule. Untwist any rolled webbing, lift the chest piece off the windpipe, and give the spot a few days without pressure. Most coat wear fills back in within a fortnight once the rub stops.

Rotate to a second well-fitted harness if you have one, so the sore area gets air. Keep the skin clean and dry, and resist the urge to bandage a moving joint yourself. The goal is to remove the cause, because no cream fixes a strap that still rubs the same line.

Where the marks show up most

In my experience the armpit is the top offender, then the sternum where a low chest strap presses during a pull, then the shoulder where the top strap rides up. Deep-chested and short-coated dogs mark fastest because there is less cushion and more barrel to shift the straps.

Reading the location tells you which strap is wrong. An armpit line means clearance; a sternum line means the chest strap is too low; a shoulder line means the harness slipped back. Map the mark to the strap and the adjustment becomes obvious instead of trial and error.

Knowing the line for a vet

Supportive care at home is for intact skin and mild coat wear. Anything broken, weeping, spreading, or making the dog guard the area is a veterinarian's job. So is a mark that returns the moment you refit, because that points to a shape issue no amount of loosening solves.

I stay in my lane as a nurse: I read the skin and prevent the sore, but I do not treat it. If you cannot tell whether a mark is serious, the safe answer is always the consult. A thirty-second check keeps those consults rare, and acting early keeps a rub from becoming a wound. Most consults I flag are caught early because an owner did the thirty-second check and noticed the line before it broke the skin. The habit is the real treatment, and the vet is the backstop for the cases where it was already too late to manage at home.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are harness marks the same as allergies?

Usually no. A harness mark traces the strap exactly and appears after walks, while allergies tend to be patchy and not strap-shaped. Match the mark to the webbing: if the line ends where the strap ends, the gear caused it. Allergies and itches rarely follow a clean harness outline, so the shape of the mark is your best clue.

Will coat wear grow back after I fix the fit?

In most cases yes, if the skin is intact. Once the rub stops, a thinned or dull patch usually fills back in within a fortnight. The key is removing the cause, not dressing the spot, because a strap that still rubs the same line will keep the fur from returning. Rotate harnesses if you can, to give the area air.

How do I tell a rub from a real sore?

Coat wear is cosmetic at first, a rubbed or dull patch with no redness. A sore is hotter, possibly weeping, and the dog may guard it or limp. If the surface is broken or the dog is clearly uncomfortable, that is beyond home adjustment and needs a vet. Intact skin and mild wear can usually be managed by fixing the fit.

Which strap causes which mark?

An armpit line means poor clearance, a sternum line means the chest strap sits too low, and a shoulder line means the harness slipped back. Mapping the mark to the strap tells you the adjustment. A red groove anywhere usually means the strap was too tight or twisted. Read the location before you loosen anything at random.

Can I treat a harness rub at home?

For intact skin and mild coat wear, yes: loosen or reroute the strap, untwist webbing, and give the spot a few days without pressure. I am a vet nurse, so this is supportive care, not treatment. Anything broken, weeping, spreading, or making the dog guard the area should go to your veterinarian rather than be managed at home.
