Helping an Older Dog In and Out of the Car Without the Strain

By Hannah WrightSep 1, 2026Dog Car Seat Covers7 min read

Short answer: the kindest way to help an older dog in and out of the car is to lower the effort, not lift the dog. A sturdy step or ramp, a non-slip cover so they do not scramble, and a slow, predictable routine take the jolt out of every trip. None of this replaces your vet's advice about sore joints, but it removes the daily strain that tends to make arthritis flare. Think of it as support around the car rather than a treatment for the condition itself, and check with your vet before you change anything about how your dog moves.

Why the jump hurts more than it looks

An older dog carrying arthritis loads their front legs hard on a jump, and the landing shocks the elbows and shoulders. I see dogs who manage the footpath perfectly well but limp after a car trip, because the height of a sill is a real impact, repeated twice on every ride, and the body keeps the score even when the dog hides it.

The aim is to turn a forty centimetre leap into a gentle step. In my experience that single change often quiets the stiffness for the rest of the day, which is the outcome owners actually notice when they start using a step or ramp every time rather than only on the bad mornings.

It is also the jump down that owners forget. Going down loads the front end even harder than going up, and a dog who is keen to get out will launch before you are ready to catch them, so a ramp that works both ways is worth more than a step you can only use one direction.

A step or a ramp

A step is quick and cheap and suits a dog who still moves reasonably well. A ramp is kinder for a heavy or very stiff dog, because it removes the up-and-down entirely. Pick the one your dog will actually use, since the best aid is the one that comes out every single time rather than the one that lives in the cupboard.

  • Measure the sill height first and match the step or ramp to that number, not to the picture on the box.
  • Look for a wide, textured surface so paws do not slide, especially when it is wet after a walk.
  • Check that it folds small enough to live in the boot without a wrestling match.
  • Practise with it on the grass before you ever ask for it at the car.

If you have an SUV with a high sill, the ramp almost always wins. A step can end up being two awkward jumps instead of one clean one, and a stiff dog will resist a step that feels like more work than the leap it replaces, which is why the ramp tends to stick.

The non-slip cover matters too

A cover is not just for the seat. An older dog scrambling on a slick surface twists in a way that hurts, so a cover with grip or a rubber-backed mat under them gives four steady feet. I have watched a dog who fought the car relax once the floor stopped sliding under them, and that calm carries into the joints.

The side flaps help as well, because they turn the open seat into a contained space, so the dog is not staring at a drop on either side. For a dog who is unsure, that containment is the difference between a confident step in and a hesitant scrabble that loads the wrong leg at the wrong moment.

Building a calm routine

Dogs love a pattern, and an older dog loves it more. Same order every time: ramp out, wait, cue, treat, settle, clip. When the routine never changes, the worry drops, because the car stops being a surprise and becomes a known set of steps they can predict. Keep your voice even and the pace slow, and let them set the speed.

I tell owners to end on a good note. If the dog gets in but then fusses, do not slam the door and go, because that teaches the car is where the stress peaks. A chew on the cover once settled, then a quiet close, and the next trip starts from a calmer place than the last one finished.

When to ask your vet

If the stiffness after a trip is new, or the dog yelps, or they start refusing the car altogether, that is a vet conversation, not a ramp conversation. As a nurse I can suggest comfort around the car, but pain that changes behaviour is a sign something medical needs checking, and the sooner the better for an older dog.

This is support, not treatment. A step will not fix arthritis, but it will stop the daily jolt that makes it flare, and that is a real quality-of-life win you can hand back to your vet as part of the plan rather than instead of it. The two work together, not against each other.

Small fixes that add up

A low-profile mat at the sill, a grip sticker on a slick step, a treat kept in the door pocket so the cue is always there. None of these are grand, but together they turn a hard moment twice a day into a non-event, and for an older dog that consistency is the gentlest thing you can offer around the car.

Frequently Asked Questions

Step or ramp for an older dog?

A step suits a dog who still moves reasonably well, while a ramp is kinder for a heavy or very stiff dog because it removes the up-and-down. Match it to the sill height you measure, not the picture on the box, and pick the one your dog will actually use every time, because the best aid is the one that comes out of the boot.

How does the cover help an old dog?

A non-slip cover or rubber-backed mat stops the dog scrambling on a slick surface, which twists joints in a way that hurts. Side flaps also contain the space so the dog is not staring at a drop, giving four steady feet. For an unsure older dog that containment is the difference between a confident step in and a painful scrabble.

Is this a replacement for vet care?

No, and I want to be clear about that. This is support around the car, not treatment for arthritis or any condition. If stiffness after a trip is new, the dog yelps, or refuses the car, that is a vet conversation. A step stops the daily jolt that makes pain flare, and that works alongside your vet's plan rather than instead of it.

How do I teach the routine?

Same order every time: aid out, wait, cue, treat, settle, clip. Keep your voice even and let the dog set the speed, and end on a good note with a chew once settled. Practise on the grass first so the car is not the first place they meet the ramp, and the worry drops once the pattern becomes predictable.

What if my dog refuses the step?

Go back to grass and lure with a treat at paw height, then build slowly. A dog who refuses is usually unsure or sore, not stubborn, so check with your vet if it is new. Never force it, because a bad experience teaches the car is the stressful part, and you want the aid to feel like the easy way in, not a battle.
