Pen to Room: Widening a Foster's Freedom Without Setbacks

By Fiona EllerySep 5, 2026Pet Playpens7 min read

Short answer: widen freedom one room at a time, hold each new space for two full days before adding another, and go back a step the moment accidents, chewing or pacing cluster at the new boundary. The pen is where a foster learns the routine. The house is what you build once that routine holds without you standing there.

The foster who taught me to slow down was a two year old border collie cross who never put a foot wrong in the pen. After nine days I opened the hallway and the kitchen together, because she seemed ready and I was tired of stepping over panels. She was fine for four hours. Then she went through the laundry door, pulled a bag of dry food off the bench and spent the afternoon guarding it from me. Nothing terrible happened, but I undid nine days of quiet in one morning. I have widened freedom in the same order ever since.

What the pen is actually teaching

The pen is not a waiting room. It is where a foster learns four things that make the rest of the house work. Where the bed is, where the water is, when food arrives, and what happens at the back door. If you are still choosing panels, the playpens I keep going back to are worth a look before the foster arrives. If a foster cannot settle in the pen while I am in another room, they will not settle in the lounge room either, because the lounge room has more in it and I am further away. It is a rehearsal of the routine in a space small enough that very little can go wrong. When I rush that stage, I spend the next fortnight fixing it.

The order I open rooms in

I use the same sequence every time, working outwards from the pen, and I do not skip steps. First the pen with the door open and a short hallway just outside it, so the foster can step out and come back. Then that small room with the pen still standing inside it. Then the lounge room, the quietest space in my house. Then the bedrooms and the back hallway together, because they are the same kind of space. The yard is last, and it goes on a lead for the first week even though my fence is solid. Each step adds one kind of floor and one set of rules. Hallway tiles, lounge carpet, bedroom carpet, then grass. The foster only has to learn one new thing at a time.

Signals that a foster is ready for more

The signals I look for are boring. The foster goes back to the pen on their own when they are tired or want water. They toilet outside on the routine rather than on the floor. They settle within about five minutes of me leaving the room. They stop following me from room to room and start finding their own spot. There is no dashing when I step over the pen panel and no pacing along the boundary of whatever space they have. When four of those show up in a row and hold for two days, I open the next space. I am looking for a baseline that has lasted long enough to be a habit.

Signals to step back for a few days

Going backwards is not a failure, it is the job. I close a room again when accidents start turning up right at the new doorway, because that tells me the foster does not trust the boundary yet. I step back when chewing appears on door frames or skirting boards, when the foster starts hiding under furniture instead of settling in the open, and when they vocalise the moment I leave the room. I also step back after any big change in the house, a new foster arriving, the pen moving, or a run of wet Ballarat days with no walks. Two days in the smaller space usually does it. Those two days have saved me a fortnight of repairs more than once.

  • Open one space at a time and hold each for two full days.
  • Ready looks like a foster choosing the pen on their own when tired.
  • Accidents at the new doorway mean the boundary is not trusted yet.
  • Add the yard last, on a lead, even with a solid fence.
  • Step back for two days after any big change in the house.

Setting the next room up first

The room a foster walks into should already be boring. Before I open a space I walk it at knee height and take out everything I do not want tested. Bins, cords, shoes and the cat's food. I put a second water bowl in the new room and, in winter, a second mat. A foster who has to travel back through two rooms to find water will find somewhere closer instead, and it is usually the bath mat. I also leave one legal chew object in the new space so there is something to do in there that is not the furniture. Then I open it and sit in it for the first hour.

Widening with resident pets at home

If you already have a dog or a cat, this order still works, but every stage takes longer and the pen stays up for the whole first month. The new foster gets the pen, the resident animal keeps the run of the house, and they meet through the panels rather than face to face. When I start opening rooms, the resident animal is shut in another room for the first hour of each new space, then they share it once the foster has settled on their own. I also feed them at opposite ends of the new room from day one, so the room becomes normal rather than a place to guard. It adds about a week. It is worth every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to go from a pen to a whole house?

For most of my fosters it is between two and four weeks, and it depends on how much has changed for them lately. A dog who is sleeping through the night will move faster than one who is still watching the front door. I would rather add a week than repair a setback.

Can I skip the pen and just watch my foster closely?

You can, but watching closely only works while you are awake and at home. The pen does its best work when you are asleep or out, which is when a new foster learns that nothing bad happens in your house. Most of the setbacks I have cleaned up came from too much space on day one.

Why does my foster do fine for a week and then start having accidents again?

In my experience that is usually a change rather than a relapse. Something moved, someone visited, a routine shifted, or a new room opened a day early. I go back one stage for two days, put the routine back as it was, and it settles. If it does not, I ring my vet.

Should the pen stay up once my foster has the run of the house?

Mine stays up for a month, sometimes longer, and I keep using it for meals and for the half hour before I go out. It gives the foster one place that never changes while everything else does. Taking it down is the last step, not the first, and I do that after a fortnight of no accidents.

What if my foster hates the pen and cries in it?

Then I sit next to it rather than inside it, and I do not open the door while the noise is happening. I feed every meal in the pen and drop treats in there as I walk past. If a foster is still distressed after three days of that, I would talk to my vet and to the rescue group.
