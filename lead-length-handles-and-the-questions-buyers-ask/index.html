Lead Length, Handles and the Questions Buyers Actually Ask

By Nina CarusoSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear7 min read

Lead enquiries are the easiest part of my job and the part where people most often thank me for something simple. Unlike harnesses, a lead does not have to fit a body; it has to fit your walking habits, your dog's size and how much slack you want to manage in a given space. There are three numbers and about five questions, and once you have them sorted the whole shelf collapses into two or three sensible choices. Here is the version of that conversation I have most days.

The three numbers that decide which lead you need

Length, width and handle count. Length is the one people think about last and should think about first. A standard lead runs about 1.2 to 1.5 metres, which suits footpaths, streets and training walks where you want your dog within arm's reach. A long line, five to ten metres, is a training tool for recall and sniffing in an open park, and it is not a walking lead; using one on a footpath is how people end up with rope burns and tangled legs. Width should match the dog: a thin lead on a strong, lunging dog is unpleasant in the hand and hard to grip, and a thick lead on a five-kilo dog feels like carrying a tow rope.

The third number, handles, is the one that changes daily life. One handle means one grip position, which is fine for a calm dog on a footpath. Two handles, one at the end and one nearer the clip, give you a traffic grip for crossings and crowded areas, and they are the design I recommend most often to city walkers. Hands-free leads replace the handle with a waist belt and a bungee section, which is brilliant for running and awkward for anything where you need to bring a dog close quickly. Ask yourself what you do on the walk: cross roads, stand at a cafe, jog a lap, or all three.

Handle shapes and what they do to your wrist

A padded, closed loop is the default and the most comfortable for long walks. Rope leads with a knotted end are popular and can be genuinely uncomfortable under load; the knot digs in exactly where a dog pulling turns it into a lever. If your dog pulls, the answer is training and a harness, not a thicker lead, but a padded or neoprene-lined handle buys your hand a lot of forgiveness in the meantime. Retractable leads are the third category and the one I talk about most carefully, because the cord can cause nasty injuries if a dog takes off at speed and it is not built for a strong puller at all.

Hardware: clips, ropes and what fails first

Look at the clip before the colour. A trigger clip with a strong spring and a snug gate is the workhorse; the cheap versions have sloppy gates that open under a twist, and that is the single failure I hear about most. Bolt snaps are stronger but heavier and clumsier to operate, and they are my pick for larger dogs. Where the rope or webbing meets the clip and the handle, check the stitching or the splice: multi-pass stitching and proper splices survive, and a single line of stitching on a webbing lead is the second failure point. On rope leads, a spliced and whipped end beats a heat-sealed one, because heat seals stiffen and crack.

Matching a lead to your harness, and to your dog

Clip size should match the harness's attachment ring: a small clip on a heavy ring can slide and bind, and a big clip on a thin ring is heavy and awkward. Double-ended leads, which clip at both ends of a harness, are worth mentioning because they are the quiet hero for dogs who pull: they spread load across the chest and stop the sideways swing that a single attachment creates. Match the lead's weight to your dog's size, and match its length to where you actually walk, not to the widest-open space you visit twice a year.

What I tell a first-time buyer

Buy a standard 1.5-metre lead with a second traffic handle, a padded loop and a bolt snap if your dog is over about twenty kilos, then add a long line later if you are doing recall work. Skip retractables for a pulling dog and skip rope leads with knotted ends if you carry the lead for an hour at a time. Check the clip every walk, the stitching monthly, and retire the lead the moment either looks tired, because the cost of a replacement is trivial compared with a dog loose near a road. That is the whole conversation, and it takes about four minutes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a dog lead be for everyday walking?

Around 1.2 to 1.5 metres suits most suburban walking, which lets your dog sniff and wander a little while staying within easy control. If you walk in busy areas or cross a lot of roads, a two-handle lead at the longer end gives you a close grip for crossings without losing the sniffing length on quiet stretches. Longer than two metres on a footpath mostly creates tangles.

Are retractable leads a bad idea?

They are a poor fit for strong pullers, dogs who lunge, or anyone who needs instant control, because the cord pays out under tension and thin cord can cause serious friction injuries if it wraps a leg or hand. Used by an attentive owner on a small, calm dog in open space, they are workable. Looking at the returns and the injuries people describe to me, I steer most buyers towards a fixed lead with two handles instead.

Should I attach the lead to a collar or a harness?

For walking, a harness, and for anything involving a car, only a harness. A collar concentrates load on the neck, and a lead attached to a collar gives a determined puller very little to push against, which is why the walk feels like an argument. Use the collar for tags, and let the harness do the work, using a double-ended lead if you want the extra control and load spreading.

How do I stop the lead tangling around my legs?

Two practical fixes: use a shorter effective length when the dog is circling (the traffic handle makes this instant), and train a loose-lead position so the dog is walking beside rather than orbiting. In the meantime, hold the slack in a loop rather than trailing it, and avoid rope leads, which tangle more than webbing because of their round profile and stiffness. It is a training problem before it is a gear problem, but the gear makes the training easier.

When should I replace a lead?

When the stitching starts to fray, the clip's spring feels soft, the webbing has a worn spot near a clip or handle, or a rope lead's splice looks loose. Most leads give warning; the failures people describe to me are almost always visible a fortnight before they happen. Check the clip and the joins while you are standing at the front door, and a lead will last years on a medium dog.
