Boarding-Grade Beds: What Survives Thirty Cats and Industrial Washing

By Ruth AldertonSep 3, 2026Beds & Mats7 min read

Short answer: a bed survives hard use when the cover zips off, the zip is metal, and the fill is bonded polyester wadding. Sewn-in fills and loose fibre clump or take a whole bed out of service at the first accident, and fine plastic zips split at the turn. For a single household, one donut bed with a zip-off plush cover plus two spare covers is the combination that lasted eleven years of boarding.

Eleven years running a boarding cattery in Sydney teaches you things about cat beds that no amount of home use will. A cattery bed is asked to do what a home bed is asked to do, multiplied by thirty cats and divided by a washing machine that runs every day of peak season. Beds that looked lovely in a showroom lasted a fortnight. Beds that looked boring lasted three seasons. This is what the difference was, written for anyone whose household is rougher on gear than the average, whether that is three cats or one very determined one.

A cattery is a stress test, not a home

Start with the numbers, because they explain every recommendation that follows. A boarding bed gets sat on by cats who do not know each other, sprayed by at least one nervous arrival, and washed after every guest checks out, which in January meant the same bed going through the machine twice a week. Multiply that by a season and a bed is doing more work in three months than it would in five years in a loungeroom. Anything that survives that is, by definition, more bed than a single household needs. So the useful question is not which bed is best in a cattery; it is which design features caused survival, because those features are the ones worth paying for at home.

The wash cycle: what a bed meets in peak season

Every bed that came through my laundry met the same conditions: a hot wash with a mild detergent, no fabric softener, and a full dry either in the sun or in a drying room. Fabric softener, by the way, is the enemy of every plush bed on the market; it coats the fibres and cuts absorbency and softness over time, and it is the reason some owners find a bed goes flat after a few months of careful washing. What killed beds was not the washing itself but the drying: thick non-removable fills stayed damp in the middle for two days, and a damp bed in a warm room is a bed that smells, regardless of how clean it was when it went in.

Covers: zip quality is the whole story

In a cattery, a bed lives or dies by its cover. Two things mattered. First, the zip: plastic zips with fine teeth jammed and split, and metal zips with a decent puller lasted years. Second, and more important, whether the cover could come off at all, and whether it went back on while the bed was still slightly warm. I ended up banning every bed with a sewn-in fill, not because they were bad beds for a household but because at scale they meant removing a whole bed from circulation for two days instead of a cover for two hours. For a home, that translates simply: a zip-off cover is worth paying for, and a cover that is easy to replace is worth more than one that is merely pretty.

  • A metal zip with a decent puller, because fine plastic teeth jam and split.
  • A cover that comes completely off, so a bed is never out of service for two days.
  • A cover that goes back on while the bed is still slightly warm.
  • No sewn-in fill, which is the design that ends a bed's working life.

Fills and drying time

Three fills, in order of cattery survival. Bonded polyester wadding dried fastest and held shape well through repeated washing, and it was the backbone of my reusable stock. Memory foam held shape beautifully and dried slowly, which limited how many I could run. Loose fibre fill clumped at the second wash and was the single most common cause of a bed being retired mid-season. If you are buying for a home and washing monthly, memory foam is fine and comfortable, especially for older cats. If you are washing weekly, buy wadding and keep the foam for the one bed that does not need frequent cleaning.

Seams, bases and the beds I banned

Seams: double-stitched edges survived, single-stitched edges opened at the first stressed corner, and any bed with decorative trim lost it in a fortnight. Bases: non-slip bases mattered more than I expected, because a sliding bed in a strange room gets avoided by nervous cats, and a bed that slides is a bed that never gets claimed. What I banned outright: sewn-in fills, feather or down blends (drying time measured in days), beds with glued-on decorative elements, and anything with a loose inner cushion that cats could dig out and sleep on the floor with, which they invariably did.

What I would buy for a single household now

One donut bed with a zip-off plush cover and a bonded wadding fill, which handles monthly washing indefinitely. One flat mattress bed with a removable cover for the older cat in the house, because joint support at fifteen years is not a luxury. And two spare covers, which is the purchase nobody makes and everybody eventually wishes they had, because a bed without a cover is a bed that sits in the laundry waiting. That is three items and it covers a household through everything except the most determined sprayer, and for that one, keep a cheap washable mat in reserve rather than sacrificing the good bed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I wash a cat bed?

Fortnightly for a bed that is used daily, weekly for a multi-cat household or a bed near a litter tray, and immediately after any accident. Cats are scent-driven, and a bed that smells of old fur gets avoided long before a human nose notices anything at all. Washing the cover is quick; the mistake is waiting until the whole bed needs it.

Can I put a cat bed in the dryer?

Check the fill first. Wadding tolerates a low tumble dry, memory foam should air dry away from direct heat because heat degrades it, and plush covers do better dried in shade to stop the fibres matting. In practice, a warm dry day beats a dryer for everything I have owned, and it avoids the shrinking that turns a generously sized bed into a cat-sized doily.

Are memory foam beds worth it for cats?

For older cats and cats with joint stiffness, yes, and you will often see them choose it within a day. The cost is drying time and price, so treat it as the one premium bed in the house rather than the default. My boarding stock ran mostly on wadding, with two foam beds reserved for elderly guests, and those two were never empty.

My cat never uses the bed I bought. What went wrong?

Usually placement, often size, sometimes scent. Put the bed where your cat already sleeps rather than where you want it, make sure it is big enough to stretch in, and give it a few days to pick up the household's smell. A bed that slides on the floor is a third common answer; a non-slip base or a rug underneath fixes more non-users than any other single change.

How many beds does a two-cat household need?

Three, in practice, plus a spare cover. Two cats rarely share one bed comfortably, and cats like to change position through the day, so a third bed in a different room avoids the negotiation. It sounds excessive until you watch two cats who like each other decide they both want the sunny bed at 3pm, which is a daily event in most houses.
