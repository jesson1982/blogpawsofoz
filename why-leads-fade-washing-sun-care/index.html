Why Leads Fade: Washing, Sun and What Support Hears

By Nina CarusoSep 3, 2026Dog Leads & Walking Gear8 min read

Short answer: most lead fade comes from ultraviolet light, hot water and harsh detergent, not from the dog. Wash in cool water with a mild soap, rinse until the water runs clear, dry in shade and keep the swivel moving, and a lead holds its colour and its supple feel far longer than one left on a balcony rail.

In February a customer sent me a photo of a red lead that had hung over her balcony rail all summer. The side facing the sun was pale pink. The side against the wall was still red, which told us the dye was fine and the sun was not. She had also washed it twice in hot water with laundry detergent, trying to get the salt out, and the webbing had gone stiff enough to stand up on its own. We replaced the lead. Then I started asking customers at the counter what they did with theirs, and the same few habits came up again and again.

The three complaints that come back to us

Three complaints land on my desk more than any others about leads: the colour has gone, the webbing has gone stiff, and the swivel has stopped turning. Customers usually assume the lead was badly made. Most of the time the lead is doing what nylon and polyester do when they are left wet, hot and salty. The colour goes because dye sits on the outside of the fibre. The stiffness comes from salt crystals and detergent residue drying inside the weave. The swivel seizes because fine sand and salt work into the gap and stay there.

All three are slow, and all three are cheaper to prevent than to fix. Very little of what I hear is a manufacturing fault.

What sunlight does to dyed webbing

Dye sits in the outer layer of the fibre, so it takes the sunlight first. Ultraviolet light breaks the dye down and the colour lifts, usually on whichever side faces the sky. That is why a lead left hanging over a fence goes pale on one side and stays dark on the other, and why a lead kept in a car goes pale along the top of the coil. Nylon fades faster than polyester in my experience, and solution-dyed webbing holds colour noticeably longer because the pigment runs through the fibre rather than sitting on the surface.

Fading does not mean weakness. A pale lead can still be perfectly sound, which is why I test the feel of the webbing rather than the colour when a customer asks whether theirs is finished.

Detergent and the wrong bucket

The fastest way to dull a lead is a hot wash with household laundry detergent. Warm water opens the weave, detergent lifts the dye along with the dirt, and the rinse never quite gets all of it out. Fabric softener is worse: it coats the fibres, which makes the strap feel clammy and holds grit. Bleach will strip colour in one wash and weaken the weave at the same time.

Chlorine from a pool does similar damage over a season, and so does a bucket that has been used for cleaning products. Cool water, a small amount of mild soap and a thorough rinse will clean a lead without touching the colour. If the lead smells of dog, that is usually the padding or the handle, not the webbing.

Salt, sand and stiff webbing

Salt is the quiet one. Sea water dries inside the weave and leaves crystals that grind against the fibres every time the lead flexes. That is what turns a soft strap into something that creases sharply and feels brittle. Sand does the same to the swivel, working into the gap between the eye and the barrel until the joint is packed solid. A lead that goes to the beach weekly and never gets rinsed will feel rough along its edges within a season.

The fix is boring: fresh water before the salt dries, and a minute spent working the swivel under the tap. A drop of silicone spray on a stiff swivel helps, but oil attracts sand and makes the next problem worse.

  • Rinse the lead in fresh water after every beach walk, swivel included.
  • Wash in cool water with mild soap, never hot water or bleach.
  • Dry the lead in shade rather than over a fence in full sun.
  • Work the swivel under running water until it turns freely.
  • Store leads coiled on an indoor hook, not on the car dashboard.

A wash routine that keeps the colour

My routine takes about ten minutes of actual work. Fill a bucket with cool water and add a small squeeze of mild soap, the sort you would use on a dog's bedding. Soak the lead for ten minutes, then run the whole length through your hands, working the weave where the handle and the clip end get the most contact. Rinse under a tap until the water runs clear, which takes longer than people expect.

Squeeze the water out rather than wringing it, hang the lead in shade with the hardware at the bottom so water drains away from the stitching, and leave it a full day before it goes back in the cupboard. Check the stitching and the clip gate while it dries, since a wet lead is the easiest time to spot a seam starting to lift. Lead care questions land on the dog leads desk most weeks.

Storage between walks and in the car

Where a lead lives between walks decides how long the colour lasts. A hook inside a laundry or hallway keeps it out of the light and lets air move around it, which is all the drying most leads need. A fence rail, a balcony railing or a car dashboard does the opposite, holding the lead in sun and heat for hours at a time.

If you keep a spare lead in the boot, put it in a bag or a box rather than loose on the floor, because a boot in an Australian summer gets hot enough to soften plastic hardware and dry out coated webbing. Coil leads loosely around your hand and hang them; a tight coil around the clip puts a permanent bend in the strap and traps water in the weave.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I wash a dog lead?

Every few weeks is enough for a lead used on footpaths, and after every beach or muddy walk. A lead that only sees dry suburban streets can go a couple of months between washes if you wipe the handle. More important than frequency is the rinse: salt and grit do the damage, and a fresh water rinse after a wet walk prevents most of it.

Can a nylon lead go in the washing machine?

It can, but I would not. The metal clip swings against the drum and can chip it, and the agitator puts a hard bend through the webbing that a lead does not need. If you must, use a cold delicate cycle with the lead inside a pillowcase and no detergent beyond a teaspoon of mild soap. A bucket and ten minutes by hand gives a better result.

Does faded webbing mean the lead is unsafe?

Colour loss alone is not a safety issue. What matters is the condition of the fibres, so bend the strap and look for pale, fuzzy patches and thin spots, and check both ends where the stitching sits. A lead that is faded but firm, with intact stitching and a clip that closes properly, is still doing its job. Replace it when the webbing feels limp.

Why has my lead gone stiff after washing?

Almost always detergent residue or salt left in the weave. Both dry hard and hold the fibres in place, which makes the strap crease instead of flexing. Soak the lead in clean cool water for twenty minutes, work it through your hands and rinse again. If it is still stiff after two rounds, the fibres have been abraded and the lead is near the end of its life.

What is the best way to free a seized swivel?

Soak the swivel end in a cup of fresh water for ten minutes, then work the joint back and forth under a running tap until the grit is out. A little silicone spray afterwards keeps it moving without attracting sand. Do not use cooking oil or petroleum grease, which collect grit and turn a stiff swivel into a grinding one.
