Barking, Chewing, Licking: What a Calming Collar Can and Can't Fix

By Nadia BrooksFeb 10, 2026Calming & Anxiety6 min read

Short answer: a calming collar can only honestly address one of those three complaints, so work out the cause before you buy, because barking, chewing and licking rarely share a fix. Almost every enquiry I get is a behaviour problem, and the collar is a plausible answer to perhaps one of them. Barking needs a named trigger, most chewing is a bored adolescent, and over-licking should see the vet first. Here's the rule: reach for the collar only after the cause is clear.

Almost every enquiry I get about calming products is really about a behaviour problem: the dog won't stop barking at the fence, the dog ate the skirting board, the cat has licked a bald patch into her flank. People want the behaviour to stop, and they've found a product that says it helps with anxiety, so the logic is understandable.

It's also mostly wrong, and not because the products are useless. It's because these three complaints have completely different causes and a collar is a plausible answer to only one of them. Sorting them out first saves a lot of money and a lot of weeks.

Barking: work out what kind first

Barking is a symptom with about six common causes, and they need different responses. Alarm barking at the fence is territorial and rewarding — the person or dog walks away, the barking appears to have worked. Barking when you leave is separation distress. Barking at the window is often frustration at being unable to get to something. Barking for attention is learned.

Only the second of those has a strong anxiety component, and even then a collar is background support rather than a fix. For fence barking, the answer is management and training: block the visual access, stop the rehearsal, reward quiet. For attention barking, the answer is ignoring it consistently, which is tedious and works.

If you're hoping a collar will make a territorial barker quiet, you'll be disappointed, and I'd rather you know that before you buy one than after.

Chewing: usually not anxiety at all

Destructive chewing gets blamed on anxiety constantly and is, in my experience, far more often a young dog with insufficient to do. The giveaway is location: anxious chewing tends to focus on exit points, doors and window frames. Bored chewing is indiscriminate, and it usually happens when nobody's home and there's nothing better going.

The fix for the boring version is also boring: more enrichment, a proper walk before you go, chew items that are more interesting than the furniture, and restricting access to the parts of the house where the damage happens. A collar has nothing to contribute here.

Where a collar can help is the anxious version, and only as part of a plan. The chewing at the door frame while you're out is separation distress, and that responds to gradual absence training with the collar running quietly in the background.

Over-licking and scratching: see the vet first

This is the one I push back hardest on. A dog licking a paw raw, or a cat over-grooming a flank, has a medical cause a large proportion of the time — allergies, parasites, a foreign body, pain, or for cats very often something urinary. Treating that as anxiety and buying a collar delays the diagnosis and lets the problem get worse.

So: vet first, always. Once medical causes are genuinely ruled out and it looks like a compulsive or stress-related behaviour, then a collar is a reasonable thing to try alongside environmental change. But going the other way round wastes the two or three weeks you'd spend waiting for it to work.

Where a collar genuinely belongs

Having said all that, there is a real place for these products in behaviour work, and it's this: a collar can lower overall reactivity enough that training becomes possible. A dog in a constant state of low-level stress doesn't learn well. Bring the baseline down a little and the desensitisation work has somewhere to start.

That's how I use them with clients. Not as a solution, as a precondition. The training is the thing that changes behaviour, and I've never seen a collar do it on its own.

Timing, since it comes up every time

If you do use one, it goes on a week or more before you start, not on the morning you've had enough. The mechanism is slow release, so it needs days on the neck, and one collar runs roughly eight weeks, which is about the length of a realistic behaviour plan.

Give it two to three weeks before deciding whether it's contributing anything. If the answer is no, take it off, and put the money towards a session with a qualified trainer instead. That's not a product failure, it's just that the problem wasn't anxiety.

I'd estimate that maybe a third of the behaviour cases that land on my desk with a calming product already purchased are actually about boredom, access or an undiagnosed medical issue. Sort the cause before you shop. It's cheaper, faster, and your dog gets better sooner. — Nadia, Adelaide, saying the unglamorous thing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a calming collar stop my dog barking?

Usually not on its own. Barking has several common causes, and only the separation-related kind has a strong anxiety component. Fence and attention barking respond to management and training rather than to any wearable product.

My dog chews the furniture when I'm out. Is that anxiety?

Often it isn't. Chewing focused on doors and window frames suggests separation distress, while indiscriminate chewing of furniture and skirting boards is far more commonly boredom or insufficient enrichment in a young dog.

My pet is licking or scratching constantly. Should I try a collar?

See your vet first. Over-licking and over-grooming have a medical cause a large proportion of the time, including allergies, parasites, pain and urinary disease. Rule those out before assuming it's behavioural.

So what is a calming collar actually good for?

Lowering baseline reactivity enough that training or desensitisation becomes possible. It's background support for a behaviour plan rather than a solution to a behaviour problem, and it works best alongside gradual, reward-based work.

How long before I know if it's helping?

Give it two to three weeks of continuous wear before judging. Put it on at least a week before you begin training rather than at the point you've run out of patience. If there is no change, review the cause rather than buying a second product.
