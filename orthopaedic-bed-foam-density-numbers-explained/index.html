Orthopaedic dog beds: what the foam density numbers mean

By Hannah WrightSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats5 min read

Short answer: the foam density number on an orthopaedic dog bed tells you how much material is packed into the foam, not how soft or supportive it feels. Higher density usually means the bed holds its shape longer and resists flattening under a heavy dog, which matters for joint comfort. Density is not firmness, and it is not a cure for arthritis, so read it alongside the bed's actual feel and your vet's advice. As a nurse, I see the number as a durability clue, not a health promise, and I would never tell an owner a bed treats a condition.

What density actually measures

Foam density is weighed in kilograms per cubic metre, often shown as kg/m3. A higher figure means more foam packed in, so the bed is less likely to compress into a pancake after months of use. It says nothing about how the surface feels on day one, only how well it keeps doing its job over time, which is the part owners forget to ask about when they are charmed by a soft top layer.

Density is not the same as firmness

A bed can be low density and feel firm, or high density and feel soft, because firmness comes from the foam's structure, not just its weight. For an older dog with sore hips, you want enough give to relieve pressure but enough support that they are not lying on the floor. The number helps you guess longevity, not comfort on first touch, so always feel the bed as well as read the spec.

  • Density: how much foam is there, and how long it lasts.
  • Firmness: how the surface feels and presses on the body.
  • Both matter, and the label often shows only one.

Why it matters for heavier dogs

A 30kg dog pushes harder into the foam than a 6kg one, so density matters more as the dog gets heavier. Low density foam under a big dog flattens fast, leaving them on the hard ground with none of the support you paid for. If the listing hides the density, that is often a sign it is on the light side, and I would treat a missing number as a quiet warning rather than shrug it off.

The nurse's view on claims

I am a vet nurse, not a vet, so I will not diagnose or say a bed treats any condition. What I can say is that a supportive bed can make rest more comfortable for an older dog, and flat foam does the opposite. Supportive bedding is one part of care alongside your vet's plan, not a replacement for it, and any shop that implies a bed cures arthritis is overselling what foam can do.

What to check before you buy

Look for a density figure in the listing, feel the bed if you can, and check the cover comes off for washing. Ask the shop if they state ILD, a firmness measure, because that pairs with density to tell the real story. If a bed feels great in the shop but lists no density, assume it may not last, and pair your purchase with a conversation with your vet about your dog's specific needs.

You can see how supportive shapes compare in our Beds and Mats hub, and I would read that before trusting a label that promises too much.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a higher density bed always better?

Not always. Very high density foam can feel hard, and a small light dog may prefer something with more give. Density mostly predicts how long the support lasts, so match it to your dog's weight: heavier and older dogs benefit most from higher density, while a tiny dog may not need it and may actually dislike the firmness a high number brings.

Does density cure arthritis or joint pain?

No, and I want to be clear as a nurse that a bed is not a treatment. A supportive bed can make resting more comfortable and take pressure off sore spots, but it does not fix the underlying condition. Please follow your vet's plan and treat bedding as comfort support alongside it, not as the answer to the diagnosis.

What is ILD and should I care?

ILD stands for indentation load deflection, a measure of firmness. Density tells you longevity, ILD tells you feel, and together they describe the bed better than marketing words. Most pet listings skip ILD, so if a shop states it, that is a good sign they know their foam and will answer questions rather than wave a brochure at you.

How can I tell if the foam has gone flat?

Press the bed and watch whether it springs back or stays dented, and check whether your dog now lies beside it on the floor instead. A lasting dent, a thinner feel than last year, and a dog avoiding the bed are the signs it is time to replace the insert or the whole bed, and ignoring those signs just leaves your dog on the ground.

Should I ask the shop for the density?

Yes, absolutely. A good retailer will know the kg/m3 of the foam they sell, and if they cannot tell you, that tells you something too. Pair the answer with a feel of the bed and your dog's weight, and you will choose far better than by price alone, because the number behind the foam is the part that decides if it lasts.
