Retiring the Pen: How We Knew Our Puppy Was Done

By Sophie BennettSep 5, 2026Pet Playpens7 min read

Short answer: We retired the pen at five and a half months, in three stages, over about three weeks. The signals were unglamorous: he settled without being shut in, asked at the balcony door instead of the mat, and stopped chewing things that were not his. We kept it half set up for a fortnight afterwards, used it twice, then folded it behind the door.

Our pen was eight metal panels in the corner of the lounge room of a flat with no hallway, so it doubled as a wall between the kitchen and the rug. For four months it was the most useful thing we owned. Then it started to feel like a cage we were both pretending not to notice. Wally would stand at the gate at 9pm and look at me, and I would look at the pen, and neither of us was convinced. Taking it down turned out to be harder than buying it, mostly because there is no alarm that tells you the job is finished.

The behaviours we watched

Four things, all observed over a couple of weeks rather than on one good day. He stopped chewing the skirting board and the pen panels, which had been a daily project since we brought him home. He started walking to the balcony door and standing there instead of toileting on the mat. He would take himself to his bed in the corner while we cooked, without being told. And he stopped scratching at the gate when we left the room, which was the one that convinced me. None of these is a rule for every puppy. They were simply the four that matched the reasons we had a pen in the first place. I wrote them on the whiteboard on the fridge, which sounds excessive and was genuinely useful.

The staged removal

We did it in three moves over about three weeks. First, the gate stayed open all day and closed only at night, which told us whether the pen was still doing anything while we were awake. A week of that with no mess and no chewing. Second, we took out two panels so the pen became an L shape against the wall, giving him the corner but not the run of the room. Third, the whole thing came down on a Saturday morning when we were home for the day, so we could watch what happened instead of coming home to it. Each stage got at least five days before the next one. Five days was enough to see a pattern and short enough that we did not lose our nerve.

Where we got it wrong the first time

We tried full removal at four months because he seemed fine, and he was fine for three days. On the fourth he found the corner of the rug and the bottom of a curtain within an hour of us going out. Nothing dramatic, but enough to show we had moved faster than he had. The mistake was reading a good week as a finished job. Puppies have good weeks. What we needed was a boring fortnight, and we did not wait for one. Putting two panels back up felt like a step backwards and was actually the thing that made the rest of it work. It also taught us to watch the dog rather than the calendar.

  • A good week is not evidence; wait for a boring fortnight.
  • Open the gate during the day before you remove any panels.
  • Take panels out two at a time rather than the whole pen at once.
  • Keep the pen's corner and bed in place after the panels go.
  • Be home for the first full day the pen is gone.

The fortnight it stayed half up

After the panels came down we left four of them folded against the wall with the base still on the floor, and the bed in the corner where it had always been. It looked like clutter. It was also the reason the change held, because the corner still smelled like his and the shape of the room had not changed all at once. We used the half pen twice: once when a tradesman needed the front door open, and once when he had a stomach upset and needed to be somewhere easy to clean. After a fortnight of not using it, we folded it properly. Ask your vet before confining a dog who is unwell, because rest and confinement are not the same instruction.

The signals that would have sent us back

Three things would have had the pen back out the same day: chewing something he had never touched before, toileting inside in a spot he had not used since month two, or pacing at the balcony door for more than a few minutes. Keeping a folded pen behind a door meant putting it back up took four minutes, which is the argument for holding onto it rather than selling it. Freedom is not a straight line. A puppy who has a bad week in a new flat is not a puppy who failed, and a pen that goes back up for a fortnight is not a defeat. The point of the pen was never containment. It was a way of slowing the room down until he could handle all of it.

What we kept from the pen

The pen lives behind the door now, folded flat, and it comes out about once a month: for a delivery that needs the front door open, for a wet dog who needs to dry off somewhere contained, and for the week the floors were sanded. It is also why I would buy the same pen again rather than a cheaper one, because the panels still line up and the latch still closes squarely after two years of being taken apart and put back. The latch is the part I would check first on any puppy playpen, since everything else about a panel can be lived with. The puppy does not need it any more. The flat still does, occasionally, and that turned out to be a different question from the one we started with when we bought the thing.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age can a puppy come out of a playpen?

There is no age that fits every puppy. Watch behaviour instead: settling without being shut in, asking at the door, and no destructive chewing for a couple of weeks. Ours came out at five and a half months. A larger or more determined puppy often needs longer, and a small calm one may be ready sooner.

Should I keep the pen after the puppy is grown?

If you have somewhere to store it flat, yes. Ours gets used for wet dogs, open front doors and floor sanding, and it took four minutes to put back up during a bad week. If storage is tight in a flat, sell it, but keep the bed and the corner routine, which matter more than the panels.

Can the pen be used as a gate rather than an enclosure?

Yes, and that is how ours spent its last month. Two or three panels across a doorway keep a puppy out of the kitchen without shutting him in a box, and it is much easier to step over than a full enclosure. Check that the panels cannot be pushed over by a dog who jumps against them.

How many panels do I need once the puppy is bigger?

Fewer than you think, because the pen stops being a bedroom and starts being a barrier. Ours went from eight panels to two for the doorway. If you are still using a full pen at night for a large puppy, measure the length when he is sprawled out and add 20cm, rather than guessing from the panel count on the box.

What if the puppy starts chewing the panels again after coming out?

Put two panels back up the same day and treat it as information rather than failure. Chewing on the pen usually means the puppy has more space than he can manage yet, or nothing suitable to chew in reach. Ours went back up for a week, with a different chew toy in the corner, and came down again quietly.
