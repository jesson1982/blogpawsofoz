Sizing a harness for a deep-chested dog: what we learned

By Sophie BennettSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses6 min read

Direct answer: sizing a harness for a deep-chested dog means measuring chest depth as carefully as girth, checking that the straps clear the armpits, and treating every brand's size chart as a rough guide rather than a rule. Our neighbour's Staffy taught us this after three returns. A deep chest changes the angle of every strap, so a harness that is perfect on a round-bodied dog can pinch or gap on a narrow-waisted one, and the only way to know is to measure twice and fit slowly. The chart on the box is a starting point, not a verdict.

What a deep chest actually changes

A deep-chested dog carries more barrel between the front legs and the ribcage than a slender one, which pushes the girth strap forward and twists the whole harness. If you size on girth alone, the chest piece rides up into the throat or sits crooked under the belly.

We measured our boy as a medium by girth, but his chest depth put him closer to a large in one brand and a small in another. The lesson was simple: the number on the box means less than the shape of the dog wearing it. A deep chest is a three-dimensional problem that a single tape reading cannot capture.

The three measurements we now take

Before any new harness, we take the readings that actually predict a good fit. Skipping any one of them is how we landed with rubbing straps in the past.

  • Girth: soft tape around the widest part, just behind the front legs, while the dog stands.
  • Chest depth: from the top of the shoulders down to the breastbone point, to judge how far straps drop.
  • Armpit clearance: confirm the front straps sit clear of the soft skin where the leg meets the body.

With those three numbers, a sizing chart starts to mean something instead of being a guess. We write them on the phone notes so the shop can match a style, not just a label.

Brand sizing is not consistent

We learned the hard way that a medium in one Australian brand was a large in another, and a small in a third. There is no universal standard, so the chart on the packet is the only honest reference for that specific maker. When in doubt, buy from a stockist with a returns policy and measure the dog, not the breed.

This is why we now photograph the tape on the dog and email it to the fitter. A good shop will tell you the truth about cross-breeds, where chest depth varies more than the chart allows. The extra ten minutes saves a refund and a sore armpit.

Armpit clearance is the deal-breaker

On a deep chest the front straps want to drop into the armpit, and that is where rubbing starts. We look for a cut where the front piece wraps the chest without cutting into the soft fold of the leg. A vest style solved it for our Staffy, spreading the load across the breastbone instead of a single webbing line.

If the strap sits proud of the body at the armpit, it gets pinched with every step. Run a finger into that fold before you leave the house. Warmth there means the fit is already wrong, and a week of that is a red line on the skin.

Fitting slowly beats buying twice

The habit that ended our returns was fitting at home before the walk. We clip the new harness on, feed a meal, and watch the dog move around the lounge for ten minutes. Then we do the two-finger check and the armpit check, and only then head out.

Most sizing mistakes show up in the first five minutes of movement, not on the stand. A harness that fits standing can rotate the moment a dog trots, so the indoor rehearsal is the cheapest insurance you can buy. Slow fitting turned a three-return saga into a one-and-done.

When to size up versus down

The instinct is to size down for a strong dog, but on a deep chest that usually makes the chest piece ride up and choke. We size to the chest depth first, then tighten the girth straps within that size. If the depth says large but the girth says medium, the depth wins and we cinch the excess.

This is the part owners miss: adjustable sliders exist so one size can fit a range, but only if the frame is right. Forcing a small frame onto a deep chest is how straps end up across the windpipe. Trust the depth, then tune the girth. We keep a note of the final size in the phone so the next purchase starts from the depth reading, not a guess. That one habit ended our return loop, because the box label stopped being the thing we trusted over the dog in front of us.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I size a deep-chested dog on girth alone?

Not reliably. Girth alone misses chest depth, which is what pushes a harness forward and twists the straps on barrel-chested breeds. Add a chest-depth reading from shoulders to breastbone and an armpit-clearance check. The depth often points to a larger size than the girth suggests, and trusting it prevents the chest piece riding into the throat.

Why does my harness rub under the armpits?

On a deep chest the front straps tend to drop into the armpit, where they get pinched with every step. That pinch becomes a hot spot within a few walks. Choose a cut that wraps the chest without cutting into the leg fold, and run a finger into the armpit before you leave. Warmth there means the fit is already wrong.

Do all brands size the same?

No. A medium in one Australian brand can be a large in another and a small in a third, because there is no shared standard. Always read the chart for the specific maker and measure the dog, not the breed label. Buy from a stockist with a returns policy so a wrong guess costs a postage trip, not a wasted purchase.

Should I size up or down for a deep chest?

Size to the chest depth first, then tighten the girth sliders within that size. Sizing down to feel secure usually makes the chest piece ride up and press the windpipe. If depth says large but girth says medium, take the large and cinch the excess. The frame must be right before the straps can do their job.

What harness shape suits a deep chest best?

A vest style that wraps the breastbone and spreads load across the chest tends to suit deep, narrow-waisted dogs better than a single webbing line. It avoids the armpit pinch and keeps the front piece from twisting. Measure depth and clearance first, then pick the cut that keeps straps clear of the leg fold on a moving dog.
