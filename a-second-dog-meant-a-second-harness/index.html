A Second Dog Meant a Second Harness: What Changed for Us

By Sophie BennettSep 2, 2026Dog Harnesses7 min read

Short answer: a second dog meant a second harness, not a hand-me-down. Wally is 7 kg with a narrow chest, Nell is 18 kg with a broad one, and the same straps sat badly on both. Two fitted harnesses now hang by the door, colour coded, with a spare in the car.

Nell arrived on a Thursday in February with a cardboard file from the rescue group and a borrowed collar. She had been walked on a flat collar and had never worn a harness. Our Cavoodle, Wally, already had a harness that took me a month to fit properly, so I assumed we would swap it between them until I bought a second one. I clipped it onto Nell the next morning and watched the chest strap ride up towards her throat while the belly strap sat four fingers clear of her ribs. On Wally those same straps had been perfect. My tidy one-hook-by-the-door system had only ever worked because there was one dog.

Two dogs, two different shapes

Wally is a 7 kg Cavoodle with a 42 cm girth and a narrow ribcage. Nell is an 18 kg staffy cross with a 62 cm girth, a broad chest and a thick neck set low on her shoulders. Put the same harness on both and you feel it straight away: on Wally the belly strap has slack to spare, on Nell the chest strap crosses the front of her shoulder instead of sitting behind it. Neither dog is built wrong. A harness is a set of angles as much as a set of measurements, and the neck to rib distance, the chest depth and the shoulder shape all shift where the straps land. I stopped treating it as one harness shared between two animals.

What broke in the morning routine

The old routine was three steps: harness off the hook, lead off the same hook, out the door. With two dogs it became a negotiation in a narrow hallway at 6am. I would grab the wrong harness, because at that hour both look like a bundle of straps, then stand there moving sliders while two dogs circled my legs. Wally stands still for a harness and Nell does not, so the order matters: Nell first, with the front door shut. The lead side changed too. One walker with two dogs means one lead in each hand, and I cannot hold them the way I used to. We leave the door separately, ten seconds apart, because two dogs hitting the footpath together tangle the leads before the gate.

The cupboard problem in a rental

We rent a two-bedroom apartment in Perth with no garage and no laundry bench, so the harnesses hang on the back of the front door. That works for one harness and turns into a pile for two. Damp is the real problem: a wet harness hung against a door in winter stays damp inside the padding, and the metal sliders start to feel gritty within a few weeks. What fixed it was an over-door shoe organiser with mesh pockets, one pocket per dog, nothing touching, air on both sides. It also stopped the pile migrating to the kitchen bench. If you rent, check your lease before drilling into a wall, because an over-door or adhesive option avoids that conversation. I marked the pockets with a letter so my partner and I stop swapping them on a dark morning.

  • Measure each dog separately instead of guessing from weight.
  • Give each harness its own hook or pocket so they never mix.
  • Walk the new dog out the door first while the other one waits.
  • Dry both harnesses before they go back on the door.
  • Keep one spare harness in the car for wet park trips.

What we would buy differently

If I did this again I would buy the second harness after the dog arrived, never before. I measured Nell with a soft tape at the rescue, around the widest part of her ribcage and again where a chest strap would sit, then went back through our own dog harnesses notes before choosing. Wally wears 15 mm webbing and a padded back clip, which suits a light dog who walks beside me. Nell has 25 mm webbing and a front clip with a chest plate, because she leans into the lead for the first ten minutes of every walk. Wider webbing spreads the same pull over more of her chest, and the front clip turns her slightly towards me without hauling on her neck. The cheap harness I first tried on her had a slider that slipped under load.

Walks that split and walks that combine

Most days we walk separately. Wally gets a slow sniff walk along the river and Nell gets a longer loop where she can stretch out, which is as much about me as it is about them. When we go together, Nell is on my left and Wally is on my right, and I keep the leads short enough that neither can cross in front of me. Harness handles help here, because I can hold both without the leads sawing through my fingers. One habit worth building is checking each harness as you take it off rather than as you put it on. That is when you spot a chewed edge, a stiff slider or a strap that has stretched. If a harness starts rubbing or leaving a mark, take it off and ask your vet.

What I would buy first next time

On a limited budget with two dogs, the order I would spend in is simple. First, one properly fitted harness per dog, because a harness that fits is the only one that gets used. Second, a spare lead, so a walk never depends on a single clip. Third, somewhere to hang everything that is not the back of a door. Fourth, a spare harness that lives in the car. The expensive harness we barely touch is the one that was nearly right: comfortable enough for a short walk, wrong enough that I always reached for the other one. Nearly right is the most expensive thing in the cupboard, because it sits there for two years and then goes to the op shop.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do both dogs need their own harness, or can they share one?

If their shapes are close enough that the same harness sits correctly on both, sharing can work, and I have done it for a short walk. In practice you move straps between dogs, and a strap that is right for one is nearly always wrong for the other.

How often should I recheck the fit?

Wally was still growing when we bought his first harness, and his chest changed enough that the girth needed two adjustments across a year. I check fit about once a month, and always after a groom or a clip, because coat changes the gap under the straps. Ask your vet if a harness leaves a mark.

Is it worth buying the second harness before the dog comes home?

I would not. Body shape varies more than weight suggests, and a rescue dog may arrive with a coat condition, an old injury or a shape you did not expect from photos. Borrow a harness for the first week, measure properly once the dog has settled, then buy.

What should I do with the harness that does not fit?

Rescue groups and some council pounds take clean harnesses as donations, and op shops usually accept them if the hardware still works. Wash it first and check the sliders and the clip. If the stitching is failing, cut the strap so nobody walks a dog on it.

Can a harness make pulling worse?

A front clip spreads the load across the chest, and for some dogs that makes pulling easier rather than harder. That is a training question rather than a gear one. Ours improved once we practised loose-lead walking on quiet streets. If your dog lunges or chokes on the lead, ask your vet or a trainer.
