Puppy's First Harness: Sizing, Step-In Fit and the Long Line That Saved My Sanity

By Jess AllenJan 15, 2026Dog Harnesses6 min read

Bringing home a puppy is chaos in the best way. Our little Cavoodle, Wally, arrived at eight weeks with more energy than I thought could fit in two kilos. The first thing I bought, before the bed and after the food, was a harness, because I had read enough to know that a collar on a tiny puppy is asking for a trachea injury. What I did not expect was how much of a learning curve the harness itself would be. This is the story of Wally's first puppy harness and lead set, the sizing mistakes I made, and the long line that genuinely kept me calm.

I ordered online from an Australian small dog harness store because I wanted it delivered fast, and I guessed at the size. That was mistake number one. The package arrived and the harness swallowed him. He looked like a toddler wearing a dad's jacket. An XS small dog harness is not a suggestion, it is a measurement, and I had skipped the measuring tape in my excitement. We tightened it as far as it would go and it still slid around when he wiggled.

Reading a puppy harness sizing guide properly

So I did what I should have done first and looked up a real puppy harness sizing guide. The guidance is straightforward but easy to ignore. You measure the chest girth behind the front legs, not the neck, and you add roughly one finger of slack. Then you pick the size where that number sits in the middle of the range. I had bought the size where Wally's measurement was at the very bottom, which meant no room to grow and a poor fit on day one.

For toy breeds especially, this matters. The best harness for toy breeds that Australia can offer is one that adjusts in at least two places, because puppies are not shaped like adult dogs. Wally had a round little belly and a thin neck, so a single-adjustment harness either fell off over his head or squeezed his ribs. Two adjustment points fixed both problems at once.

Why I switched to a step-in fit

The over-the-head style stressed Wally out. He would duck and twist every time I tried to slip it on, and I worried I was teaching him that harness time was scary. A friend who owned a chihuahua harness recommended by Australian owners suggested a step-in design, and it changed everything. You lay it flat, the puppy steps into the two loops, you pull it up and clip it on the back. No sleeves, no head pressure, no wrestling. Within two days Wally was walking to the harness on his own.

I also borrowed a harness for a flat-faced dog from a neighbour with a pug, just to compare, and I noticed the chest panel sat higher and wider than Wally's. Flat-faced dogs need that broader chest coverage for breathing, while a puppy needs lighter fabric and smaller buckles. It was a useful reminder that "small" is not one single shape.

The long line that saved my sanity

House training was where I nearly lost it. Wally would go inside the minute my back was turned, and a standard short lead meant I was either holding him every second or cleaning the floor. The fix was a long training line, basically a lightweight puppy harness and lead combo with a ten-metre cord. I clipped it to his harness, let him roam the fenced yard, and watched. When he circled, I called him to the grass. He learned the routine in a weekend.

The long line kept my sanity because it gave him freedom without giving up control. He was not tethered tight to my hand, but he also could not disappear behind the shed. For a first-time puppy owner, that balance is worth more than any gadget.

A note on no-pull for later

People with flat-faced breeds often ask me about the no-pull harness style used for French bulldogs, and while Wally is not a Frenchie, the principle carries over. When he hit the teenage pull-everywhere phase, a front-clip ring on his harness took the edge off. I am not claiming this is a French bulldog no-pull harness review, because I have not owned a bulldog, but the front-clip idea worked on a small pup and I trust it would work on a flat-faced dog too. The key is starting gentle, before the habit sets in.

Breathability came up as well. On warm afternoons, a breathable mesh pug harness my sister uses for her pug looked far cooler than Wally's padded one, so I picked up a lighter summer harness for him. Puppies overheat quickly and a heavy pad against the chest just made him pant.

What I would tell a new puppy owner

If I could hand a new owner one piece of advice, it is this: measure first, buy second, and choose a step-in harness with two adjustment points. Get a long line early, because it makes training less stressful for both of you. And do not fall for the cutest colour if the fit is wrong, because a poorly fitted harness is worse than no harness at all.

Wally is nearly a year old now and walks nicely on a lead. The puppy chaos is mostly behind us, but I still keep that first XS harness in a drawer, a reminder of how much the right gear smoothed the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why choose a step-in harness for a puppy?

Puppies often hate things going over their head, and a step-in design avoids that wrestle entirely. You lay it flat, they step in, and you clip it on the back, which keeps harness time from becoming scary.

How do I read a puppy harness sizing guide?

Measure the chest girth behind the front legs, not the neck, add about one finger of slack, then pick the size where that number sits mid-range. Buying the size where your pup is at the edge means no room to grow.

What is a long training line for?

It is a lightweight lead, often around ten metres, that lets a puppy explore a fenced space while you keep control. It gives freedom without the risk of them disappearing behind the shed during house training.

Can I use a no-pull front clip on a small puppy?

Yes, the principle carries over from larger dogs. A front-clip ring takes the edge off pulling once they hit the teenage phase, but start gentle before the habit sets in rather than waiting for it to become a problem.

How do I keep a puppy cool in a harness?

Choose lighter fabric and smaller buckles than an adult dog needs, and swap to a breathable mesh style on warm afternoons. Puppies overheat quickly, and a heavy pad against the chest just makes them pant.
