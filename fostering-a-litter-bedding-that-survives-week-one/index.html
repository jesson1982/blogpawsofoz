Fostering a Litter: Bedding That Survives Week One

By Fiona EllerySep 4, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

Short answer: A litter needs bedding you can wash and replace faster than they can mess it, so build a rotation of three full sets and keep the floor layered underneath. Soft beds come last, once the mess is predictable. Plan for constant washing in week one and you will stay ahead of it.

I had six orphan kittens arrive in a cardboard box at nine pm, eyes still shut, and by midnight the first towel was already warm and wet. With a single foster dog you notice the accident and deal with it. With a litter the maths is different, because while you are sorting one, two more are going behind you. Over more than forty fosters in Ballarat I have learned that the bedding plan decides whether week one is calm or chaotic. The animals do not care about colour or label, they care that the spot under them is dry and warm. Get the rotation right and the rest of the fortnight looks after itself.

Why a litter changes the bedding plan

A single foster animal soils one bed and waits for you to notice. A litter works in a pack, and the moment you turn to fold washing, three of them have shifted to a fresh corner and left it behind. The first lesson is that you are not washing for one, you are washing for many, and the pile grows quicker than you expect. I keep a tall esky in the laundry just to carry wet loads from the pen to the machine without dripping across the floor. The second lesson is that cheap soft beds do not cope, because a kitten or puppy will dig, chew and rotate whatever is under them until it moves. Plan the bedding as a system, not a single purchase, and the pet beds range is where I source the pieces that cope.

What I keep in the wash rotation

My standing rotation is three full sets per pen, and that number is what keeps a litter from ever sitting on a wet floor. A set means every layer from the base rug to the top fleece, so swapping is a five minute job rather than a rebuild. I buy fleece vet bed by the metre at the fabric shop and cut it to size, which costs far less than ready made pieces and lets me match awkward pen shapes. Old towels from the op shop make the middle layer, because they are already soft and forgive a hundred washes. The trick is labelling: each set gets a different colour, so I never mix a clean load with a half done one. When the machine runs twice a day, colour coding is the only thing that stops the confusion.

Floor layers before the soft beds

The biggest mistake I see new fosters make is putting a lovely soft bed on a bare tiled floor and hoping for the best. A litter needs a base layer that catches the mess and protects the floor, then a middle layer they can burrow into, then the soft top only where it makes sense. I lay a cheap washable rug square across the whole pen first, so even a missed towel leaves them on fabric, not cold tile. On top of that go the towels, then fleece where the animals actually sleep. This order means a midnight accident is a towel swap, not a floor scrub, and the resident pets in the next room stay none the wiser. Build from the floor up and the soft beds last longer than a single day.

Beds that survive the first week

Soft beds are not all equal once a litter gets hold of them. The covered corner beds with a low entrance are the ones shy kittens choose, because they can disappear and still hear the room, and they wash without losing shape. Flat fleece pads take the most punishment and come out of a 60C cycle ready to use again, which is why I own more of them than anything else. Foam filled beds look cosy but they hold odour and they are slow to dry, so I keep them for the older pups who are past the worst of the mess. Whatever you pick, avoid anything with a crunchy filling that can be chewed and swallowed, and keep the pen cleared of loose stuffing. In week one, washability beats comfort every time.

  • Run three full bedding sets so one is always dry.
  • Wash on the hottest cycle the label allows.
  • Layer the floor before you add any soft bed.
  • Keep one pen corner bare for the worried ones.
  • Swap bedding at night so the mornings start clean.

Washing without losing a day

The washing is the job that eats the fortnight if you let it. I run the machine on a hot cycle for the fleece and the towels, then a cooler rinse for anything with a waterproof backing, because heat cracks those coatings over time. Drying is the real bottleneck in a Ballarat winter, so I keep two clothes horses near the heater and rotate the sets through them in the order they came off the line. If the weather is kind I use the ute's tray outside, but mostly it is the laundry. The rule that saves me is never start a new task until the current wet load is sorted, because a forgotten pile in the boot goes sour fast and then the whole set is useless.

Knowing when the floor is enough

There is a point in week one where the animals stop treating the bedding as a toilet and start treating it as a bed, and that is when the rotation can ease. I watch for the litter to curl up together on the fleece instead of spreading to the corners, because that tells me the mess is slowing. Until then, less is more: a bare warm corner and a single pad beats a pen full of beds they will only wreck. When they are old enough to be adopted, I keep one favourite blanket from the set and send it with them, so the new home smells of something they already know. That small, unwashed scrap does more for a scared kitten than any new bed I could buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many beds do I need for a litter of kittens?

For a litter I run three full sets per pen, and that is the number that keeps the floor dry through constant washing. A set is every layer from the base rug to the top fleece, so a swap is quick. With fewer than three you will hit a point where the clean pile runs out before the machine finishes, and then the kittens sit on something wet. Build the rotation before the litter arrives.

Can I use old human blankets instead of pet bedding?

Old blankets from the op shop are exactly what I use for the middle layer, so yes, they work well once they are washed hot. The thing to avoid is anything with loose filling or long tassels a kitten can swallow, and anything so thick it never dries. Thin towelling and fleece are better than a heavy doona, because they wash and dry inside a day.

What wash temperature gets the smell out of fleece vet bed?

I wash fleece and towels on the hottest cycle the care label allows, which for most vet bed is 60C and that is what keeps the odour from settling in. A cold wash leaves a smell that builds with every use. Dry the pieces fully before they go back in the pen, because damp fleece goes sour and then no amount of washing fixes it. If a piece still smells, retire it.

Should a litter sleep on one shared bed or separate ones?

In week one I keep them together on shared fleece, because a litter settles faster when they can feel each other and the warmth spreads. Separate beds make sense later, once they are weaned, but a newborn or young litter wants the pile. The one exception is any animal that is unwell. For the healthy group, one big washable pad beats five small ones you have to swap all at once.

When can I cut back on all the washing?

Ease off once the litter starts curling up on the fleece instead of spreading to the corners, because that is the sign the mess is slowing. For most healthy litters that point lands in the second week. Until then keep the three set rotation running. Ask your vet if you are unsure whether an animal is well enough to change routines.
