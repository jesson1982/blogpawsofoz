Dog Playpen with Floor: Pros, Cons & Top Picks

By Sophie BennettAug 1, 2026Pet Playpens7 min read

Short answer: pick a floored pen for a rental, because the floor protects the landlord's timber when Wally drags his water bowl and Miso leaves a hairball in the corner. Beneath ours I keep a mat, and the removable fabric goes through a gentle wash once the puppy mess builds up. The truth is a floor is not automatically better outdoors, where a floorless mesh ring lets air move underneath and weighs less to carry.

The first pen I bought for Wally was floorless, basically a ring of mesh panels I dropped on the lounge-room rug. It worked for about a day, until he dragged his water bowl across the carpet and Miso decided the corner was a great spot for a hairball. That was the moment I started looking properly at a dog playpen with floor, and I have not gone back. But a floor is not automatically better for everyone, so this is my honest pros-and-cons take, written from a rental flat where the floor is the landlord's, not mine.

Floored versus floorless, plainly

Before we get to picks, it helps to be clear about what each style actually gives you. A floored pen has a solid base sewn into the bottom, so your pet sits on a continuous surface that you control. A floorless pen has open mesh or bars at the bottom and rests directly on your room's floor or the grass outside. Both have a place, and the right one depends on where you live and what you are protecting.

Escape riskHarder to dig out under the edgesA clever dog can nudge under a gap

For a renter like me, the floor column wins on most days. The whole reason I pay a bond is that the flat has to go back in decent shape, and a pen that catches every spill is one less thing to worry about at inspection time.

The renter floor-protection angle

If you rent, think of a dog playpen with floor as insurance you can fold away. Our timber floors are lovely but scratchy, and Wally's claws at eight weeks were like tiny ice picks. The coated base means his nails tap on fabric, not timber, and any puddle stays on the pen, not the boards. I still slide a cheap rubber mat underneath for extra grip, because a pen can slide on polished floors when a puppy launches at the mesh, and that sliding is its own kind of scuff.

Miso benefits too. Cats are fastidious, and a floored pen gives her a clean, defined space away from the dog. When she uses her own little pen, the base catches the litter that sticks to her paws, which keeps it off the couch. Small wins, but they add up in a flat you do not own.

Door types and why they matter

A dog playpen with door is far easier to live with than one you have to lift a puppy over. There are a few styles, and they are not equal. The zip door is the most common on soft pens: a panel that zips open so your pet can wander out, or you can leave it closed for containment. It is light and cheap, but a determined chewer will go for the zip, so supervise at first.

The step-through door has a low lip you can step over, which is gentle on older dogs and easy for you. The swing gate door, like a little hinged fence panel, is the sturdiest and best for daily in-and-out use, though it needs clearance in front. For Wally I use the zip door at home and a step-through at the campsite, where the ground is soft and a swinging gate would catch on pegs. Match the door to the space, not the other way around.

If you mostly use the pen outside on dry grass and your dog is calm, a floorless panel set can be lighter and cheaper, and it lets the breeze run underneath. But for indoor rental life, the floored version is the one I recommend, with a washable base you can chuck in the machine after a seaside trip.

When a floorless pen is the better call

I do not want to sound one-note. If you have a secure backyard and a full-grown, toilet-trained dog, a floorless pen on the lawn is refreshing for them and easy for you. The ground stays cool, the smells are natural, and you are not hauling a wet base around. For puppy toilet training indoors, though, the floor catches accidents so you are not scrubbing the carpet at midnight, which is the scenario most new pet parents actually face.

The other time floorless wins is pure portability. On a multi-stop caravan loop, every gram and every fold counts, and a lighter pen is one less thing to wrestle into the boot beside the esky and the camp chairs.

My bottom line

For a first-time, renting, small-space pet parent, a dog playpen with floor is the safer starting point, and a dog playpen with door is the feature that keeps it practical day to day. Choose the floor for protection and the door for sanity, then size it with a little room to grow. Wally has not ruined a single floorboard, and that is the kind of boring success I am happy to report.

Frequently asked questions

Is a dog playpen with floor better for renters?

Usually yes. The built-in base catches spills, claws and accidents that would otherwise hit the carpet or timber, protecting your bond. Add a rubber mat underneath for grip on polished floors.

What door types come on soft pens?

Common styles are the zip door, a step-through low-lip door, and a hinged swing gate. Zip doors are light and cheap; swing gates are sturdiest but need clearance in front.

Can I use a floored pen outdoors?

Yes, on dry ground or under an awning. The floor keeps your pet off damp grass and dirt, but make sure the sides stay breathable so the pen does not trap heat.

How do I clean a pen with a floor?

Wipe the coated base with a damp cloth for daily mess, and machine-wash the removable fabric on a gentle cycle if your model allows. Air-dry before folding away.

Does a floor stop my dog escaping?

It helps, because there is no gap to dig under at the edges. A determined dog can still chew a zip door, so supervise early training and zip it closed when unsupervised.

When should I choose a floorless pen?

If you have a secure yard, a calm adult dog and mainly outdoor use, floorless is lighter and lets air move underneath. Indoors with a puppy, the floored version is the easier choice.
