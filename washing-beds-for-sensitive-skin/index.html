Washing Beds for Sensitive Skin: Detergent and Rinsing

By Hannah WrightSep 4, 2026Beds & Mats8 min read

Short answer: Wash the cover on a normal cycle with a plain, fragrance free detergent, run one extra rinse, and dry it completely before it goes back on. Residue from detergent, softener and too much powder is what usually stays behind, and an extra rinse removes more of it than a hotter wash will.

A few months ago a client sat in the consult room and listed everything she had tried for her dog's itchy skin: three foods, two shampoos, a new vacuum. The vet asked what the bedding was washed in. She went quiet, then said the family detergent and a softener sheet in the dryer, every week, for two years. I am not going to tell you that caused anything, because I cannot know that and it is not my call. I can tell you what was sitting in that fabric, and what I would change first.

What actually stays in the fabric

Washing removes dirt and leaves things behind. Detergent residue, fabric softener, optical brighteners and the perfume that makes bedding smell clean all sit in the fibres, and a pet lying on that fabric for eight hours is in close contact with all of it.

The heaviest load is in the padded parts, where the filling holds water and takes far longer to rinse than the outer fabric. A cover that feels dry outside can still be damp and soapy in the middle, and that layer is the one under the pet.

Residue is not automatically a problem. Plenty of dogs sleep on freshly washed bedding with no issue. But when the skin is already sensitive, the number of things touching it matters, and bedding is one variable you can control completely. The skin itself is a question for your vet.

Choosing a detergent that rinses clean

Plain is the aim. A liquid or powder sold for babies or sensitive skin, with no perfume, no dye and no softener built in, is a sensible default. Eco and plant based does not automatically mean gentler, so read the panel for fragrance rather than the front of the bottle.

Dose matters more than brand. Most people use more detergent than the load needs, and the excess is what ends up in the filling. For a bed cover, measure for a lightly soiled load even if the cover looks bad going in.

Skip fabric softener entirely. It works by coating fibres, which is the opposite of what you want on bedding, and it builds up over repeated washes. Skip the scented beads for the same reason. Do not add bleach or disinfectant unless the care label allows it, because both leave residue of their own.

The rinse cycle people skip

The most useful setting on a washing machine for pet bedding is the extra rinse.

A standard front loader cycle uses relatively little water, which is good for the bill and less good for a thick cover full of suds. One extra rinse pushes more clean water through the filling and carries more detergent out with it.

Do not overload the machine. A cover needs room to move so water can circulate, and a drum packed with two beds and a pile of towels will rinse neither. Check the detergent drawer now and then, because a clogged drawer drops powder into the load in lumps that never dissolve. If a cover comes out stiff, that is usually residue rather than fabric.

  • Use a fragrance free detergent and measure it for a lightly soiled load.
  • Add one extra rinse cycle to every bed wash, every time.
  • Leave fabric softener and scented booster beads out of the machine entirely.
  • Dry the cover right through the filling before it goes back on.
  • Keep a spare cover so a bed never has to go back on damp.

Drying is half the job

A damp bed is worse than a dirty one. Damp filling holds warmth and moisture, it smells, and it goes cold under a pet who is lying still for hours. A cover that feels dry at the zip can still be wet through the padding.

Line drying in shade is easiest on the fabric and works across most of Australia for most of the year. Sun dries well and is hard on dyes and elastic, so a covered veranda beats full afternoon sun. Turn the cover inside out so the seams get air first.

In a dryer, use low heat and check the filling by feel at the thickest point rather than the edges. Wool, memory foam and anything with a rubber backing should not go in at all. Give it an extra hour rather than guessing, and if the middle is still cool, it is still wet.

Beds that cannot go in the machine

Not every bed has a removable cover, and those need a different routine.

For a foam mattress with a fixed cover, vacuum it, sponge the surface with diluted plain detergent and wipe with a clean damp cloth twice. Do not soak it. Stand it on edge in moving air until it is dry through, which can take a day or more in humid weather.

Raised cots with mesh are easy. The frame wipes down, the mesh hoses off and dries fast, and the whole thing comes apart in a minute. Wool and sheepskin need cool water, a wool detergent, and flat, slow drying, because heat and agitation will felt them into a mat. If a bed cannot be washed and cannot be aired, replace it rather than patching it.

Building a wash routine that holds

Most households do better with a rotation than one big clean. Two covers for the main bed means one can be washed and dried properly while the other is in use, and nobody puts a damp cover back because the dog is tired and it is nine at night.

A weekly shake and vacuum, a wash every two to three weeks for a dog who sleeps on the bed every night, and a wash straight after an accident or a muddy walk is a workable pattern. Cats often need it less often but benefit from the same routine.

Write the wash temperature inside the cover with a laundry marker while it is new. Care labels fade, and the wrong cycle on a good cover is an expensive mistake. If you are buying, the pet beds range includes covers that unzip fully. If the skin problem is ongoing, ask your vet to look at the skin itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I wash my dog's bed?

For a dog who sleeps on it every night, a wash every two to three weeks with a weekly shake and vacuum in between is a reasonable baseline. Wash sooner after an accident, a muddy walk or heavy shedding. A cat bed can often go a month, though the one they use most needs it first.

Can I use the same detergent I wash my clothes in?

Often yes, if it is a plain liquid without added softener or heavy perfume. The problem is usually the dose and the rinse rather than the product. If your usual detergent is strongly scented, a separate fragrance free bottle for pet bedding is a cheap change and easier than rewashing twice.

Does white vinegar help with pet smells?

A small amount in the rinse can help with some odours, and it rinses out cleanly. It is not a disinfectant at the strength used in a laundry rinse, so do not treat it as one. For urine, an enzyme cleaner used properly does more, and the cover still needs a normal wash afterwards.

Can I put the bed cover in the dryer?

Many can go on low heat, and low is the important word. Check the care label, then check the filling at its thickest point rather than the edges, because the outside dries long before the middle. Anything with wool, memory foam or a rubber backing should be air dried instead.

What if my dog chews or licks the bedding?

Some dogs chew bedding out of boredom or because they are young, and some lick fabric as a settling habit. It is worth removing a cover that is being eaten rather than washing it again. If a dog is swallowing fabric, or the licking is new and constant, that is a conversation for your vet.
