Small Dog & Apartment Living: The Fold-Flat Indoor Pen

By Sophie BennettJul 24, 2026Pet Playpens11 min read

Sophie & the Fur Babies — pet playpens for renters, puppies & cats

My flat is fifty-four square metres. One bedroom, one bathroom, a lounge that is also a dining room, and a balcony just big enough for a clothes airer and a sad basil plant. When Wally arrived as an eight-week-old Cavoodle, three separate people told me a dog was a bad idea in a space this size. They were wrong, but only because I found a way to give him a defined patch of floor that I could make vanish.

That is the whole trick of small-space dog ownership as far as I can tell. It is not about square metres. It is about being able to change what a room is for, several times a day, without owning more furniture.

Why square metres are not really the problem

Wally is 7kg and about 33cm at the shoulder. He does not need a paddock. On any given day he sleeps for roughly sixteen hours, mostly in a rectangle about the size of a doormat, and the walking he needs happens on the footpath and at the beach rather than indoors.

What he does need is a place that is unambiguously his. In a house, that place tends to happen by accident — a laundry, a mudroom, a corner of a family room nobody walks through. In a flat this size there is no unclaimed corner. Every square metre is doing a job, and if you do not deliberately assign him one, he will assign himself the middle of the hallway, which is the single worst place for a small dog to nap in a flat where two people and a cat need to get past.

That was our first three weeks. I stepped over him about forty times a day, and twice I nearly stepped on him. The pen solved a traffic problem before it solved a training one.

The second thing a small dog needs in a flat is a way to be off duty. Wally can see the front door, the kitchen and the balcony from almost anywhere in the lounge, which means he can hear and monitor everything. In a building with shared walls that is a lot of information — the lift, the bins, the neighbour's kettle. A small dog playpen tucked into the corner facing away from the door gives him a spot where he is allowed to stop supervising. His barking at hallway noise dropped noticeably within a fortnight of that change alone.

Where it stores, with actual measurements

This is the part I care about most and the part product pages are vaguest about, so here are the numbers from ours.

Our medium pen is 85–90cm across and 58–60cm high when it is up. Folded, it twists down into a disc about 48cm in diameter and 6cm thick, weighing a bit under 3kg. The clearance under my bed base is 14cm. The pen slides under with plenty of room, flat, and it does not need to be lifted or wrestled — I tip it on edge, walk it to the bedroom and slide it in one-handed.

The large size, at 114 × 114cm, folds to roughly a 60cm disc at the same 6cm thickness. Same under-bed clearance requirement, just a wider circle. If your bed sits on a base with a centre rail, measure the gap between the rail and the side of the frame before you buy the large, because 60cm needs a genuinely open span.

Other places mine has lived: behind the couch, which is a 9cm gap and works fine; flat on top of the wardrobe shelf; upright between the wardrobe and the wall; and in the boot of a hatchback, standing against the side. What it does not fit is my hallway cupboard, because that has shelves at 40cm spacing and the disc is 48cm across. Measure your intended spot before you order rather than after.

Flat versus house — what actually changes

I grew up in a house on a quarter-acre block in the hills, so I had a lot of assumptions to unlearn. This is how the same pen ends up being used completely differently depending on where you live.

The row I would underline if I were allowed to underline anything is the noise one. I looked seriously at a wire panel set because it was cheaper, and I am glad I did not buy it. My neighbour on the other side of the bedroom wall works night shifts. A dog who shifts position at three in the morning against metal panels is a conversation I did not want to have.

Making it comfortable for a small dog

A pen a dog dislikes is just an expensive obstacle. Ours works because it is the nicest place in the flat to lie down, and that took a bit of deliberate effort.

Under the pen goes a washable rug — grip on floorboards, and insurance for the base, which is water resistant rather than waterproof. Inside goes a bolster bed rather than a flat mat, because Wally sleeps with his chin on something and a bolster gives him that. In summer the bolster gets swapped for a gel cooling mat, since the lounge faces west and gets genuinely hot from about four o'clock.

He gets a chew in there and nowhere else. That was intentional. It means the pen is where the good thing happens, and after about a week he started taking himself in when he wanted to settle. Water sits in a weighted stainless bowl at the edge, low enough that he does not have to reach up.

Two small things that turned out to matter more than I expected. First, the pen faces away from the front door, so he is not looking at the thing he most wants to bark at. Second, one side sits against a wall. An enclosure with a solid back reads as den rather than exposed platform, and he settles faster in that orientation than he did when it was floating in the middle of the room.

This is general pet-care information, not veterinary advice — always follow your vet's guidance for your own animal.

A quick word on that. If your dog cries, paces or scrabbles the moment you are out of sight rather than just at the pen, that is worth raising with your vet or a qualified behaviourist rather than solving with more confinement. Distress and a mild preference for your company are different things, and I would not want anyone to read a post about pens and assume a pen is the answer to genuine separation anxiety. It is not.

The renting side of it

My lease is the standard WA residential agreement with a pet annexe, and the two lines that shape everything are no modifications to the premises and the property is to be returned in the condition it was received, fair wear and tear excepted.

An apartment dog pen that sits on the floor and holds its own shape satisfies both without any negotiation. Nothing goes into plaster, nothing clamps a doorframe, nothing leaves an adhesive shadow on the skirting boards. When the property manager did the six-month inspection, the pen was under the bed and the rug was in the wash, and the flat presented as a flat.

The floors are the thing I would actually worry about. Floating floorboards and a puppy are not natural allies. The rug underneath has taken every accident, every spilt water bowl and every wet-from-the-beach dog, and it costs thirty dollars to replace. Replacing a section of floating floor does not.

Eighteen months in

Wally is a year and a half old now and fully toilet trained, so the pen has changed jobs. Its door stays open. He uses it as a bed roughly from nine in the morning until early afternoon, and it comes into proper service for tradie visits when the front door is propped open, for the week after his desexing, and for every camping trip we take.

Would I still buy it, knowing my flat is this small? It is the only piece of pet gear I have that solves a space problem instead of creating one. Eighty dollars, three kilograms, and it lives under the bed. In fifty-four square metres, that maths is very easy.

Frequently asked questions

Which size suits a one-bedroom flat?

Medium, at 85–90cm across. The large, at 114cm, is a lovely space for a dog and takes up roughly a quarter of a small lounge room, which you will resent within a week. A 7kg Cavoodle has plenty of room to turn, stretch and keep a water bowl at the edge in the medium.

Will it actually fit under a bed?

Ours folds to a disc about 48cm across and 6cm thick, and my bed base has 14cm of clearance, so it slides under flat with room to spare. Behind the couch works too if you have a gap over about 8cm. Measure your intended spot first — mine does not fit my hallway cupboard because the shelves are 40cm apart.

Is a soft pen strong enough for a small dog?

For most small dogs, yes. Wally has never tested the walls, and small dogs generally do not think upwards the way cats do. A committed chewer will eventually get at the top piping, so keep something better to chew inside. If your dog is over about 15kg or a known escape artist, rigid panels are the safer choice.

How do I protect floating floorboards?

Put a washable rug under the whole pen. The base is water resistant rather than waterproof, so anything that pools will eventually find a seam, and floorboards are the last thing you want to explain at a bond inspection. A thirty-dollar rug has absorbed every accident and every wet dog we have had.

Is it quiet enough for a flat with shared walls?

Much quieter than wire. Fabric panels make almost no noise when a dog shifts position, which matters at three in the morning in a building where the bedroom wall is shared. That was the deciding factor for me over a cheaper wire panel set, and I have no regrets about the extra spend.

Can I leave my dog in it while I am at work?

Not all day. A soft-sided pen is fine for a few hours with water, a comfortable bed and something to chew, but a whole working day in a space that size is too long for any dog regardless of the enclosure. If you cannot get home mid-day, look at a walker or day care rather than a longer stint in the pen.
